# Phase 5 — Meeting English
### Goal: Speak up in meetings, contribute your ideas, agree and disagree professionally, and ask questions confidently.

---

## Why Meetings Are Hard

Meetings are harder than one-on-one conversations for a few reasons:

- Multiple people are talking and you have to find the right moment to jump in
- You don't want to sound rude when disagreeing
- You might not understand something but feel awkward asking
- You want to contribute but don't know how to start

This phase gives you exact phrases for every one of these situations. Once you have the phrases, the only thing left is confidence — and that comes with practice.

---

## The 5 Things You Need to Do in a Meeting

1. **Open and introduce yourself** — at the start of a call
2. **Contribute a point** — add your idea or opinion
3. **Agree** — show you're aligned with someone
4. **Disagree** — push back without being rude
5. **Ask for clarification** — when you didn't understand something

Let's go through each one with phrases and examples.

---

## 1. Opening a Meeting or Joining a Call

When you join a call and need to introduce yourself or greet the group:

> "Hi everyone, good morning. I'm Vin, joining from the development side."

> "Hello, can everyone hear me okay? Great. I'm Vin — I'll be handling the technical part of this discussion."

> "Hi, sorry I'm a minute late. I'm Vin. I've been briefed on the agenda so we can go ahead."

**Practice:** Say all three of these out loud. Pick the one that feels most natural for you and make it yours.

---

## 2. Contributing a Point

Jumping into a meeting conversation is one of the hardest things for non-native speakers. You need a phrase that signals you want to speak — before you actually say your point. This buys you a second to organize your thought.

**Phrases to jump in:**
- "If I may add something here..."
- "I wanted to bring up one point..."
- "From my side, what I've observed is..."
- "Just to add to what [name] was saying..."
- "I have a thought on this, if that's okay..."
- "Can I jump in here for a second?"

**Then say your point using PREP.**

**Full example:**

> "If I may add something here — I think we should test on mobile before we finalize. The reason is that most of our users are on phones. We had a situation last month where a layout looked perfect on desktop but was broken on mobile. So testing early would save us that problem."

---

## 3. Agreeing

Don't just say "yes" or "I agree." That sounds passive. Strong agreement sounds like this:

**Simple agreement:**
- "That makes a lot of sense."
- "Absolutely, I think that's the right call."
- "I completely agree with that."

**Agreement + adding your own point:**
- "That's a great point, and I'd also add that..."
- "Exactly — and what that also means is..."
- "I agree, and I think we should also consider..."

**Agreement when you're slightly surprised:**
- "Actually, I hadn't thought of it that way, but you're right."
- "That's a really good point. I think you've nailed it."

---

## 4. Disagreeing Professionally

This is the skill most people are afraid of. Disagreeing in English — especially in a professional setting — is about acknowledging the other person's point first, then presenting yours. Never dismiss. Always acknowledge.

**The formula:** Acknowledge → But → Your point

**Phrases:**

- "I see where you're coming from, but I think..."
- "That's a valid point. However, my concern is..."
- "I understand the reasoning, but I'm not fully convinced because..."
- "I respect that perspective. I'd like to offer a different view though."
- "I'm not sure I agree with that approach. Could we explore an alternative?"

**Full example:**

> "I see where you're coming from on pushing the deadline. However, my concern is that the testing phase isn't complete. Last time we rushed a delivery, we had to spend two days fixing issues that should have been caught before launch. I'd feel more comfortable with one extra week."

Notice: You didn't say "you're wrong." You said "here's my concern and here's why."

---

## 5. Asking for Clarification

Never sit in a meeting pretending you understood something you didn't. Asking for clarification is professional — it shows you're paying attention.

**When you didn't catch what someone said:**
- "I'm sorry, could you repeat that? I didn't catch it."
- "Could you say that again? I want to make sure I understood."

**When you need more detail:**
- "Could you elaborate on that a bit?"
- "What do you mean exactly by [word/phrase]?"
- "Can you give an example of what that would look like?"

**When you want to confirm your understanding:**
- "Just to make sure I'm following — you're saying that...?"
- "So if I understand correctly, the plan is to...?"
- "Let me just confirm — you want me to handle [X] and [Y] is being handled by [name]?"

## Full Meeting Dialogue — Read and Shadow

**Scenario:** Team meeting to decide whether to add a new feature before launch.

---

**Manager:** "Okay, should we add the live chat feature before we go live, or push it to version two?"

**Person A:** "I think we should add it now. Customers expect it these days."

**You:** "I see where you're coming from — it's definitely a feature users appreciate. However, my concern is the timeline. Adding it now means we need at least another week, and we've already moved the launch date once. Could we launch without it and add it in version two?"

**Manager:** "That's a fair point. What does the team think?"

**Person B:** "I agree with Vin. Let's keep the scope clean and ship on time."

**Manager:** "Okay, let's go with that. Vin, can you make sure the version two roadmap includes it?"

**You:** "Absolutely. Just to confirm — I'll document the live chat feature as a priority for version two and share the updated roadmap by end of week. Does that work?"

**Manager:** "Perfect. Thanks everyone."

---

Shadow this dialogue. Read your lines (marked "You") out loud 5 times with full confidence.

## Practice Scenarios

Speak your response out loud for each situation. Record yourself.

**Situation 1:**
Someone in a meeting says: *"I think we should redesign the whole website before the client presentation."*
→ Disagree professionally. You think it's too much work in too little time.

**Situation 2:**
The manager says something quickly and you didn't fully understand the action item assigned to you.
→ Ask for clarification.

**Situation 3:**
Someone makes a good point about simplifying the project scope.
→ Agree and add your own supporting point.

**Situation 4:**
You want to bring up a concern about the budget that nobody has mentioned yet.
→ Contribute your point.
