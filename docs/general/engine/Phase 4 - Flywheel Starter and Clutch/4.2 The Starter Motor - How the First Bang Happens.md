The flywheel's momentum carries an engine between bangs - but momentum has a deadlock built into it: a spinning flywheel keeps the engine alive, yet a still flywheel cannot start itself. Every part of the engine is at rest when you first sit in the car, and the first power stroke cannot happen until the pistons are already moving. Something must break that deadlock, and it is this file's subject: the starter motor.

# 4.2 The Starter Motor - How the First Bang Happens

## The deadlock, stated precisely

Trace the engine's own logic backwards. The power stroke needs the piston already moving (the crank must be spinning so the spark's bang lands on a running cycle). The pistons moving need the crankshaft spinning. The crankshaft spinning needs either a push that already happened or momentum that already exists. At key-off, none of these exist.

So the engine cannot start itself, ever, by design. It needs an outside hand to spin it the first few rotations - just enough that combustion can catch and the engine becomes self-sustaining.

## The part: a small electric motor with a gear

That hand is the **starter motor**: a small electric motor powered by the car's **battery**. Its only job is to spin the engine's crankshaft a few times at start-up, and then get out of the way.

When you turn the key, the starter motor runs, and a **small gear on its shaft pushes outward and meshes with the toothed ring around the flywheel's edge**. Those teeth were named in file 4.1 as one of the flywheel's two faces - and this is their entire job: the starter's handle.

## The chain of starting, part by part

From the key turn, the sequence runs through links this course has already built:

1. The battery powers the **starter motor**.
2. The starter's small gear meshes with the **flywheel's toothed ring**.
3. The spinning starter turns the **flywheel**, which is bolted to the **crankshaft** - so the crankshaft turns.
4. The crankshaft drags the **pistons** through their strokes, and the **timing belt** turns the **camshaft** (file 1.5), so the valves open and close in rhythm.
5. Within a second or two, a power stroke fires on its own - the first bang - and the engine is running itself.

Notice the starter touches none of the pistons or valves directly. It turns one end of the crankshaft, and every established link from Phases 0 and 1 propagates the motion: flywheel to crankshaft, crankshaft to pistons, timing belt to camshaft. This is the whole engine acting as one connected machine, started from a single input.

## Why a *small* gear on a *large* ring

The starter motor is small and its battery power is limited, yet it must move a heavy flywheel from rest. The gear sizing is how it manages: the starter's **small gear** driving the flywheel's **large ring** is the slow-but-strong arrangement - a small gear driving a large one turns the large one slowly but with a lot of turning force, because the small gear must rotate many times for one turn of the ring. That size-versus-force trade is exactly the logic the gearbox will use in Phase 6 (file 6.1); here it lets a motor the size of a fist rotate a crankshaft that a person could not budge by hand.

## Getting out of the way: automatic disengagement

The moment the engine catches and runs on its own, the flywheel spins far faster than the starter motor ever could. If the gears stayed meshed, the fast flywheel would now be *driving* the little starter gear at absurd speed - spinning it to destruction within seconds.

So the starter's gear is designed to **retract automatically as soon as the engine starts**: the mesh exists only while cranking, and pulls back the moment combustion takes over. The starter motor never experiences engine speed.

## The grinding noise, explained

Now the sound almost every driver has made once: turning the key while the engine is already running - say, with music loud enough that you could not hear it was on. What happens is precisely the disengagement problem caught mid-crash:

The starter's gear pushes out toward a **flywheel that is already spinning fast**. The two sets of teeth are moving at completely different speeds, so they cannot lock together - they clash and skim past each other, producing that harsh metal-on-metal screech, a bit like a chain zipping.

Doing this repeatedly has a real cost: it chews up the teeth - on the starter gear, on the flywheel's ring, or both - and replacing those parts is expensive. Modern cars reduce the trap with a **lockout**: the starter simply will not engage while the engine is running. Older cars lack it, which is why the mistake belongs to beginners with loud music.

## The start chain, in one line

**Battery → starter motor → flywheel → crankshaft → pistons (and, via the timing belt, camshaft and valves) → first bang → engine self-sustaining.** From a quiet car to a running engine in a second, through links built across four phases of this course.
