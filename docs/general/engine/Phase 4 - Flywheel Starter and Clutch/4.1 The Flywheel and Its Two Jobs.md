# 4.1 The Flywheel and Its Two Jobs

## What it is

The **flywheel** is a heavy metal disc bolted to the end of the crankshaft, at the back of the engine. It spins whenever the crankshaft spins, so it always turns at engine speed. That is the whole part: a disc, heavy on purpose, rigidly attached to the shaft the pistons turn.

Its weight is not incidental - the weight *is* the function. A heavy spinning object resists changes to its spinning. Speed it up and it wants to keep its new speed; try to slow it and it fights back. That stubbornness is called **momentum**, and everything about the flywheel follows from it.

## Job one: carrying the engine between bangs

Recall the four-stroke's accounting from Phase 1: only one stroke in four produces a push. During the other three - intake, compression, exhaust - nothing is driving the piston, and one of those three (compression) actively pushes *back*.

The flywheel is what bridges those gaps. Each power stroke shoves the piston, spins the crankshaft, and adds speed to the flywheel. The flywheel then refuses to slow down through the quiet strokes: its stored spin carries the crank around, dragging every piston through its housekeeping - including paying the effortful squeeze of compression. When the next power stroke arrives, it tops the flywheel's speed back up.

Two clarifications that make the mechanism honest:

1. **The flywheel is not a battery.** It does not "give away a portion" of its energy in measured doses. It is simply a heavy thing that resists slowing; the bangs arrive often enough that its speed stays roughly steady, and the resistance to slowing is what carries the shaft between them.
2. **The link works in both directions.** The piston, rod, throw, shaft and flywheel are one rigid chain (file 0.3). So the push travels piston-to-flywheel during the power stroke, and flywheel-to-piston during the quiet ones. One connection, used both ways.

A second effect rides along: smoothness. One bang per two turns would make a bare engine lurch - shove, coast, coast, shove. The heavy disc averages those jerks out, resisting sudden speed changes in either direction, so the rotation at the crankshaft's end is far steadier than the pushes that feed it.

## Job two: the meeting point between engine and car

The flywheel's second job is positional, not mechanical: it is where the engine ends and the rest of the car begins. Its outer face is the surface everything downstream grips:

- **The teeth around its outer edge** are the ring that the starter motor meshes with to spin the engine from rest. That story is the next file's subject.
- **Its flat face** is the surface the **clutch** presses against to pass the engine's rotation on toward the gearbox and wheels. Release the clutch and the two spin together; press it and the engine spins free of the wheels. That story is file 4.3.

So the flywheel is simultaneously the engine's momentum store and its output flange - the part whose face literally connects the burning inside the cylinders to the road.

## Size scales with loneliness

One more connection from Phase 1: the flywheel's workload depends on how often bangs arrive. A single-cylinder four-stroke fires once every two turns - long, lonely gaps - so it needs a large, heavy flywheel to coast through them. Add cylinders firing in turns (Phase 5) and a push is always arriving somewhere, so the gaps shrink and the flywheel has proportionally less to carry. More cylinders means the smoothness job is shared, and the flywheel can be lighter for the same smoothness.

## What this file is not

This file has not covered *how* the flywheel gets the engine spinning in the first place - a flywheel at rest is just a heavy disc, and momentum cannot bootstrap itself. Something else must spin it first, and that something is the starter motor: the next file. It also has not covered the clutch's gripping mechanics; that is file 4.3.

## Bridge to the next file

The flywheel carries the engine between bangs - but at the moment you turn the key, nothing has ever banged, the flywheel is still, and every part of the engine is at rest. Momentum cannot create momentum. `4.2 The Starter Motor - How the First Bang Happens.md` introduces the small electric motor whose only job is to break that deadlock - and explains the awful grinding noise you get for turning the key twice.
