# 4.4 Quiz - Flywheel, Starter and Clutch
## MCQ

Q: Why is the flywheel deliberately heavy?
A. To give the engine more rotating speed
B. A heavy spinning object resists changes to its spin, and that resistance carries the crankshaft through the strokes where nothing pushes
C. To make the starter gear mesh better
D. To balance the camshaft's weight
> B. The weight is the function: momentum. The power stroke tops up the flywheel's spin, and its refusal to slow carries the pistons through intake, compression and exhaust while smoothing the jerks between bangs.

---

Q: What are the two jobs of the toothed ring around the flywheel's edge?
A. Gripping the clutch and driving the gearbox
B. Receiving the starter motor's gear at startup - and nothing else
C. Cooling the engine
D. Timing the valves
> B. The teeth exist solely as the ring the starter's small gear meshes with. The clutch grips the flywheel's smooth flat face by friction, not teeth.

---

Q: What is the starter motor's job?
A. To power the spark plugs during cranking
B. To spin the engine a few rotations until combustion catches and the engine becomes self-sustaining
C. To pump fuel to the injectors
D. To keep the flywheel spinning while idling
> B. At key-off every part is at rest and the engine cannot start itself. The starter - a small electric motor on the battery - spins the flywheel and crankshaft until the first bang, then its gear retracts automatically.

---

Q: Why does the starter's gear retract automatically once the engine starts?
A. To save battery power
B. Because the running flywheel spins far faster than the starter could, and staying meshed would spin the small starter gear to destruction
C. To let the clutch engage
D. Because the ignition key springs back
> B. The mesh exists only while cranking. Once the engine runs, the flywheel would drive the little starter gear at absurd speed and wreck it, so the gear is designed to disengage as soon as combustion takes over.

---

Q: What causes the harsh grinding screech when the key is turned on an already-running engine?
A. The battery overheating
B. The starter gear trying to mesh with a flywheel already spinning fast - the teeth clash at different speeds instead of locking
C. The timing belt slipping
D. The clutch disengaging violently
> B. Teeth moving at completely different speeds cannot lock; they clash and skim, producing the screech. Repeated attempts chew the teeth on the starter gear or flywheel ring - an expensive repair modern cars try to prevent with a lockout.

---

Q: When your foot is off the clutch pedal, what is the clutch's state?
A. Disengaged - waiting to be used
B. Engaged - the springs hold the clutch plate squeezed against the flywheel, so engine and wheels are connected
C. Half-engaged, regardless of driving
D. It depends on which gear is selected
> B. The resting state is engaged: the pressure plate's springs constantly squeeze the clutch plate to the flywheel. Pressing the pedal pulls the plate back against the springs - the foot works against the springs, so pressing separates instead of joining.

---

Q: What is the part of the clutch assembly that wears out and needs replacement?
A. The flywheel, which is cast iron
B. The pressure plate springs
C. The clutch plate's friction material, which thins every time the two sides spin at different speeds during engagement
D. The input shaft bearings only
> C. The clutch plate's faces carry friction material - organic composites, ceramics or Kevlar blends. Each engagement or disengagement at unequal speeds slides the material briefly, wearing it slowly until the plate no longer grips and must be replaced.

---

Q: Releasing the clutch pedal too fast from a standstill makes the car lurch, and doing it without any gas can kill the engine. What is the mechanism?
A. The starter motor re-engages and fights the engine
B. The sudden full grip locks the idle-speed engine to a stationary car, and dragging the car's whole weight from zero pulls the engine below the speed it needs to keep running
C. The gearbox selects the wrong ratio
D. The flywheel loses its momentum instantly
> B. With the pedal up fast, the pressure plate slams the clutch plate against the flywheel, locking two very different speeds together instantly. The idle engine - alive but with little spare force - is forced to accelerate the car's entire weight, bogs, and falls below its self-sustaining floor: a stall. The gentle release through the bite point lets the grip build gradually so the speeds converge instead of slamming.

---

## TrueFalse

Q: The flywheel stores energy like a battery, releasing measured portions between power strokes.
- True
- False
> False. The flywheel is not a battery - it does not hand out measured doses. It is simply a heavy spinning mass that resists slowing, and its momentum bridges the gaps until the next bang tops it up.

---

Q: The starter motor drives the pistons and camshaft directly.
- True
- False
> False. It meshes with the flywheel's toothed ring, turning the crankshaft; the pistons follow through the rod-and-throw link, and the camshaft follows through the timing belt. One input point, the whole connected machine moves.

---

Q: A clutch connects the engine to the wheels by gears, matching tooth to tooth.
- True
- False
> False. The clutch junction has no gears - the clutch plate grips the flywheel's flat face by friction: two rough surfaces pressed hard enough behave as one. Teeth would clash whenever the two sides spun at different speeds.

---

Q: A multi-cylinder engine can use a lighter flywheel than a single-cylinder engine of similar size.
- True
- False
> True. With cylinders firing in turns, a power push arrives more often, so the gaps between pushes are shorter and the flywheel has less coasting to do. A lonely single cylinder needs a large, heavy disc to smooth one bang per two turns.

---

Q: Idling means the engine has shut down and is coasting.
- True
- False
> False. Idling is the engine running at a few hundred rpm up to about a thousand - alive and self-sustaining, but with no accelerator input and only a sliver of spare force. That sliver is exactly why a fast clutch release at a standstill, which forces the idle engine to accelerate the car's whole weight, drops it below its floor and stalls it.

---

## ShortAnswer

Q: Write the complete starting chain from turning the key to a self-sustaining engine, naming every link.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** The battery powers the starter motor; its small gear pushes out and meshes with the toothed ring on the flywheel; the flywheel turns, rotating the crankshaft it is bolted to; the crankshaft drags the pistons through their strokes while the timing belt turns the camshaft, so the valves open and close in rhythm; within a second or two a power stroke fires on its own, the engine becomes self-sustaining, and the starter's gear retracts automatically so the fast flywheel cannot wreck it.

</details>

---

Q: A friend says "pressing the clutch pedal engages the clutch to the engine." Fix the statement and explain the mechanism that makes the truth feel backwards.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** It is the opposite: pressing the pedal disengages the clutch, separating the clutch plate from the flywheel so the engine spins free of the wheels. The mechanism behind the reversal is the springs: the pressure plate's springs constantly squeeze the clutch plate against the flywheel, making engaged the resting state. Pressing the pedal pulls the pressure plate back - the foot works against the springs, overpowering the connection they want to keep. Foot down: disengaged. Foot up: engaged.

</details>
