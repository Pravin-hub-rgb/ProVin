# 7.1 What 1.5 Litre Means - Displacement

## The question, restated with the tools you now have

"1.5 litre petrol" is describing the engine's **size** - but not its weight, length, or physical bulk. It is describing something you have been reading all course long: the volume the pistons move through.

## The definition: displacement

A single piston, during one full cycle, travels from its highest position to its lowest and back. The space it **sweeps** in one direction of travel is the cylinder's **swept volume**: the cross-sectional area of the cylinder multiplied by the distance the piston travels (the stroke).

**Displacement is the total swept volume of all the engine's cylinders added together.** That is the entire definition - one litre is a unit of volume, and "1.5 litre engine" means the pistons collectively sweep 1.5 litres of space per full cycle.

## The units, and a worked example

Engine sizes are quoted in two interchangeable units:

- **Cubic centimetres (cc)** - the small unit. 1,000 cc = 1 litre.
- **Litres (L)** - the readable form. 1.5 L = 1,500 cc.

So a "1.5 litre petrol engine" displaces 1,500 cc. If it is a four-cylinder engine - the common configuration from Phase 5 - the arithmetic is a simple split: **four cylinders, about 375 cc each**. If it is a three-cylinder engine, each cylinder is about 500 cc. The litre figure says nothing about how the volume is divided; the cylinder count does that, which is why the spec sheet states both ("1.5 L, 4-cylinder") and why they belong on the same line.

A size ladder worth memorizing, since it maps directly onto real cars: small city cars run 1.0 to 1.2 L; mainstream hatchbacks and sedans run 1.2 to 1.5 L; larger cars and SUVs run 1.5 to 2.5 L; performance and luxury machines go higher, and a V8's eight cylinders add up fast - which is why "V8" in a racing video carries a weight that "1.5" does not.

## Why size matters at all

Displacement measures the engine's **breathing capacity per cycle**. A bigger swept volume inhales more air-fuel mixture on every intake stroke, and more mixture means a bigger bang on every power stroke. All else equal, a 2.0 L engine burns and shoves with half again as much mixture per cycle as a 1.3 L engine.

"All else equal" is doing work in that sentence, and the next sentence is the file's most important caveat.

## What displacement is NOT

**Displacement is not power.** It is a capacity - how much the engine *can* process per cycle - not a promise of what it *will* produce. Two 1.5 L engines can make noticeably different power because power depends on everything the course has built: how freely the engine breathes (valve design, camshaft timing), how high it spins (revs - file 7.3), and whether it is **turbocharged** (file 7.2) - a device that lets a 1.5 L engine breathe like a much larger one. Engine makers exploit exactly this gap: a modern turbocharged 1.5 L can out-power an older naturally-aspirated 2.0 L, which is why "1.5 turbo" appears on so many current spec sheets.

So read displacement as the engine's lung capacity, quoted in litres - and then read the rest of the sheet to see what the engine does with that capacity.
