RPM gave the axis; now the whole sheet can be read. This is the course's capstone: one real-looking engine specification, decoded field by field, using nothing but the machines you have built across seven phases. After this file, an engine spec sheet reads like plain language.

# 7.4 Reading a Full Spec Sheet

## The sheet, stated plainly

Here is a typical line from a modern hatchback's brochure:

> **1.5 L, 4-cylinder inline, DOHC 16-valve, turbo petrol, 110 hp at 6,000 rpm, 150 Nm at 3,500 rpm, 6-speed manual, front-wheel drive**

Before this course, that sentence was noise. Watch it become a sentence you could have written.

## Field by field

**"1.5 L, 4-cylinder inline"** - Displacement and layout (files 7.1 and 5.2). The pistons collectively sweep 1.5 litres - 1,500 cc, about 375 cc per cylinder - arranged four in a row, a slant-four or compact vertical block. Nothing about power yet; this is the engine's lung capacity.

**"DOHC 16-valve"** - Valve hardware (file 1.5). Sixteen valves across four cylinders is four per cylinder - two intake and two exhaust each - a breathing-friendly arrangement. DOHC - **Double OverHead Camshaft** - means two camshafts sit in the head: one operating the intake valves, one the exhaust valves, each driven by the chain or belt at half crankshaft speed (file 1.4's geometry). Sixteen valves and DOHC together say: this engine was built to breathe.

**"turbo petrol"** - Induction and fuel (files 7.2, 3.1). The intake air is pressurized by an exhaust-driven turbocharger before it reaches the cylinders - the 1.5 litres breathe like a larger engine - and the fuel is petrol, which means ignition by spark plug and a knocking margin the engine must guard with an intercooler and fuel-grade sensitivity.

**"110 hp at 6,000 rpm"** - Peak power and where it lives (file 7.3). **hp** - horsepower - is the rate of doing work: how much pushing per second the engine delivers. The engine's output peaks at 110 hp, but only *at* 6,000 rpm - near the top of its band, below the redline. At 2,000 rpm in traffic, it makes far less; the number is a peak location, not a flat guarantee.

**"150 Nm at 3,500 rpm"** - Peak torque and where it lives (files 6.1, 7.3). **Nm** - newton-metres - is the unit of **torque**: the turning force available at the crankshaft, the twist the flywheel carries into the clutch. This is the file 6.1 figure - the engine-side force that the gearbox's ratios will trade against speed. 150 Nm peaking at 3,500 rpm, well down in the mid-range, says this engine pulls strongly in everyday rev territory - a usable, flexible tune rather than a top-end sprint.

**"6-speed manual"** - Transmission (files 6.1-6.3). Six selectable gear pairs behind a driver-operated clutch: six ratios to hold the engine inside its power band across the wheels' whole speed range, plus reverse. The low first pair for launch force, the tall sixth for relaxed highway cruising, and everything between chosen by the driver's hand and the sliding collar.

**"front-wheel drive"** - Where the power goes (file 6.4). The differential and axles drive the front wheels - the engine, gearbox and differential are packaged together at the front, and the drive shaft's final-drive pair feeds the front axle's differential. Front-wheel drive is the layout of most mainstream cars; rear-wheel drive layouts move the differential to the rear axle.

## The horsepower story, and the words around it

The "hp" in the sheet deserves its own paragraph, because the name is a piece of history that loops back to this course's first file. **James Watt** - the Scottish engineer whose improvements turned the early steam engine of the 1700s into a commercially practical machine - sold his engines to mine and mill owners whose work was currently done by horses. Buyers asked the obvious question: how many horses does one engine replace? To answer, Watt measured what a strong horse could sustain - how hard it pulled, how fast it walked - and fixed that work rate as one unit: one **horsepower**. It was a sales tool that became a permanent unit; "110 hp" literally means this engine can do the steady work of 110 of Watt's horses.

And horsepower ties the sheet's two numbers into one: **horsepower is torque multiplied by rpm** (with a fixed conversion constant to make the units come out right). That single fact explains why the two figures are quoted at different rpm - and how different engines reach their power. A diesel truck engine makes enormous torque but modest horsepower, because it cannot rev high: a strong twist, slowly. A small sports engine makes modest torque but high horsepower by screaming toward redline: a lighter twist, extremely fast. The car-meet shorthand says it best - torque is the push you feel against your back; horsepower is what decides the top speed.

A closing pocket-dictionary for car meets, since every term on it is now within reach: **revs** and the **redline** are file 7.3's tachometer story; **boost** is the turbo's intake pressure (file 7.2); **NA** - naturally aspirated - means no turbo or supercharger, breathing only on its own suction; a **tune** or **remap** is changing the engine computer's settings for more power; a **swap** is replacing the engine with a different one (dropping a 2JZ into another car is the classic example); and a **dyno** is the machine that measures an engine's actual power at the wheels.

## The full chain, one last time

Read the sheet's sentence again - this time it unfolds into the complete machine:

**Fuel and air → bang → piston → connecting rod → crankshaft → flywheel → clutch → gearbox → drive shaft → differential → axles → wheels.**

Every field of the spec sheet sits on one station of that chain: displacement and layout on the cylinders, valves and camshafts on the breathing, turbo on the induction, hp and Nm on the crankshaft's output, the gearbox and clutch on the transmission, the differential on the final split to the road. One sentence, seven phases, no unknown words left.

## What this file is not

A spec sheet's numbers are **peak figures measured at specific rpm under controlled conditions** - not promises about everyday driving, and not directly comparable across manufacturers who measure slightly differently. Real-world feel also depends on gearing, weight and tuning choices the sheet's single line cannot carry. Two engines with identical printed figures can drive very differently. The sheet is a map of the machine's abilities, not the machine itself.
