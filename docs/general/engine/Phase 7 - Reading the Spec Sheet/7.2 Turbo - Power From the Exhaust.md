Displacement set an engine's breathing capacity per cycle - and left one door open: what if you could push *more* air into the same cylinders? That is exactly what a turbocharger does, and it does it with energy the engine was already throwing away. This file answers the second parked question from this course's first page: turbo boost.

# 7.2 Turbo - Power From the Exhaust

## The waste the engine produces every cycle

Watch a four-stroke cycle's accounting once more. The power stroke uses the burning mixture's expansion to shove the piston - but the expansion is not perfectly harvested. When the exhaust valve opens (file 1.3), the leftover gas leaves **still hot and still pressurized**, carrying energy the engine got nothing from. That energy is the exhaust's temperature and pressure, and in a naturally-aspirated engine - one that breathes only on its own suction - it simply exits into the atmosphere, wasted, every cycle, forever.

That waste stream is what a turbo lives on.

## Why the engine cannot simply breathe more

First, the question the turbo exists to answer. Fuel is never the bottleneck - the tank holds plenty, and the injectors (file 3.2) can spray as much as asked. The bottleneck is air, and the reason sits in the intake stroke itself: the piston moving down only *makes room*, and the outside air pushes in to fill it at ordinary atmospheric pressure. Suction can fill the cylinder; it cannot pack anything extra in. So the air per cycle is capped by two factors - cylinder volume times outside air density - and that cap limits how much fuel can burn, and therefore how big each bang can be.

The turbo's idea is to attack the second factor. Air, like any gas, can be **compressed**: squeezed into a smaller space, so more of it fits and it becomes **denser** - more air molecules in the same volume. Pack the intake air denser before it enters, and the same cylinders breathe more per cycle. More air means the fuel system adds proportionally more fuel (Phase 3), and more of both means a bigger burn on every bang. Same engine size, bigger bangs.

## The two wheels and one shaft

A **turbocharger** is two wheels on one shaft, one at each end of the engine's breathing:

- The **turbine** sits in the **exhaust path**. The escaping exhaust gas - hot and pressurized, the waste stream just described - blows through its blades and spins it. The turbine is a wheel driven entirely by the engine's own waste.
- The **compressor** sits in the **intake path**, on the same shaft as the turbine. As the shaft spins, the compressor wheel pressurizes the incoming air and packs it into the cylinders **denser than the atmosphere would deliver it**.

So the loop runs: exhaust spins the turbine, the shaft carries the spin to the compressor, the compressor force-feeds the intake. The engine's waste product is recycled into the engine's food supply.

Physically, each wheel sits in its own spiral-shaped housing, and the two housings sit back to back at the ends of one short shaft. On the exhaust side - the **turbine housing** - gas from the cylinders enters the spiral, flows around it and pushes the turbine's blades on its way to the tailpipe. On the intake side - the **compressor housing** - the spinning compressor blades pull clean air in through the centre of the spiral and fling it outward; the spiral channel narrows as it goes, and that narrowing is what squeezes the air before it exits toward the engine. The two airs never meet: only the shaft connects the sides. That shaft spins at tens of thousands of rpm, so oil flowing through the middle of the housing keeps its bearings cool and lubricated - the small cooling section every turbo diagram shows between the two snails. The turbo itself sits tucked against the engine - usually beside the exhaust manifold, where the burnt gas leaves the cylinders - because it needs that exhaust the moment it exists; what is visible at the front of an engine bay is the intake pipework and the intercooler, not the turbo.

## Why denser air means more power

Follow the chain to the bang. The cylinders can only process what fills them: air plus injected fuel. When the intake air arrives pressurized - denser, more of it in the same cylinder volume - the engine's controllers (the injectors and computer from Phase 3) add fuel in proportion. More air **and** more fuel per cycle means a bigger burn and a bigger push, on every power stroke.

And the beautiful part: the cylinders themselves did not grow. A turbocharged 1.5 L engine still displaces 1.5 litres (file 7.1) - it just breathes as if it were larger. That is why the phrase from file 7.1 works: a 1.5 turbo can out-power a 2.0 without a turbo.

## Boost, and its price

The extra intake pressure a turbo generates is called **boost**. More boost means denser intake, more fuel, bigger bangs - and boost is therefore the knob tuners reach for. But the physics of file 3.1 sets a limit on the petrol side of the house: compressing air heats it - the very heat a diesel engine relies on (file 3.1) - and the heat undoes the turbo's own work twice over. First, hot air expands again, so fewer molecules fit back into the same cylinder volume - part of the density gain is thrown away. Second, hotter, denser air raises the risk of the petrol mixture self-igniting early - **knocking**. Turbocharged petrol engines therefore cool the compressed air before it enters the cylinders with a radiator for the intake charge called an **intercooler**: cooler air is denser again, and the margin against knocking survives. Turbocharged petrol engines also stay sensitive to fuel quality, because that margin narrows as boost rises.

Diesels, whose fuel is *designed* to self-ignite from compression heat (file 3.1), have no such conflict - which is one reason turbos became universal on modern diesel cars: the turbo's denser air burns proportionally more diesel with no knocking penalty to fear.

## Turbo lag, and the twin answer

The turbine is spun by exhaust flow, so it can only push as hard as the exhaust is pushing. At low engine speeds, exhaust flow is weak, the turbine spins slowly, and the compressor delivers little extra air - press the accelerator and the extra power arrives only as the revs climb and the exhaust strengthens the turbine. That delay between demanding power and receiving it is **turbo lag**.

One common countermeasure is running **two** turbos - the "twin turbo" from this course's opening page. Two arrangements exist. In the **parallel** layout, two identical smaller turbos each serve half the cylinders - less exhaust feeds each one, and small wheels spool faster than big ones, so the lag shrinks. In the **sequential** layout, two turbos work in turns: one alone at low revs for quick response, the second joining at high revs for full top-end air. And the engine nickname from file 5.2 closes its own loop here: the **2JZ**, Toyota's inline-six, is famous among tuners precisely because its internals tolerate large amounts of boost reliably - it is an engine people buy to turbocharge (its twin-turbo GTE version ran the sequential arrangement).

## Lift off, and the air has nowhere to go

One last piece needs a part this course named only in passing: the **throttle**, the disc-shaped flap in the intake pipe that the accelerator pedal opens and closes (file 3.3). In a turbo engine the intake path runs, in order: air filter → compressor → intercooler → throttle → cylinders.

Now lift off the gas mid-boost. The throttle snaps shut, but the compressor - spun by exhaust that is still flowing - keeps pushing air into the pipe in front of it. That trapped air has nowhere to go, and its pressure pushes back against the compressor's blades, dragging down the wheel that took so long to spool up. The fix is a small spring-loaded valve on that stretch of pipe between compressor and throttle: the **blow-off valve**, which pops open at exactly that moment and vents the trapped air out. It is the source of the sharp *pssh* sound turbo cars make when the driver lifts off - literally the sound of trapped boost escaping so the turbo can keep spinning freely. (Turbo cars have a second, gentler sound: the rising whistle under hard acceleration is simply the compressor spinning at its tens of thousands of rpm.)

## What this file is not

A turbo is **not a fuel pump** and **not a bigger engine**. It adds no fuel of its own and adds no displacement - it raises the intake air's density so the existing fuel system and existing cylinders can process more per cycle. It is also not the only forced-air design: a **supercharger** does the same compression job but is driven by a belt off the crankshaft instead of by exhaust - instant response with no lag at all, but it consumes engine power to run, which is the trade the turbo avoids by living on waste. The best-known supercharger designs squeeze the air between two interlocking screws rather than a wheel - the **twin-screw** type, of which Whipple is a famous maker - and a belt-driven supercharger rising through a car's hood is the classic image of an American V8 built for power.
