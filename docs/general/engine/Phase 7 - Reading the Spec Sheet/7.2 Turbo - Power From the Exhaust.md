Displacement set an engine's breathing capacity per cycle - and left one door open: what if you could push *more* air into the same cylinders? That is exactly what a turbocharger does, and it does it with energy the engine was already throwing away. This file answers the second parked question from this course's first page: turbo boost.

# 7.2 Turbo - Power From the Exhaust

## The waste the engine produces every cycle

Watch a four-stroke cycle's accounting once more. The power stroke uses the burning mixture's expansion to shove the piston - but the expansion is not perfectly harvested. When the exhaust valve opens (file 1.3), the leftover gas leaves **still hot and still pressurized**, carrying energy the engine got nothing from. That energy is the exhaust's temperature and pressure, and in a naturally-aspirated engine - one that breathes only on its own suction - it simply exits into the atmosphere, wasted, every cycle, forever.

That waste stream is what a turbo lives on.

## The two wheels and one shaft

A **turbocharger** is two wheels on one shaft, one at each end of the engine's breathing:

- The **turbine** sits in the **exhaust path**. The escaping exhaust gas - hot and pressurized, the waste stream just described - blows through its blades and spins it. The turbine is a wheel driven entirely by the engine's own waste.
- The **compressor** sits in the **intake path**, on the same shaft as the turbine. As the shaft spins, the compressor wheel pressurizes the incoming air and packs it into the cylinders **denser than the atmosphere would deliver it**.

So the loop runs: exhaust spins the turbine, the shaft carries the spin to the compressor, the compressor force-feeds the intake. The engine's waste product is recycled into the engine's food supply.

## Why denser air means more power

Follow the chain to the bang. The cylinders can only process what fills them: air plus injected fuel. When the intake air arrives pressurized - denser, more of it in the same cylinder volume - the engine's controllers (the injectors and computer from Phase 3) add fuel in proportion. More air **and** more fuel per cycle means a bigger burn and a bigger push, on every power stroke.

And the beautiful part: the cylinders themselves did not grow. A turbocharged 1.5 L engine still displaces 1.5 litres (file 7.1) - it just breathes as if it were larger. That is why the phrase from file 7.1 works: a 1.5 turbo can out-power a 2.0 without a turbo.

## Boost, and its price

The extra intake pressure a turbo generates is called **boost**. More boost means denser intake, more fuel, bigger bangs - and boost is therefore the knob tuners reach for. But the physics of file 3.1 sets a limit on the petrol side of the house: compressing air heats it - the very heat a diesel engine relies on (file 3.1) - and hotter, denser air raises the risk of the petrol mixture self-igniting early - **knocking**. Turbocharged petrol engines therefore cool the compressed air before it enters the cylinders with a radiator for the intake charge called an **intercooler**, and they stay sensitive to fuel quality, because the margin against knocking narrows as boost rises.

Diesels, whose fuel is *designed* to self-ignite from compression heat (file 3.1), have no such conflict - which is one reason turbos became universal on modern diesel cars: the turbo's denser air burns proportionally more diesel with no knocking penalty to fear.

## Turbo lag, and the twin answer

The turbine is spun by exhaust flow, so it can only push as hard as the exhaust is pushing. At low engine speeds, exhaust flow is weak, the turbine spins slowly, and the compressor delivers little extra air - press the accelerator and the extra power arrives only as the revs climb and the exhaust strengthens the turbine. That delay between demanding power and receiving it is **turbo lag**.

One common countermeasure is running **two** turbos - the "twin turbo" from this course's opening page. Smaller turbines spool faster (less lag), and in a V engine each bank can carry its own. And the engine nickname from file 5.2 closes its own loop here: the **2JZ**, Toyota's inline-six, is famous among tuners precisely because its internals tolerate large amounts of boost reliably - it is an engine people buy to turbocharge.

## What this file is not

A turbo is **not a fuel pump** and **not a bigger engine**. It adds no fuel of its own and adds no displacement - it raises the intake air's density so the existing fuel system and existing cylinders can process more per cycle. It is also not the only forced-air design: a **supercharger** does the same compression job but is driven by a belt off the crankshaft instead of by exhaust - instant response, but it consumes engine power to run, which is the trade the turbo avoids by living on waste.
