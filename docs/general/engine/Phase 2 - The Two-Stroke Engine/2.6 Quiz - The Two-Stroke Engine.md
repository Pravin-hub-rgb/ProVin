# 2.6 Quiz - The Two-Stroke Engine
## MCQ

Q: What was the two-stroke's actual design goal?
A. To make the crankshaft turn faster than in a four-stroke
B. To get more power pushes per turn - a bang on every rotation, in a lighter and simpler package
C. To make the engine quieter than a four-stroke
D. To burn less fuel than a four-stroke
> B. Shaft rotation is the result, not the target. The goal was twice the power pushes for the same cylinder size - one bang per rotation instead of one per two turns - with the valve apparatus deleted.

---

Q: What replaces the valves in a two-stroke engine?
A. Spring-loaded flaps in the intake pipe
B. Ports - fixed holes in the cylinder wall, covered and uncovered by the sliding piston itself
C. A rotating disc driven by the crankshaft
D. The spark plug's ceramic tip
> B. Ports are simple fixed holes; the piston's sliding body stands in front of them or drops past them. The piston is its own valve, deleting the camshaft, lobes, springs and timing belt.

---

Q: In the worked example (10 cm cylinder, ports at 4 cm, piston top face travelling 1 to 5), when are the ports open?
A. When the piston is at the top of its travel
B. When the piston's top face has dropped below the 4 cm mark, leaving the ports in the open space above it
C. Always - ports never close
D. Only while the piston moves upward
> B. At the top the piston's body spans 1 to 5, covering the port at 4. At the bottom the top face is at 5 and the body spans 5 to 9, so the port at 4 sits above the piston - open. Ports open only near the bottom of the stroke.

---

Q: Where does the fresh mixture in a two-stroke enter the machine?
A. Directly into the cylinder through the transfer port
B. Through the intake port on the crankcase wall, into the sealed crankcase
C. Through the exhaust port on the way out
D. Through the spark plug hole
> B. The intake port is on the crankcase wall: fresh mixture enters the sealed crankcase first, is stored there, then travels up the transfer passage into the cylinder through the transfer port. Two hops, same gas.

---

Q: What pushes the stored mixture from the crankcase into the cylinder?
A. Suction created by the rising piston above it
B. Gravity, since the crankcase sits lower
C. The descending piston squeezing the sealed crankcase, pressurizing the mixture so it shoots up the transfer passage
D. A small electric pump
> C. On the down-stroke the piston crushes the sealed crankcase into a smaller space, so the stored mixture is pressurized. When the transfer port opens near the bottom, the pressure drives the mixture up the passage into the cylinder.

---

Q: Why do two-stroke engines need oil mixed into the petrol?
A. Oil makes the fuel burn hotter
B. The sealed crankcase is filled with fuel mixture, so there is no separate oil supply - oil must travel with the fuel to lubricate the parts
C. Oil cools the spark plug
D. Petrol alone is too slippery for the ports
> B. A four-stroke lubricates from oil sitting in its crankcase; the two-stroke's crankcase is occupied by the fuel charge. So oil rides with the mixture - lubricating on the way - and burns with the fuel afterward.

---

## TrueFalse

Q: The two-stroke was invented before the four-stroke as a simpler first attempt.
- True
- False
> False. The four-stroke came first (Otto, 1876). The two-stroke arrived in the 1880s as a deliberate simplification of the four-stroke, attacking its weakness of one bang per two turns.

---

Q: In a two-stroke, the hole on the cylinder wall is called the intake port.
- True
- False
> False. The cylinder-wall hole is the transfer port - it transfers stored mixture from the crankcase into the cylinder. The intake port is the hole on the crankcase wall where mixture enters the machine from outside.

---

Q: In a two-stroke, the up-stroke performs compression above the piston and intake below it at the same time.
- True
- False
> True. The rising piston squeezes the trapped mixture above while enlarging the sealed crankcase below, whose falling pressure sucks fresh mixture in through the intake port. Two jobs, one stroke.

---

Q: Modern cars use two-stroke engines because they are more fuel-efficient.
- True
- False
> False. Modern cars are almost all four-stroke. The two-stroke wastes part of every fresh dose - some follows the smoke out the exhaust during the shared port window - which made it less efficient, dirtier, and unable to meet emissions rules from the 1970s onward.

## ShortAnswer

Q: Walk through one full two-stroke cycle using the three ports, saying what each side of the piston does in each stroke.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Up-stroke: above the piston, the trapped mixture is compressed and the spark fires at the top; below, the growing sealed crankcase sucks fresh mixture in through the intake port. Down-stroke: the burning mixture shoves the piston down - the power push - while below, the squeezing crankcase pressurizes the stored mixture. Near the bottom, the piston uncovers both cylinder-wall ports: burnt gas escapes through the exhaust port while pressurized fresh mixture shoots up the transfer passage into the cylinder, helping push the smoke out. The piston then rises, covers both ports, and compresses the new dose. One bang per rotation.

</details>

---

Q: A two-stroke and a four-stroke have the same cylinder size. Give two structural reasons why the two-stroke makes more power pushes, and two structural reasons why it wastes fuel.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** More pushes: every stroke carries two jobs (compression plus intake on the way up, power plus exhaust-refill on the way down), so the cycle completes in one rotation instead of two - one bang per turn versus one per two turns. Wasted fuel: the exhaust and transfer ports stand open simultaneously near the bottom, so part of each fresh dose follows the burnt gas out of the exhaust; and the oil mixed into the petrol burns with the fuel, adding to the exhaust's waste.

</details>
