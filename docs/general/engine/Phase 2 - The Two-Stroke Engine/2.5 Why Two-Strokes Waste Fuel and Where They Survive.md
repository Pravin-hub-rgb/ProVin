The last file delivered the two-stroke's promise: one bang per rotation, twice the pushes per turn, no valves, no camshaft. Now comes the invoice. The same design choices that produced the punch also produce waste, and following that waste explains both a fuel-station ritual and which engines won the roads.

# 2.5 Why Two-Strokes Waste Fuel and Where They Survive

## The flaw: one window, two flows

Go back to the down-stroke's port window from file 2.4. Near the bottom of the stroke, the exhaust port and the transfer port stand open **at the same time** - burnt gas leaving through one, pressurized fresh mixture shooting in through the other, and the incoming mixture helping shove the smoke out.

The flaw is hiding in that sentence. The fresh mixture's job is to push smoke out - but gas does not aim. While the two flows share the window, a portion of the fresh, unburnt mixture follows the smoke straight out the exhaust port, never getting compressed, never getting burnt, never pushing anything. It leaves the engine as wasted fuel, and it leaves the exhaust as unburnt hydrocarbons - smoke and smell.

This is not a tuning error that a better mechanic could fix. It is baked into the geometry: two flows, one window, no wall between them. **A two-stroke wastes part of every dose by design.** That is one reason it is less fuel-efficient than a four-stroke, and dirtier with it.

## The second bill: oil, and the petrol-station ritual

Recall the strange sharing from file 2.3: the two-stroke's sealed crankcase is full of fresh air-and-petrol mixture - the fuel and the mechanism share a room. Now ask: how do the moving parts down there get lubricated?

A four-stroke has a separate oil system: oil sits in the crankcase, splashes and pumps around the bearings and cylinder wall, and stays there, recirculating. The two-stroke *cannot* have that, because its crankcase is occupied by fuel mixture - there is no separate oil supply.

The solution is the ritual: **oil is mixed directly into the petrol** before it ever enters the engine. The oil travels with the mixture - into the crankcase, around the spinning parts it must protect, then up the transfer passage into the cylinder, where it burns along with the fuel. Lubrication is delivered by the fuel itself.

This is the extra liquid that two-stroke scooters demand: petrol pre-mixed with oil in a measured ratio, mixed by hand at refuelling time on older machines. And it explains a second visible cost: because the oil burns with the fuel, a two-stroke's exhaust carries burnt oil as well as unburnt petrol - the blue haze trailing behind an old two-wheeler is both bills being paid at once.

## The verdict: which engines won the roads

Put the two designs side by side and each owns a column of the ledger. The two-stroke has: one bang per rotation, half the moving parts, no camshaft or timing belt, light weight, and punch for its size. The four-stroke has: a dedicated refill stroke with no leakage window, a separate oil system, and clean, efficient burning.

For cars, the four-stroke won every column that matters on a road: it uses fuel more completely, exhausts cleaner, and runs quieter. The decisive shove was **emissions regulation from roughly the 1970s onward** - legal limits on what an exhaust may release. The two-stroke's structural waste (unburnt mixture out the exhaust, oil burnt in the charge) made those limits nearly unreachable, and two-strokes faded from road vehicles almost everywhere.

Modern cars - petrol *and* diesel - are four-stroke machines, without exception in practice. Electric cars sit outside this entire course: no cylinders, no bangs, no stroke counting at all.

## Where the two-stroke still lives

The two-stroke did not die; it retreated to the jobs where its column of the ledger matters more. **Chainsaws, older scooters and small outboard boat motors** are its homeland. In a chainsaw, the priorities are: as light as possible (it is held in one hand), as simple as possible (it gets knocked around), and as punchy as possible for its tiny size. Fuel inefficiency over a few minutes of cutting costs pennies; adding a camshaft and valve gear to a machine that must weigh almost nothing costs the product. There, the two-stroke's trade - waste and smoke in exchange for simplicity and punch - is still the winning trade.

## What this file is not

This file does not cover the two-stroke's later engineering attempts to plug its leaks - designs with extra valves added back, direct fuel injection that avoids the shared window, and racing uses. Those are refinements on the same skeleton, and the skeleton is what this course teaches. It also does not touch what happens when a two-stroke is run on straight, un-mixed petrol: the brief answer is that the crankcase parts run dry, and that is exactly why the ritual exists.
