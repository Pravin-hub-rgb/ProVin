The last file set the goal: a bang on every shaft turn, with two jobs happening on every stroke. The first step toward that is subtraction - delete the parts that force jobs to take separate strokes. Those parts are the valves and everything that drives them: the camshaft, the lobes, the springs, the timing belt. Their replacement is the cleanest trick in this course: the piston becomes the valve.

# 2.2 Ports - The Piston Becomes the Valve

## What gets deleted, what replaces it

In the two-stroke design, the top of the cylinder has no valves at all - only the **spark plug**. The breathing holes move out of the head and into the **side wall** of the cylinder, where they are called **ports**: simple, fixed holes cut straight through the wall.

A fixed hole has no moving parts and can never open or close itself. So the closing is done by the only moving part available: the **piston**. As the piston slides up and down past a port, its body either stands in front of the hole - shutting it - or has slid down past it, leaving the hole open to the space above the piston.

That is the entire mechanism: **the piston is its own valve.** No camshaft, no timing belt, no springs. The piston's position in its travel *is* the valve state, because the piston's body is the shutter.

## The numbers: one worked example

Words like "covers" and "uncovers" are easy to tangle, so this file uses a fixed worked example with real numbers, and every later file in this phase reuses it. Set the scale once:

- The cylinder is **10 cm** long. Position **0** is the top, where the spark plug sits. Position **10** is the bottom of the cylinder.
- The piston is a plug about **4 cm tall** - its top face is the flat surface that bounds the chamber above it, and its body hangs 4 cm below that face.
- The piston's top face travels between **1 cm** at its highest and **5 cm** at its lowest. That leaves a 1 cm gap under the spark plug at the top, and a 1 cm gap below the piston's lowest position.
- The ports are cut into the wall at **4 cm** - low, but not at the very bottom.

Now check both extremes:

**Piston at the top:** the top face is at 1 cm, so the 4 cm-tall body spans positions 1 to 5. The wall at 4 cm sits inside that span - the piston's body is standing in front of it. **The ports are covered.**

**Piston at the bottom:** the top face is at 5 cm, so the body spans 5 to 9. The wall at 4 cm is now *above* the piston's top face - inside the chamber space, with nothing in front of it. **The ports are open.**

## The rule, extracted from the numbers

From the worked example, the general rule for any port position:

- A port is **covered** while the piston's body is standing in front of it - which happens whenever the piston is high in its travel.
- A port is **open** once the piston's top face has slid down below the port's position, leaving the port inside the open space above the piston.

With ports at 4 cm and a top face travelling 1 to 5, the ports are shut for most of the stroke and open only in the last stretch near the bottom. That narrow window is not a flaw - it is the timing. The two-stroke is built entirely around *when* those holes breathe, and the next two files schedule everything into that window.

## Why this matters for the goal

Compare the part count with the four-stroke: the intake valve, exhaust valve, both springs, the camshaft with its lobes, and the timing belt are all gone, replaced by holes in the wall and the piston that happens to slide past them. Fewer parts, less weight, less to maintain - exactly the "simpler package" the two-stroke promised in file 2.1.

And notice what the piston's body does *not* do: it does not seal the port against the space below the piston. What lies below the piston in a two-stroke is a subject of its own - the sealed crankcase - and it is the next file's opening.

## What this file is not

This file has only described *how* the holes open and shut, not yet what flows through them or when. Two ports have been implied - one for burnt gas to escape, one for fresh mixture to arrive - but their full identities, including the fact that the fresh mixture's journey starts *below* the piston, need one more part: the sealed crankcase. That is the next file.

It also does not explain what pushes fresh mixture through a hole that opens for a fraction of a second - pressurizing that mixture is the crankcase's secret job.
