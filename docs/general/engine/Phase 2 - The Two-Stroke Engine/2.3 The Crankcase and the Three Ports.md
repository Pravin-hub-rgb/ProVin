The ports are cut, the piston operates them, and one question is left hanging in the air: where does the fresh mixture come *from*, and what pushes it through a hole that stands open for only a fraction of a second? The answer reuses a part every engine already has, for a job no four-stroke ever asked of it. This file completes the two-stroke's plumbing and gives you the full map of all three holes.

# 2.3 The Crankcase and the Three Ports

## The crankcase, redefined

Every engine has a **crankcase** - the housing around the crankshaft, below the cylinder. In the four-stroke it is just a box the crankshaft spins in. The two-stroke does something bolder: it **seals** the crankcase completely airtight, and uses the space around the spinning crankshaft as a **storage tank for fresh mixture**.

Pause on how strange that is. The crankcase sits *below* the piston, contains the rotating crankshaft and connecting rod - oily, mechanical territory - and this design fills that same space with the engine's next dose of air and petrol mist. The fuel and the mechanism share a room. The consequences of that sharing show up at the end of this phase (file 2.5); for now, just hold the fact: **in a two-stroke, the sealed crankcase holds fresh mixture.**

## The transfer passage: the route around the piston

The stored mixture sits below the piston. The chamber it needs to reach sits above it. The piston is in the way, and no mixture can leak past its sealing rings. So the design builds a detour: a small tunnel called the **transfer passage**, running up beside the cylinder wall, connecting the sealed crankcase at the bottom to a second port in the cylinder wall at the 4 cm mark.

So the mixture's route around the piston has two legs:

1. **In:** from outside, through a hole in the crankcase wall, into the sealed crankcase.
2. **Up and in:** from the crankcase, up the transfer passage, and into the cylinder through the port at its top end.

The same gas makes both hops. Nothing is split, nothing is made on the way - the mixture that enters the crankcase is the mixture that later enters the cylinder.

## The complete map: three ports, three jobs

Here is the full count, which is worth memorizing as a set because the naming has one famous trap in it:

| Port | Where it sits | What flows through it |
|---|---|---|
| **Intake port** | On the **crankcase** wall, below the cylinder | Fresh mixture: outside, into the crankcase |
| **Transfer port** | On the **cylinder** wall, at the 4 cm mark (top end of the transfer passage) | The same fresh mixture: crankcase, up the tunnel, into the cylinder |
| **Exhaust port** | On the **cylinder** wall, at the 4 cm mark | Burnt gas: out of the cylinder |

**The naming trap:** the hole on the cylinder wall is *not* called the intake port. It is the **transfer port**, because its job is transferring stored mixture from the crankcase into the cylinder. The word "intake" belongs to the hole on the crankcase, where the mixture enters the machine from outside. Mixing these two names up is the single most common mistake when first learning two-strokes, and the reason is obvious: both holes carry the same gas, one hop apart.

Note also which holes the piston-operated valve action covers: the transfer port and the exhaust port both sit on the cylinder wall at the 4 cm mark, so they are both shut when the piston is high and both open near the bottom (file 2.2's numbers). The intake port is lower still, on the crankcase, and its opening is tied to the piston's motion in a different way - through pressure, not covering - which the next file works out stroke by stroke.

## Why the storage exists at all

Why not just pipe fresh mixture straight into the cylinder from outside, like the four-stroke's intake does? Because a hole in the wall stands open for a tiny fraction of the stroke, and at that moment there is no suction above the piston to draw anything in - the four-stroke's "piston pulls the mixture in" trick (file 1.3) does not apply here, because the ports open near the *bottom* of the stroke, when the piston is barely moving.

So the design pre-stores the dose below the piston, ready and waiting, and gives it a push of its own. What that push is - and why the piston's own down-stroke provides it - is the last piece, and it is the next file's subject.
