The four-stroke quiz closed Phase 1 with a complete, choreographed engine - and with its one nagging flaw still on the table: only one stroke in four makes power, so the crankshaft turns twice for every single bang. This phase is about the engineers who looked at that accounting and asked: can we get a bang on *every* turn? The answer became the two-stroke engine, and the first thing to get straight about it is the history, because the obvious guess about it is wrong.

# 2.1 The Goal - One Bang Every Rotation

## What the four-stroke leaves on the table

Run the numbers from Phase 1 again. In a four-stroke engine:

- The shaft turns **twice** per cycle.
- One of every four strokes is the power stroke.
- The other three strokes - intake, compression, exhaust - are housekeeping that consumes effort.

So a four-stroke engine delivers **half a power push per turn of its shaft**: one bang every two turns. Three-quarters of the piston's working life is spent not making power. That is the unfairness, and it was visible from the day Otto's engine appeared in 1876.

## The history first, because the guess is backwards

The natural assumption: someone wanted a simpler engine, so they first built a two-stroke and later refined it into the four-stroke. The truth is the opposite. **The four-stroke came first (Otto, 1876), and the two-stroke arrived in the 1880s as a deliberate simplification of it.** The Scottish engineer Dugald Clerk is credited with the first practical two-stroke design, refined further in the years after.

So the two-stroke is not a stepping stone that failed to become the four-stroke. It is a clever hack built *on top of* the four-stroke, attacking its known weakness: too many strokes per bang.

## What exactly was the goal?

This needs one careful correction, because it is easy to misread. The goal was **not** to make the shaft turn more. Shaft rotation is the *result* of the engine working, not the target. More shaft turns per bang would just mean more spinning and less pushing.

The goal was **more power pushes for the same size of engine**. In the four-stroke, the shaft turns twice and delivers one bang; three strokes of housekeeping sit in between. The two-stroke asks: can the housekeeping be squeezed into the cracks, so a bang happens on **every turn** of the shaft?

The prize, if it works: twice the power pushes per turn from the same cylinder size - a punchier engine - in a **lighter and simpler** package, because the whole valve apparatus (camshaft, lobes, springs, timing belt) might be removable.

## The trick, in one sentence

Squeeze the four jobs into two strokes by **overlapping** them: instead of each job waiting for its own piston trip, two jobs happen at once on every trip. The up-stroke does two jobs, the down-stroke does two jobs, and every rotation ends with a bang.

How a piston can do two jobs at once - one above it, one below it - is the engineering heart of this phase, and it depends on a clever reuse of a part you have already met: the sealed space underneath the piston, around the crankshaft.
