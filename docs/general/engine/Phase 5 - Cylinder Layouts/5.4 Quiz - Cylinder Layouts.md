# 5.4 Quiz - Cylinder Layouts
## MCQ

Q: What was the first engine layout in history?
A. A V8, because more power was needed from the start
B. A single cylinder - Otto's 1876 engine and Benz's 1885 Motorwagen both ran one
C. A flat twin, for balance
D. A two-rotor Wankel
> B. The single cylinder came first. Its flaws - roughness, a power ceiling, no backup - pushed engines toward multiple smaller cylinders firing in turns.

---

Q: Why does adding cylinders smooth an engine's running?
A. Each cylinder fires more gently
B. The bangs are staggered around the crankshaft's rotation, so a power push is always happening somewhere - a four-cylinder engine averages two pushes per turn
C. The flywheel is removed
D. More cylinders make the fuel burn slower
> B. Four pistons firing once per two turns, staggered, give two power pushes per shaft turn on average. The dead stretches vanish, the run smooths, and the flywheel has less coasting to do.

---

Q: How do the pistons of one engine end up at different points of their cycles?
A. They are started at different times each morning
B. Each piston's rod attaches to its own crank throw, and the throws point in different directions around the shared shaft
C. Their cylinders have different sizes
D. The camshaft slows some pistons down
> B. Each rod grips its own throw on the shared crankshaft. Throws pointing in different directions - two on opposite sides - mean two pistons descend while two ascend, so the cylinders occupy different points of the four-stroke cycle.

---

Q: In a V8, what does the 8 count?
A. The number of crankshaft throws only
B. The total number of cylinders - four per row in two tilted rows
C. The number of camshafts
D. The number of valves per cylinder
> B. The number is always the total cylinder count; the letter describes the arrangement. A V8 is four cylinders per side sharing one crankshaft at the bottom of the V.

---

Q: Which layout puts a single camshaft low in the middle of the V, reaching the valves through long pushrods?
A. DOHC
B. SOHC
C. Pushrod
D. Inline
> C. The pushrod engine's single camshaft sits in the valley of the V and works all valves through long pushrods - compact and cheap, which kept it in some American V8s, but the long rods carry more moving mass and it faded from modern designs.

---

Q: Why does the flat (boxer) engine handle well?
A. It is lighter than all other layouts
B. Its two rows lie flat, so heavy parts sit low, lowering the car's centre of gravity
C. Its pistons are bigger
D. It always has eight cylinders
> B. The opened-to-180-degrees layout is wide but very low; heavy engine parts close to the ground lower the centre of gravity, so the car corners flatter. The cost is width and awkward servicing.

---

Q: What performs the four jobs in a rotary engine?
A. Three small pistons sharing one cylinder
B. The three sealed pockets between a wobbling rounded-triangle rotor and the fixed pinched-oval housing, each cycling through intake, compression, power and exhaust in parallel
C. A spinning fan driven by the exhaust
D. Two flat pistons moving in mirrored motion
> B. The rotor's three tips seal against the housing wall, forming three pockets at all times. As the rotor follows its wobbling path on the eccentric shaft, each pocket grows, shrinks, burns and empties in turn - the same four jobs as a piston engine, with no reciprocating piston.

---

## TrueFalse

Q: In a multi-cylinder engine, one cylinder's power stroke hands its energy directly to the next cylinder in line.
- True
- False
> False. The cylinders are not connected to each other - all connect to the same crankshaft. A bang turns the shaft, and the shaft carries every piston through its current job. The sharing happens through the shaft.

---

Q: The word "overhead" in DOHC means the camshafts sit above the cylinders, at the valves.
- True
- False
> True. Overhead refers to camshaft position - above, at the valves - as opposed to the pushrod engine, whose single camshaft sits below in the valley of the V.

---

Q: The flat engine's middle shaft is the camshaft.
- True
- False
> False. The crankshaft sits in the middle between the two rows; the camshafts sit at the outer ends of each row, near the valves. The crankshaft is where the connecting rods meet.

---

Q: A rotary engine's internal small gears are the power path from rotor to output shaft.
- True
- False
> False. The fixed gear on the housing and the toothed ring on the rotor only guide the rotor's wobbling path so its tips stay sealed against the wall. The power path is the rotor riding on the eccentric shaft's offset lobe.

## ShortAnswer

Q: Write the four piston-engine layouts with one line each on their arrangement and their signature trade.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Inline - all cylinders in one straight row beside one crankshaft; simple and narrow, but long at high cylinder counts, so most common at four and six. V - two rows tilted apart sharing one crankshaft at the bottom; compact length for many cylinders, at the cost of duplicated heads and camshafts. Flat/boxer - the V opened to 180 degrees, rows pointing outward from a central crankshaft; very low for handling and smooth by mirrored piston motion, but wide and awkward to service. Rotary/Wankel - a rounded-triangle rotor wobbling in a pinched-oval housing, no pistons; tiny, smooth and rev-happy, but thirsty, prone to tip-seal wear, and weak on emissions.

</details>

---

Q: Explain why an inline-six is smoother than an inline-four, using the shared-crankshaft idea from file 5.1.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** Smoothness comes from how often a power push arrives on the shared crankshaft. A four-cylinder engine has four bangs staggered across two turns - about two pushes per turn. An inline-six has six bangs across two turns - three pushes per turn - with throws arranged so the pushes overlap into a near-continuous supply. More cylinders firing in tighter turns means shorter dead stretches, which is why the inline-six is a famously smooth layout, and why more cylinders also lets the flywheel be lighter.

</details>
