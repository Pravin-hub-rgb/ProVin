# 5.1 Why One Cylinder Was Not Enough

## The first engines were single

History starts where the four-stroke was born: **Nikolaus Otto's 1876 engine was a single-cylinder machine**, and so was the first car - **Karl Benz's 1885 Motorwagen ran on one cylinder**. One cylinder, one piston, one bang every two turns of the crankshaft. Everything learned in Phases 0 through 4 was already present in that single-bang arrangement.

And for a while, that was fine. A stationary engine can be heavy and rough; nobody has to sit beside it. But the moment engines went into vehicles - where people ride and weight matters - the single cylinder's flaws became the design's defining problem.

## The three flaws of one cylinder

**Flaw one: roughness.** One bang every two turns means long dead stretches: push, coast, coast, push. The engine lurches through its own cycle. The only fix available to a single-cylinder design is a bigger, heavier flywheel to coast the dead stretches more smoothly - but a heavy flywheel means slow response and more rotating weight to carry.

**Flaw two: the power ceiling.** To make more power from one cylinder, you make the cylinder bigger: wider bore, longer stroke, more mixture per bang. But a bigger cylinder needs a heavier piston, a stronger connecting rod, and a sturdier crankshaft - and the heavier piston *slams harder* at every direction change, which worsens flaw one. Power and smoothness fight each other inside one cylinder, and the single-cylinder design loses both ways as it grows.

**Flaw three: no backup.** One cylinder means one ignition sequence. If the mixture fails to light on a given cycle, that entire two-turn stretch produces nothing and the engine stumbles. There is no partner covering the miss.

## The fix: split the work

The answer was not a better single cylinder - it was **several smaller cylinders sharing the same power**. Instead of one big piston making one big bang, use four smaller pistons, each making a smaller bang, with the bangs **staggered around the crankshaft's rotation** so that a power push is always happening somewhere.

Check the accounting: a four-cylinder four-stroke engine has four pistons each firing once per two turns. Four bangs spread across two turns means, on average, **two power pushes per turn of the shaft** - the same push frequency that the double-acting steam engine of Phase 0 achieved by pushing both directions. The dead stretches vanish, the running becomes smooth, the flywheel has far less coasting to do and can be lighter, and total power scales by *adding cylinders* instead of inflating one.

The consequences line up with every flaw: roughness cured by staggered bangs; the power ceiling removed (need more power? add a cylinder, keep the pistons small); reliability improved (a miss in one cylinder is covered by the others). By the late 1800s and early 1900s, twins, fours and eventually V8s had appeared, and the multi-cylinder engine became the shape of the automobile.

## How they share one crankshaft

The mechanism is a direct extension of file 0.3. Every piston has its own connecting rod, and each rod attaches to **its own crank throw on the shared crankshaft**. The crucial detail is the throws' *directions*: they point in different directions around the shaft, so the pistons are never all in the same part of their cycle at once.

Watch a four-cylinder engine and you will see it plainly: two pistons descend while the other two ascend, because their throws sit on opposite sides of the shaft. The four cylinders are at four different points of the four-stroke cycle at any moment, and their bangs are scheduled in turns - cylinder one fires, then a different cylinder a quarter-turn later, and so on.

One correction worth making explicit, because it is a natural misreading: **one cylinder's bang does not hand its energy directly to the neighbouring cylinder.** The cylinders are not connected to each other at all. They are all connected to the same crankshaft - a bang turns the shaft, and the turning shaft carries *every* piston through whatever job it is currently in, exactly as file 1.4 described for the flywheel. The sharing happens through the shaft; the scheduling (which throw points which way, which cylinder fires when) is what the engine designer arranges.

## What this file is not

This file says the cylinders are arranged "in turns" and around a shared shaft, but not yet *where physically to put them* - all in a row, in two rows tilted, flat, or not in cylinders at all. Those layout choices, and the names every car conversation uses for them (inline, V8, boxer), are the next two files.

It also does not cover the cylinder-count arithmetic on a spec sheet - why "inline-four" and "V6" describe the same idea differently - which is Phase 7's closing material.
