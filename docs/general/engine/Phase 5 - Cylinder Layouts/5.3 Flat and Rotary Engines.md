The inline and V layouts both keep the pistons pointing upward. This file covers the two designs that broke that habit: one that opens the V all the way flat, and one that abandons up-and-down pistons completely - the rounded triangle that still runs the same four jobs from Phase 1.

# 5.3 Flat and Rotary Engines

## The flat engine: the V opened to 180 degrees

Take the V engine from file 5.2 and keep opening the angle between its two rows until the rows lie **flat** - 180 degrees apart, pointing away from each other horizontally. That is the **flat engine**, and the result is distinctive in every dimension:

- The **crankshaft sits in the middle**, between the two rows, with cylinders pointing out to the left and out to the right. (A common mix-up worth killing: the middle shaft is the *crank*shaft, not the camshaft - the camshafts sit at the outer ends of each row, up near the valves, exactly as the V engine's heads do.)
- The pistons move **in and out sideways**, horizontally.
- Pistons on opposite sides move in **mirrored motion**: as one moves toward the shaft, the one across from it moves away at the same instant.

That mirroring is the flat engine's mechanical signature, and it is also the origin of its nickname, the **boxer**: the two piston rows move toward and away from each other in matching pairs. Because each piston's motion is cancelled by its partner's, their shakes cancel too, and the engine runs notably smoothly.

The packaging trade is unusual: the engine is **wide but very low**. Low matters for handling - heavy parts sitting close to the ground lower the car's centre of gravity, so the car corners flatter. The width is the price: the layout is harder to fit around wheels and suspension, and routine parts (the spark plugs, one per row's outer face) sit in awkward spots. **Porsche and Subaru** are the names most associated with keeping this layout alive.

## The rotary: no cylinders, no pistons

Now leave pistons entirely. The **rotary engine** - also called the **Wankel**, after its inventor - has no cylinder, no piston, no up-and-down motion anywhere. Its four jobs from Phase 1 run inside a completely different shape:

- The outer body is a fixed **housing**, shaped like a slightly pinched oval.
- Inside it spins a **rounded triangle called the rotor**, whose three curved tips stay in contact with the housing wall at all times.
- Because the tips seal against the wall, the spaces between each rotor face and the wall form **three sealed pockets** at any moment.

The rotor does not spin in a plain circle; it follows a **wobbling path** around the centre, held on its route by an **eccentric shaft** - the rotary's version of the crankshaft, a shaft with an offset lobe that the rotor rides on. (The small gears visible inside a rotary diagram - a fixed gear on the housing and a toothed ring on the rotor - are only the guides that keep the wobble on track; they are not the power path.)

Now read the four jobs in rotary terms. As the rotor wobbles, each pocket in turn **grows** (drawing in mixture - intake), **shrinks** (squeezing it - compression), receives its spark and **drives the rotor around** (power), and **opens to the exhaust port** (exhaust). With three pockets always present, all four jobs run **in parallel** around the rotor - one pocket is always breathing while another burns. Same physics, same four jobs, no reciprocating piston anywhere.

## Why anyone wanted this

The motivation is visible in the piston's own motion: a piston **stops and reverses direction twice per stroke**, every stroke, forever - and stopping and restarting heavy metal wastes energy and generates vibration. Wankel wanted an engine with **continuous rotation** instead. The German engineer worked on the idea through the 1920s and 1930s, and the first working prototype was built in **1957** with the engineering company NSU. **Mazda** became the design's most famous manufacturer, most of all with the two-rotor **RX-7** and **RX-8**; it also built three- and four-rotor engines for racing. Multiple rotors simply stack along one eccentric shaft, spaced at offset angles so their power pushes spread out.

Rotaries are rare today because their costs caught up with them: they **burn more fuel** than piston engines of similar output, their **tip seals wear out** (the three rotor tips do hard sealing duty around the whole housing), and they **struggled with emissions rules**. What kept them loved is what made them special: compact, smooth, and - in the slang you will hear - **rev-happy**, meaning the engine climbs to high shaft speeds eagerly. "Revs" is short for revolutions: how many times the shaft turns per minute, the number a car's tachometer displays; it gets its own full file in Phase 7 (file 7.3). A rotary is naturally suited to spinning fast because nothing in it ever has to stop and reverse - unlike a piston, which reverses twice per stroke.

## The four layouts, side by side

| Layout | Arrangement | Signature trade |
|---|---|---|
| Inline | One straight row | Simple and narrow; long at high cylinder counts |
| V | Two rows tilted, one crankshaft | Compact length; duplicated heads and cams |
| Flat (boxer) | Two rows opened to 180 degrees | Low and smooth; wide, awkward packaging |
| Rotary (Wankel) | Rounded triangle in an oval housing, no pistons | Tiny, smooth, rev-happy; thirsty, seal wear, emissions |
