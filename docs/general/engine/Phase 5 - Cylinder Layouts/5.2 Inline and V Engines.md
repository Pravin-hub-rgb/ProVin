The last file settled that engines split their power across several cylinders sharing one crankshaft. That immediately creates a packing problem with real consequences: *where do the cylinders physically sit* around that shaft? The answer decides the engine's length, height, width, and half the vocabulary of car conversation. This file covers the two most common answers - one straight row, and two tilted rows meeting in a V.

# 5.2 Inline and V Engines

## The inline engine: one straight row

The **inline engine** lines all its cylinders up in a single row, side by side, with the crankshaft running along underneath them. An **inline-four** has four pistons in one straight line, each with its own connecting rod reaching down to its own crank throw on the shared shaft - and, as file 5.1 established, those throws point in different directions so the pistons sit at different points of their cycles.

The inline layout is the natural default: it is the shortest engineering distance between "one cylinder" and "several cylinders", the parts are all in one line, and the engine stays narrow. Its cost is length: eight cylinders in a row makes a very long engine, which is why inline designs are most common at four and six cylinders. The **inline-six** is a famously smooth-running layout, and engine nicknames you will meet - **2JZ, the Toyota inline-six beloved by tuners** - are inline engines by birth.

Above the row sit the camshafts, in the arrangements file 1.5 introduced: many modern engines run **two camshafts along the top** - one operating the intake valves, one the exhaust valves.

## The V engine: two rows, one shaft

The **V engine** solves the length problem by folding. It splits the cylinders into **two rows tilted apart**, meeting at the bottom like the letter V, with **one shared crankshaft at the bottom of the V**. Each row's connecting rods reach down to throws on the same shaft as the other row's.

The name encodes the count: a **V8** has four cylinders per side (four plus four); a **V6** has three per side. The number is always the *total* - eight cylinders, six cylinders - and the letter describes their arrangement. The gain over inline is compactness: eight cylinders fold into roughly the length of an inline-four, which is why V layouts dominate wherever many cylinders must fit under a car's hood.

The cost is complexity: two cylinder heads (the tops of the two rows) means duplicated camshafts, duplicated valve gear, and a wider, denser packaging job.

## The camshaft count: DOHC, SOHC and pushrod

Now the vocabulary that confuses every spec-sheet reader, settled by counting camshafts (the valve pushers from file 1.5):

| Layout | Camshafts | Where they sit |
|---|---|---|
| **DOHC** - double overhead cam | Four in a V (two per side), two in an inline | On top of each cylinder head - one camshaft for intake valves, one for exhaust |
| **SOHC** - single overhead cam | Two in a V (one per side), one in an inline | One camshaft per row, with lobes for *both* the intake and exhaust valves of its side |
| **Pushrod** | One total | In the **middle of the V**, low down, operating all valves through long pushrods reaching up |

Read the table as an evolution of one trade-off. DOHC puts the camshaft closest to each valve set - precise control, the modern default. SOHC halves the camshaft count by giving one shaft both jobs per row. The **pushrod** design is the oldest answer: one single camshaft sitting in the valley of the V, pushing long thin rods upward to work valves it cannot reach directly. It keeps the engine compact and cheap, which is why it survived for decades in some American V8s - but the long pushrods are the reason it faded: more moving mass between the camshaft and the valve.

The word **overhead** simply means the camshaft sits above the cylinders, at the valves - as opposed to the pushrod engine, where it sits below, in the valley.

## What this file is not

This file does not cover the two remaining piston layouts - the flattened V whose cylinders lie sideways, and the machine with no pistons at all. Both are the next file. It also does not rank the layouts by quality; each exists because it wins a particular packaging or manufacturing trade, and "better" depends entirely on which constraint matters for the car.
