The last file closed the steam engine into a self-running loop, but the loop still has a lopsided habit: steam only ever pushes from one side, and the return trip is borrowed motion. Steam is cheap and pressure is pressure, so engineers asked the obvious upgrade question: why not let steam shove the piston in *both* directions? The answer - the double-acting engine - is the last piece of Phase 0, and it hands you the measuring stick you will use to compare every engine type from now on.

# 0.5 Double-Acting Engines - Power on Every Stroke

## The single-acting engine, restated

Everything built so far works on one side of the piston only. Steam enters the sealed pocket at the closed end, shoves the piston away, and leaves through the exit. The piston's return trip is powered indirectly - by the rotation the push created, carried through the shaft.

This design is called **single-acting**: the steam acts on one face of the piston. It works. But every second movement of the piston is unpowered, and the pressure sitting in the boiler is only being used half the time.

## The double-acting idea

Now look at the piston differently. It has **two faces** - one toward the closed end, one toward the open end - and each face seals its own space. Both of those spaces can be fed steam and drained. Do that, and the piston can be pushed from either side:

- **Push down:** steam enters the space above the piston (at the closed end), shoving it down, while the space *below* the piston is opened to exhaust, so the used steam there escapes as the piston descends.
- **Push up:** the valves flip. Steam now enters the space below the piston, shoving it up, while the space above opens to exhaust, draining the used steam from the first push.

Each side of the piston takes turns being the steam side and the exhaust side. Four openings in total - two steam inlets, two exhaust outlets - and a valve system that flips between them in step with the piston.

## Every stroke is a power stroke

A **stroke**, remember, is one single-direction trip of the piston - down is a stroke, up is a stroke. In the single-acting engine, only the down stroke received a real push. In the double-acting engine, *both* strokes receive one: steam shoves the piston down, then steam shoves it back up. Every stroke is a power stroke.

This also corrects an earlier simplification from file 0.2, and it is worth being explicit about: in a double-acting engine, the flywheel is not needed to *drag* the piston back - steam does the pushing in both directions. The flywheel is still there and still useful, because its weight keeps the rotation smooth between pushes, but the return trip is powered directly.

## Not a "one-stroke" engine

A natural trap at this point: if every stroke powers, is the steam engine a "one-stroke engine"? No. The piston still takes two strokes - one down, one up - to complete one full turn of the shaft, exactly as the crank geometry of file 0.3 dictates. The difference is that both of those strokes deliver a push.

## The measuring stick: power pushes per turn of the shaft

Here is the number that lets you compare engine types fairly. Count how many real power pushes the engine delivers per full turn of its shaft:

| Engine type | Power pushes per full shaft turn |
|---|---|
| Double-acting steam engine | 2 - one per stroke, both strokes push |
| Two-stroke petrol engine | 1 - one bang per turn |
| Four-stroke petrol engine | 0.5 - one bang every two turns |

Two of the rows in that table are engines you have not met yet. The **four-stroke petrol engine** is the subject of all of Phase 1, and the **two-stroke petrol engine** is Phase 2 - both will be explored in detail there, so take the table on faith for now and watch it get earned. What you can already read from it is the punchline of Phase 0: the double-acting steam engine, the oldest machine in the story, delivers more pushes per turn than either of its combustion descendants. What steam could never fix was something else entirely - its weight - and that is exactly where the next phase begins.

## What this file is not

This is not the end of the steam engine's story in real history - steam engines kept evolving for over a century, in locomotives, ships and factories. This course stops here because Phase 0 has already extracted everything a car reader needs: pressure, cylinder, piston, crankshaft, self-timing valves, and the pushes-per-turn measuring stick.

And to keep the wall from file 0.1 intact: nothing in this file mentions fuel burning inside the cylinder. Steam still arrives from outside, made in a boiler by burning coal somewhere else entirely. The next phase tears that arrangement down.
