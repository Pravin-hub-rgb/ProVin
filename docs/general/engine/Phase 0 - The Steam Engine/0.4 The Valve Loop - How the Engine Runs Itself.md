The machine now has a push and a rotation, and it runs - with one silent assumption hiding in the middle. Every cycle, the valve must open the steam path at exactly the right moment, let the piston be shoved, then switch to the exit path so the spent steam can escape. Miss the timing and the engine stalls: steam arriving at the wrong time just fights the piston. So the real question is the one the last files left open: **who decides when each opening happens?**

# 0.4 The Valve Loop - How the Engine Runs Itself

## The naive answer, and why it fails

Someone could stand next to the engine and flip the valve by hand - steam in, wait for the stroke, steam out, wait, repeat. For a demonstration engine, that is exactly what happened historically.

But think about what the job demands. The valve has to flip at precise moments, thousands of times per hour, perfectly locked to the piston's position, without getting tired or drifting. A human cannot do it. And an engine that needs an operator standing at its valve is not really an engine - it is a machine with a person attached.

## The elegant answer: the shaft already knows the timing

Look at what the machine has that is perfectly in step with the piston: the **crankshaft**. It spins exactly once per push-and-return, because the piston is rigidly connected to it through the rod and throw. The crankshaft's position *is* the piston's position, expressed as rotation.

So the design lets the crankshaft do the timing job. Attached to the same turning shaft is a second small rod arrangement - driven by the shaft's rotation - which slides the valve back and forth. As the shaft rotates, the valve moves in step with it: one position of the shaft opens the steam-in path, a later position opens the steam-out path, and the switch happens exactly when the piston's position calls for it.

No human. No computer. No separate timer. **The rotation timing itself does the work.**

## The loop, stated in one breath

Now the whole steam engine closes into a self-sustaining circle:

1. Steam pushes the **piston**.
2. The piston turns the **crankshaft** (through the rod and throw).
3. The crankshaft moves the **valve** (through its driven rod).
4. The valve admits the next dose of **steam**.

The engine runs its own valves. The push creates the rotation, and the rotation creates the conditions for the next push. That is what makes this a genuine engine rather than a one-shot device - a loop that keeps itself going.

Pause on why this is clever. The engine contains no part whose job is "thinking about timing". The timing is not stored in a brain; it is stored in the geometry of the machine - in how the valve's driver is fixed to the shaft. The engine is its own clock, and its clock hands are physically bolted to its own crankshaft.

## Keep this idea in your pocket

File 0.2 asked for stop-and-continue. This file answered it with a loop: push makes rotation, rotation makes the next push possible. Burn that pattern into memory, because it is about to return in a much more famous form.

A modern petrol engine faces the same problem with more parts: four different valve events must each happen at a precise moment in a repeating cycle. The solution there is the same idea at larger scale - a shaft spinning in exact step with the crankshaft, whose shape opens the right valve at the right moment. That shaft is the **camshaft**, and it gets its full file in Phase 1 (file 1.5). When you read it, you will recognize the steam engine's trick wearing new clothes.

## What this file is not

This file does not yet say how the valve's driver is shaped or geared - the steam engine's internal linkage stays at the level of "the shaft drives a rod that slides the valve", because the details differ between designs and add nothing to the concept.

It also does not cover the upgrade that many real steam engines used: instead of one steam path and one exit path, they used four openings and pushed the piston in both directions. That is the last piece of Phase 0, and it is the next file's subject.
