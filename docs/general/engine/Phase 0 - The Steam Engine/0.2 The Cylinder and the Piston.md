The last file ended with a sealed boiler full of pressurized steam - a tank of stored push, pressing hard on every wall, with nowhere to go. Now the push finally gets a job. This file adds the two parts that appear in every engine from here to the end of the course: the cylinder and the piston.

# 0.2 The Cylinder and the Piston

## The two parts, plainly defined

A **cylinder** is a straight tube with one end closed. A **piston** is a plug that slides inside that tube, fitting so snugly that no gas can leak past it. Put together: the piston divides the cylinder into two spaces - a sealed pocket between the closed end and the piston, and everything on the other side of the piston.

Picture it physically: a tube lying on its side, closed at one end, with a tight plug inside it. Behind the plug (toward the closed end) is the sealed pocket. The pocket is where the steam will go.

Two facts about this arrangement drive everything that follows:

1. The closed end and the tube walls are rigid. They cannot move.
2. The piston is loose - it can only slide along the tube, because a plug cannot move sideways inside a tube that grips it.

## Step 1: connect the boiler

A **pipe** runs from the boiler to the cylinder, entering near the closed end - so it feeds the sealed pocket directly. On that pipe sits a **valve**, which is a tap: open it, and steam flows; close it, and steam stops.

The boiler has been boiling away the whole time, so its steam is under high pressure, waiting. Open the valve and high-pressure steam rushes down the pipe into the sealed pocket behind the piston.

## Step 2: why the piston moves and nothing else does

Now the pocket is full of steam, and that steam is pressing outward on every surface it touches - the closed end, the tube walls, and the back face of the piston.

Go back to the two facts. The closed end and walls are rigid: they take the push and nothing happens. The piston is the only part that is free to move, and it is free in exactly one direction: away from the closed end, along the tube.

So the piston slides. The steam's push, which was useless against a static tank wall, is now useful, because it is pushing the one wall in the whole machine that can move.

**This is the single most important sentence in Phase 0, so it gets its own line: the gas pushes the piston. The piston does not push the gas.**

It sounds obvious, but it is the most common early mistake, so it is worth killing now. The piston does not "compress the steam to make power" - the power comes first, from the expanding steam, and the piston merely receives the push. The piston is the machine's hands, not its muscles.

## Step 3: the one-way problem

Watch what happens if we stop here. Steam came in, the piston slid to its new position, and... nothing more can happen. The pocket is still full of steam, the piston is shoved against its limit, and the machine has moved exactly once.

You have spotted the real problem if you are already asking: where is the "stop and continue"? One dose of steam produces one push. An engine needs push, return, push, return, thousands of times.

## Step 4: the valve flips

The valve is the answer, and it does two jobs in turns:

- **Steam in:** with the piston near the closed end, the valve opens the boiler pipe. Fresh steam floods the pocket and shoves the piston away.
- **Steam out:** once the piston has traveled, the valve switches - it closes the boiler pipe and opens an **exit path** instead. The spent steam, which has now expanded and lost its pressure, escapes. Pressure in the pocket drops to nothing.

With the pressure gone, the pocket is empty, the piston is at the far end, and the machine is reset for the next dose.

## Step 5: the return trip

One piece is still missing. Something must push the piston back toward the closed end before the next dose of steam can shove it away again. In the earliest engines that "something" was the **momentum of a heavy spinning wheel**, called the **flywheel** (a heavy disc bolted onto the engine's rotating shaft - its full story is in Phase 4, file 4.1). A heavy thing that is spinning resists stopping, so between steam pushes, the flywheel's momentum carries the mechanism around and drags the piston back into position.

Then the valve flips again: steam in, push, steam out, return. Push, return, push, return. The cycle now runs by itself.

## What this file is not

It is not the whole steam engine yet. Two big pieces are deliberately missing. First, the piston's straight back-and-forth slide has not yet been converted into the rotation that a wheel needs - that conversion is the next file's entire subject. Second, the valve in this file is still being described as if someone flips it by hand; the fact that the engine times its own valve is the file after that.

Also note what was *not* needed here: no spark, no fuel burning in the cylinder, no knowledge of petrol. Every steam engine part so far works identically for any gas under pressure.

## In Your Own Words

1. Why does the steam push the piston instead of the cylinder walls?

**Sample Answer:** The steam presses on every surface it touches, but the closed end and the tube walls are rigid - their push does nothing. The piston is the only free-moving part, and it can only slide along the tube away from the closed end, so the push moves it.

</details>

2. Correct this statement and explain why: "The piston compresses the steam to generate the engine's power."

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** It is backwards. The power comes from the expanding, pressurized steam, which pushes the piston. The piston receives the push and passes it on; it is not the source of the power and does not squeeze the steam to create it.

</details>

3. What two switches does the valve perform, and in what order?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** First it opens the boiler pipe so fresh steam enters and shoves the piston. Then, after the piston has traveled, it closes the boiler pipe and opens the exit so the spent steam escapes and pressure drops, resetting the pocket for the next dose.

</details>

4. Why does a steam engine need a heavy spinning wheel between pushes?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** Because the steam only pushes in one direction and only during part of the cycle. Something must return the piston to its starting position, and in early engines that was the momentum of the flywheel - a heavy spinning disc that resists stopping and carries the mechanism around between pushes.

</details>
