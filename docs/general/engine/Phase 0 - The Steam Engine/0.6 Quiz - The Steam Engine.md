# 0.6 Quiz - The Steam Engine
## MCQ

Q: Where does the push that moves a steam engine's piston physically come from?
A. The piston compressing the steam until it releases energy
B. Expanding steam under pressure pressing on the only movable surface
C. The flywheel shoving the piston from below
D. The connecting rod pulling the piston toward the crankshaft
> B. Steam occupies about 1,600 times more space than the water it came from, and sealed in the cylinder pocket it presses on every surface. The piston is the only part free to move, so it receives the push. The piston never compresses steam to make power.

---

Q: Why is a push aimed at the exact center of a shaft useless for producing rotation?
A. It is too weak to move a metal shaft
B. It produces too much friction at the center
C. It contains no twist - it only tries to slide or crush the shaft lengthwise
D. The shaft must spin in the opposite direction for a central push to work
> C. Rotation requires a force acting off-center, roughly across the shaft. This is why the connecting rod attaches to a crank throw, an offset point held away from the center by the crank arm.

---

Q: What keeps the crank throw traveling in a circle instead of moving straight down when pushed?
A. The cylinder walls guide it sideways
B. Its distance from the shaft's center is fixed, and a point at constant distance from a center can only move along a circle
C. The steam pressure reverses direction halfway
D. The connecting rod stretches and recoils
> B. The throw is rigid metal at a fixed distance from the center, so a straight push forces it to swing around the center, spinning the shaft.

---

Q: What controls when the steam valve opens and closes in a self-running steam engine?
A. A spring that fires at fixed time intervals
B. The operator watching the piston and flipping the valve
C. The same rotating crankshaft, through a driven rod that slides the valve in step
D. A separate small steam engine dedicated to the valve
> C. The crankshaft's position is the piston's position expressed as rotation, so driving the valve from the shaft locks the timing mechanically. The engine is its own clock.

---

Q: In a double-acting steam engine, what happens during the stroke where steam enters below the piston?
A. Nothing - that side is reserved for exhaust only
B. The steam pushes the piston up while the space above drains its used steam through exhaust
C. The steam is compressed to prepare the next stroke
D. The piston is held still while pressure builds
> B. Both faces of the piston get fed in turns: steam entering below shoves the piston up while the upper space opens to exhaust and drains the previous push's steam. Every stroke is a power stroke.

---

Q: Which engine type delivers the most power pushes per full turn of the shaft?
A. Four-stroke petrol engine
B. Two-stroke petrol engine
C. Double-acting steam engine
D. They are all equal in pushes per turn
> C. Double-acting steam delivers 2 pushes per turn (one per stroke), the two-stroke petrol 1, and the four-stroke petrol 0.5 - one push every two turns.

## TrueFalse

Q: A steam engine with every stroke powered still takes two strokes to complete one full shaft turn.
- True
- False
> True. The crank geometry does not change: one down stroke plus one up stroke equals one full turn. "Double-acting" means both strokes push, not that the cycle is shorter - so it is not a one-stroke engine.

---

Q: In a single-acting steam engine, the flywheel directly supplies the force of the power stroke.
- True
- False
> False. The steam supplies the power stroke's force. The flywheel's momentum carries the piston back between pushes; in double-acting engines even that job is taken over by steam pushing from the other side.

---

Q: The valve timing in a self-running steam engine is stored in the geometry of the machine rather than in any brain-like part.
- True
- False
> True. The valve's driver is fixed to the rotating shaft, so opening happens at the right moment purely because of how the parts are linked. No human, no computer, just rotation timing.

---

Q: Steam engines and petrol engines are treated as one combined topic in this course's Phase 0.
- True
- False
> False. Phase 0 teaches the steam engine completely on its own, with a deliberate wall before petrol. Burning fuel inside the cylinder only begins in Phase 1.

## ShortAnswer

Q: Walk through one full single-acting steam cycle from coal to rotation, naming every part involved.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Coal burns under the boiler, heating water into steam that builds pressure in the sealed tank. Opening the valve sends steam down the pipe into the sealed pocket behind the piston in the cylinder. The steam presses on every surface, but only the piston can move, so it slides away. The connecting rod transmits the push to the crank throw, which - held off-center by the crank arm - swings around the shaft's center and spins the crankshaft. The valve then switches to the exit, spent steam escapes, and pressure drops. The flywheel's momentum (carried through the same rigid link) drags the piston back, and the shaft's rotation moves the valve to admit the next dose.

</details>

---

Q: Explain in your own words why "the engine runs its own valves" is the idea that separates an engine from a one-shot device.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** A one-shot device produces a push once and stops, because nothing arranges the next push. The steam engine closes a loop: the steam push turns the crankshaft, and the crankshaft - being perfectly in step with the piston - drives the valve that admits the next push. The rotation creates the conditions for the next push, so the cycle sustains itself with no operator. The same self-timing idea returns in modern engines as the camshaft and timing belt.

</details>
