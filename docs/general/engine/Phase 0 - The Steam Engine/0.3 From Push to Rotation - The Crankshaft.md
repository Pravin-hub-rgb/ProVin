The previous file produced a clean, repeatable straight-line push: steam in, piston slides, steam out, piston returns. The next file in the chain has one job on its mind, and it is the oldest problem in machinery: wheels do not slide, they spin. A push along a straight line and a rotation are different kinds of motion, and nothing connects them automatically. That connection is the crankshaft.

# 0.3 From Push to Rotation - The Crankshaft

## First word: shaft

A **shaft** is a long metal rod that spins. That is the entire definition. In a car, shafts exist to carry rotation from one place to another - from the engine toward the wheels, in the end.

A shaft by itself receives no instructions about how to spin. If you want a push to turn a shaft, the push has to act on the shaft in a way that forces rotation. This is where the design gets clever.

## The failed design first: pushing the center

Suppose you attach the piston's rod directly to the center of the shaft's end, head-on. Now the piston shoves. What happens? Almost nothing useful. A push aimed at the center of a shaft just tries to slide the shaft lengthwise or crush it - it contains no twist. To rotate a shaft, the force has to act somewhere other than the center, and roughly across it.

So the design does the opposite of the obvious: it attaches the rod **off-center**.

## The off-center point: the crank throw

On the spinning shaft, there is a rigid bump that holds one point out to the side, at a fixed distance from the shaft's center. That offset point has names - **crank throw**, or **crank pin** - and the rigid piece holding it away from the center is the **crank arm**. The rod between the piston and this offset point is the **connecting rod** (people also say "piston rod", which means the same thing).

Now trace the push. The piston shoves the connecting rod straight down. The rod pushes the crank throw. And here is the key geometric fact: **the throw cannot move straight down.** Its distance from the shaft's center is fixed and unchangeable - it is rigid metal. The only path a point can take while keeping a constant distance from a center is a circle around that center.

So the throw swings sideways and around, the shaft turns with it, and rotation has appeared. A straight push went in one end of this mechanism and rotation came out the other. That is the entire trick, and it is the same in every piston engine ever built, including the one in a modern car.

## The full loop so far

Follow one cycle with the names now fixed:

1. Steam enters the sealed pocket behind the **piston**.
2. The steam shoves the piston along the **cylinder**.
3. The **connecting rod**, attached to the piston, transmits the push to the **crank throw**.
4. The throw, held off-center by the **crank arm**, swings around the shaft's center.
5. The **crankshaft** spins.

## The link works in both directions

One more fact, and it matters more than it looks. The piston, rod, throw and shaft are all rigidly connected. That mechanical link does not care which end is doing the pushing.

So after the steam push has turned the shaft some way, the shaft's own rotation can push back: the throw comes around and the rod drives the piston back up the cylinder. In the single-acting engine of the previous file, the piston's return was described as being dragged by the flywheel's momentum - and it is the same thing said from the other end. The flywheel spins with the shaft; the shaft carries the throw; the throw carries the rod; the rod carries the piston. One connected loop, working in whichever direction the current push is coming from.

## The vocabulary, in one table

Because these five names will be used for the rest of the course, here they are in order, from the gas to the rotation:

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| Piston | The plug that slides in the cylinder and receives the push |
| Connecting rod | The arm joining the piston to the off-center point |
| Crank throw (crank pin) | The offset point on the shaft that the rod pushes |
| Crank arm | The rigid piece holding the throw away from the center |
| Crankshaft | The whole spinning shaft, throws included |

Say the chain once: **piston, connecting rod, crank throw, crankshaft.** Every car engine discussion you will ever hear assumes you know this chain.

## What this file is not

This is not yet the story of how fast or how far the shaft turns per piston stroke - that counting (one stroke equals half a turn, and why) arrives in Phase 1, file 1.4, where it matters for the four-stroke cycle. Here the only claim is directional: straight push in, rotation out.

It is also not a description of how a real crankshaft looks - real crankshafts have several throws along their length for several cylinders, all on one shaft. That detail belongs to Phase 5, when engines grow multiple cylinders.
