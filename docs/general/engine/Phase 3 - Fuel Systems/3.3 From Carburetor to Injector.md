The injector's mist now makes sense - but injectors are electronic parts, and cars ran fine for most of a century before electronics reached the fuel system. The part that did the mixing instead was the carburetor, and its rise and fall is this file's story: how suction alone can mix fuel and air, what the accelerator pedal actually does, and the four adjustments that decided the carburetor's fate.

# 3.3 From Carburetor to Injector

## What a carburetor does

A **carburetor** is a purely mechanical fuel-mixing device that sits on the engine's intake path - between the air outside and the cylinder. It uses **no electronics, no pump, no moving spray mechanism**. Its working principle is suction:

The engine inhales air through the carburetor (that is what the intake stroke's low pressure does - file 1.3). Inside the carburetor, that moving air passes a deliberately **narrow spot** in its path. Air moving through a narrow spot moves faster, and faster-moving air pulls sideways on whatever is next to it. A small bowl of petrol sits beside that narrow spot, and the fast air **pulls petrol up and out of the bowl**, drawing it into the stream, where it mixes with the air on the way to the cylinder.

So the carburetor is an air-powered pump: the engine's own suction does all the work, pulling fuel from a small reservoir as the air rushes past. The faster the air moves, the more fuel gets pulled. Mixing happens continuously, mechanically, with zero control electronics.

## What the accelerator pedal does - in both systems

This is a good place to settle a question the course has been quietly owing since the pedal was first mentioned: **what does pressing the accelerator actually do?**

In a carburetor engine, the pedal is connected to a **flap** in the air path. Pressing the pedal opens the flap wider, so more air rushes through the narrow spot - and faster-moving air pulls proportionally more petrol out of the bowl. More pedal: more air, more pulled fuel, bigger doses, more power. The pedal works on the carburetor, honestly and physically.

But note the word: *proportionally*, roughly, mechanically. The carburetor responds to the pedal - it just cannot respond to anything else. That limitation is the next section.

(In an injector engine, the pedal is a sensor input: the computer reads the pedal position, decides how much mixture the engine needs, and commands the injectors to spray accordingly. Same driver action, but the decision moved from a flap to a calculation.)

## Why the injector took over

The carburetor's weakness is not that it fails to work - it works, and it powered cars for decades. Its weakness is that it can only react to **air speed**, while the ideal air-fuel mixture depends on **everything at once**:

- **Temperature** - cold air is denser, cold engines want richer mixtures to start.
- **Altitude** - thinner air at height changes the ratio for the same fuel flow.
- **Engine speed** - the right dose differs at idle versus full throttle.
- **Pedal position** - how hard you demand power.

The ideal mix changes with all four, simultaneously, continuously. A carburetor gets it roughly right most of the time - it is a mechanical compromise device - but it cannot fine-tune for all factors at once. An injector system, controlled by a computer reading sensors (air temperature, air pressure, engine speed, throttle position), sprays **exactly the right amount each time**, for every factor, in every condition.

That precision converts directly into the four benefits that ended the carburetor's reign: **better fuel economy** (no chronically over-rich doses), **cleaner exhaust** (complete burning, since mist plus correct dosing means less waste - the same surface-area logic from file 3.2), **easier cold starts** (the computer can deliberately richen the mixture for a cold engine), and **more reliable running** (no mechanical drift as parts wear).

## The verdict of history

Emissions rules did to the carburetor what they did to the two-stroke: they drew a line the old design could not reliably cross. From roughly the **1980s onward**, tightening exhaust standards pushed carburetors out of new cars, and injector-plus-computer became the standard fuel system for petrol engines. Diesels, which always needed precisely timed injection for their self-ignition design, were never carbureted at all.

So the timeline of the fuel system reads: carburetor (suction mixing, mechanical) → injector (pressurized mist, computer-dosed), with emissions law holding the door open for the winner.
