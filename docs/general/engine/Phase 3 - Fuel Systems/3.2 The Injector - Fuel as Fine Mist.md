# 3.2 The Injector - Fuel as Fine Mist

## First, correct a likely assumption

The last file may have left the impression that injectors belong to diesels only. They do not. **Modern petrol engines have injectors too.** What differs is not ownership but *where and when* each type sprays. Older petrol engines did their mixing with a different part - the carburetor - and that handover story is file 3.3. This file establishes what an injector is and why every modern engine wants one.

## What an injector does

An **injector** is a nozzle that sprays fuel under pressure as a **fine mist** - the fuel broken into thousands of tiny droplets instead of a stream or a puddle. That is its entire physical action: pressurized fuel, small hole, spray.

## Why mist and not liquid

Here is the part worth understanding rather than memorizing. **Liquid fuel does not burn well in lumps.**

Burning happens at the surface where fuel meets air. A puddle of petrol has a small surface compared with its volume: the fire eats inward from the outside, slowly, and much of the fuel never finds oxygen at the right moment before the pressure cycle moves on. The result is incomplete burning - wasted fuel, soot, unburnt hydrocarbons out the exhaust.

Break the same amount of fuel into a fine mist and the arithmetic flips. Thousands of droplets have enormously more combined surface area than one lump, so every droplet is surrounded by air and burning can reach essentially all of the fuel at once. A fine mist mixes evenly with air and burns fast and completely - which is exactly what the engine wants, since the four-stroke cycle gives the burn only a moment before the piston must be shoved.

So the injector is not about elegance; it is about **surface area**. Mist turns the fuel from something that must be slowly eaten into something that can burn in one instant.

## Where each engine's injector sits in the cycle

Now place it, and the placement is exactly the timing story from file 3.1:

**In a petrol engine**, the injector sprays petrol - just the fuel, as mist - during the intake phase, into the incoming air. Air arrives separately through the intake valve; the spray meets it and the two mix **before compression**. The mixture is then squeezed all cycle long and finally lit by the spark plug at the top. The injector's job here is dosing: spraying the right amount of fuel into the right amount of air, early.

**In a diesel engine**, the injector sprays diesel **directly into the cylinder at the very end of compression**, into air that is already hot and squeezed to ignition point. The droplets meet the hot air and ignite instantly, droplet by droplet, as they arrive. The injector's job here is *timing*: each spray is a command to burn now.

One part, two completely different roles, decided entirely by the fuel personalities from file 3.1 - petrol that waits, diesel that ignites on contact.

## The whole fuel story in one paragraph

Both modern engine types have injectors that spray fuel as fine mist, because mist is what allows fuel to burn completely in the instant a cylinder cycle allows. A petrol engine injects early, mixes before compression, and lets the **spark plug** choose the burn moment. A diesel engine injects at the last moment, into self-igniting air, and the **injector's timing** chooses the burn moment. Same hardware, opposite philosophies - and the fuel's personality is what selects the philosophy.
