The two-stroke quiz closed Phase 2 with two breathing designs, both burning petrol lit by a spark plug. Every engine so far in this course has obeyed one rule: someone must decide the exact instant the fuel burns, and that someone has been the spark. This phase begins by breaking that rule - the diesel engine has no spark plug at all - and working out who decides the timing instead.

# 3.1 Petrol vs Diesel - Who Decides the Timing

## The petrol recipe, restated in one breath

From Phase 1: the intake stroke draws in air mixed with petrol mist; the compression stroke squeezes that mixture into a small space; the spark plug fires at the top; the mixture burns and shoves the piston down. Two ingredients, one igniter: fuel plus air, and a spark that chooses the instant.

## The diesel recipe: no spark, hotter air

A diesel engine removes the spark plug entirely. Its cycle runs like this:

- The intake stroke pulls in **only air** - no fuel at all.
- The compression stroke squeezes that air **much harder** than a petrol engine squeezes its mixture.
- Hard squeezing makes the air extremely hot.
- At the end of compression, fuel is injected into that hot air, and it ignites **on its own** - no spark needed.

Why does squeezing make air hot? It is a physical fact about gases: force the same molecules into a smaller space and their average speed rises - the gas's temperature is that average molecular speed, so compressing a gas heats it. A petrol engine relies on this only mildly; the diesel engine is *built around* it, compressing its air so hard that the temperature crosses the point where fuel ignites spontaneously on contact.

## The fuels have different personalities

The two designs exist because the two fuels behave oppositely when heated and squeezed, and this is the heart of the whole comparison:

- **Petrol resists lighting itself.** You can heat it, squeeze it, mix it with air, and nothing happens - until a spark fires at the chosen instant. That willingness to wait is what makes the petrol engine's design possible: the fuel can be mixed in early, carried through compression, and ignited exactly when the spark decides.
- **Diesel lights itself.** The moment diesel meets sufficiently hot, compressed air, it burns - immediately, uncontrollably, with no waiting. There is no "holding it ready" in a diesel cylinder.

Follow that second personality to its logical conclusion. If you mixed diesel into the air at the start, as petrol is mixed, the mixture would ignite by itself somewhere in the middle of compression - at whatever instant the temperature crossed the line - and the engine would bang at the wrong moments, with no control over timing. So the diesel design does the opposite: **it keeps the fuel out of the cylinder until the last possible moment**, injecting it into air that is already hot and squeezed. The fuel meets the ignition condition and burns the instant it arrives, at exactly the moment the injector chooses.

## Who decides the timing

Now the title question has an answer with two rows:

| Engine | When fuel enters | Who decides the burn timing |
|---|---|---|
| Petrol | Fuel mixed in during intake, early | The **spark plug** - it chooses the instant |
| Diesel | Fuel sprayed at the very end of compression | The **injector** - its spray timing is the spark's job |

In a petrol engine, fuel waits for the command to burn. In a diesel engine, fuel *is* the command: the injector's timing does the spark plug's job. Everything else in this phase - injectors, mist, cold mornings - hangs off this one distinction.

## Two consequences worth naming now

**Knocking.** The petrol engine's compression must stay *below* the level where petrol would light itself, because if petrol self-ignites early - in the middle of compression, at the wrong instant - the early burn collides with the piston still rising, producing a harsh metallic knock and damaging stress. That failure mode is called **knocking**, and it is why petrol engines squeeze less hard than diesels: their fuel must *not* be pushed to self-ignition.

**Build strength.** The diesel squeezes much harder than any petrol engine dares, so every diesel cycle puts higher forces on the piston, rod and cylinder. Diesel engines are therefore built heavier and stronger throughout - and they feel it in weight, which is one reason small budget cars historically leaned petrol and trucks and tractors leaned diesel.
