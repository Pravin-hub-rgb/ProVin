The oil carries a little of the engine's heat, and the last file left the larger shares unaccounted for. This file follows them. The question sounds simple - the bang is hot, the metal is hot, where does the heat actually go? - and the answer is a loop the driver never sees, running from the cylinders to the front of the car and back, every second the engine runs.

# 8.2 The Radiator - Where the Heat Goes

## The heat has three exits

Every bang releases heat - the burn is hot, and the squeezed gas was already hot before it burned. That heat leaves the engine by three routes.

The first is already familiar: the exhaust stroke (file 1.3) sweeps the hot burnt gas out of the cylinder, and a large share of the heat leaves with it down the tailpipe. File 7.2's turbo lives on exactly this stream, catching some of the exhaust's energy on its way out.

The second is the oil's small share from file 8.1 - heat soaked up at the rubbing points and released as the oil circulates.

The third is the largest of the remaining heat, and the one this file is about: the heat that soaks straight into the engine's own metal - the block around the cylinders and the head above them. That metal runs hot enough that leaving it alone means destruction. Something must carry that heat out, continuously, and that something is the coolant.

## The coolant loop

The coolant is a liquid - water mixed with **antifreeze**, the additive that keeps the mixture from freezing solid on winter mornings and from boiling away in summer heat. It flows through passages cast inside the block and head, wrapped around the outside of each cylinder, and soaks up heat from the metal as it passes.

From there the loop runs in one direction:

1. A **water pump**, driven by the engine, pushes the heated coolant out of the engine.
2. It flows into the **radiator** - a thin grid of narrow tubes and fins at the very front of the car, in the path of the air the car moves through.
3. Air rushing between the fins pulls the heat out of the coolant. When the car is moving, the passing air does the job; when the car is stopped, an electric fan behind the radiator pulls air through instead.
4. The cooled liquid flows back into the engine, around the cylinders again, and the loop repeats.

The word **radiator** says its own job: to radiate - the heat the coolant brought from the cylinders is given up to the passing air at the fins. The loop's whole purpose is to move heat from the one place it must not stay - the engine's metal - to the one place it can scatter freely - the air outside.

## What happens without coolant

Run the loop dry and the failure is not gradual. The metal climbs past the temperatures it was built for, and metal expands when it gets hot - parts machined with tiny clearances between them begin to grip. The oil overheats too and loses its ability to protect the surfaces it separates (file 8.1's film breaks down). The cylinder head's flat sealing face, the most heat-stressed large surface in the engine, can **warp** - bend out of true - and in the worst case the moving parts grip each other and the engine **seizes**: it stops dead, often permanently. Overheating is therefore among the fastest ways to destroy an engine, which is why the coolant level is treated as a check, not a formality.

## What this file is not

This file does not cover the **thermostat** - the valve between engine and radiator that decides when the coolant is allowed out to the radiator and when it circulates inside the engine only, letting a cold engine warm up quickly. It also does not cover the electric fan's control logic, the coolant's service intervals, or air-cooled engines - designs with fins on the cylinders that skip the liquid loop entirely. And it stays away from repair territory: diagnosing leaks, topping up, flushing - the mechanic's guide line holds here too.
