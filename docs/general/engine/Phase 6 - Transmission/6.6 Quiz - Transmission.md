# 6.6 Quiz - Transmission
## MCQ

Q: Why does the engine need a gearbox between itself and the wheels?
A. The wheels cannot spin at engine speed
B. The engine works well only in a band of speeds, while the wheels need very slow, high-force turning at launch and fast, low-force turning at cruise - no fixed ratio satisfies both
C. The gearbox multiplies the engine's total power
D. The differential requires one
> B. The engine's comfort band (roughly two to six thousand turns per minute) is narrow; the wheels' needs are wide. The gearbox swaps ratios to keep the engine in its band while the wheels get whatever speed and force the road demands.

---

Q: With a 1 cm-radius gear driving a 5 cm-radius gear, what is the trade?
A. Speed multiplied, force multiplied
B. Slow speed at the big gear, multiplied turning force
C. Fast speed, no change in force
D. Force divided, speed divided
> B. Meshed teeth force both edges to move at the same line speed, but the big gear's edge is five times longer - so the small gear turns five times for each big-gear turn. Small driving big: slow, strong. The reverse pair: fast, weak. A ratio only trades speed against force.

---

Q: Inside the gearbox, where does the selectable gear sit?
A. On the input shaft, which carries a set of swappable gears
B. On the output shaft, whose gears spin freely until the sliding collar locks one onto it
C. On the countershaft, which is disconnected during shifts
D. Outside the gearbox, on the drive shaft
> B. The input shaft carries one fixed gear; the countershaft is a solid block of fixed gears; only the output shaft's gears are unlocked, free-spinning - and the collar locks exactly one to the shaft to complete the power path.

---

Q: What is the countershaft?
A. A spare shaft used only in reverse
B. The second parallel shaft, permanently meshed with the input gear, carrying a solid block of fixed gears that all spin together
C. The shaft that connects to the wheels
D. The shaft the gear stick slides on
> B. The countershaft spins as one piece with the engine (through the input mesh), and its fixed gears - one per speed - are each permanently meshed with an output gear. Nothing on it is ever selected.

---

Q: Why are all the gear pairs permanently meshed?
A. To save metal
B. Because teeth meeting at mismatched speeds clash - selection locks a connection rather than bringing teeth together
C. Because the oil pump requires it
D. To make the gearbox lighter
> B. The same physics that grinds a starter gear against a spinning flywheel applies inside the gearbox. Constant mesh means no teeth ever collide during a shift; the collar merely locks or frees a connection.

---

Q: What is rev matching?
A. Revving the engine high to gain power before a shift
B. Adjusting the engine's speed with the accelerator while the clutch is open, so both sides spin at similar speeds when the clutch re-engages
C. Holding the engine at exactly 3000 rpm while driving
D. Matching the wheels' speed using the brake
> B. With the clutch open, the engine side falls toward idle while the wheels side follows the road. A blip of the accelerator lifts the engine side to the speed the selected gear needs, so the re-engagement is a settle instead of a violent drag.

---

Q: What does the differential do that the gearbox cannot?
A. Change the total power
B. Split one input rotation into two wheel outputs while allowing them to spin at different speeds from each other
C. Reverse the direction of travel
D. Store torque for later use
> B. In a turn the outer wheel must outrun the inner one. The differential's spider gears absorb that difference - one axle speeds up as the other slows - while passing the drive to both, something a rigid connection would fight with scrubbing tires.

---

Q: Why must the clutch pedal be pressed before moving the gear stick?
A. To stop the engine from stalling while stationary
B. To unload the gears, so the sliding collar can lock the new pair without teeth fighting under load
C. To spin the countershaft faster
D. To protect the differential from turning
> B. With the clutch engaged, engine force flows through the gear train and the meshed teeth press hard against each other. Sliding the collar then means forcing its teeth to lock against a hub that is being pushed - the crunch and grind. The pedal cuts the input shaft free, the load disappears, and the collar slides freely.

---

Q: In an automatic car, what sits between the engine and the gearbox in place of the clutch?
A. A second flywheel with stronger teeth
B. A torque converter - a sealed, oil-filled housing where two sets of blades face each other and power crosses as oil pushed from one wheel to the other
C. An electric motor that engages on demand
D. A clutch plate operated by the computer
> B. The engine-side wheel flings oil at the gearbox-side wheel; the moving oil pushes it around. No solid contact exists, so the two sides can spin at different speeds without anything needing to disconnect - the fluid link replaces the clutch pedal.

---

## TrueFalse

Q: First gear is the smallest gear in the gearbox.
- True
- False
> False. Each gear position is a pair. First gear is a small engine-side arrangement driving a large wheel-side one - the pair trades speed for force, roughly three or more engine turns per wheel turn. Higher positions are pairs that shrink the difference toward one.

---

Q: The countershaft's gears are free-spinning until selected.
- True
- False
> False. The countershaft is a solid block: its gears are fixed to it and all spin together whenever the input spins. The free-spinning gears are on the output shaft; the collar locks one of them.

---

Q: A downshift creates a larger speed mismatch at re-engagement than an upshift.
- True
- False
> True. On a downshift the engine side falls toward idle while the new, lower ratio demands it spin faster, so the wheels' side must lurch the engine upward through the gearbox - a bigger correction than an upshift's.

---

Q: The gear pair at the differential's entrance is called the final drive and is the drivetrain's last speed-for-force reduction.
- True
- False
> True. The drive shaft's pinion gear turns the differential's larger ring gear - one more reduction after the gearbox, trading engine speed for turning force before the rotation is split to the two wheels.

---

Q: An automatic car held at a red light with the engine idling still receives a weak oil push toward the wheels, which is why it may creep forward if the brake is released.
- True
- False
> True. At idle the engine flings the converter's oil weakly, so the push reaching the gearbox-side wheel is far too feeble to move a loaded car - but it is not zero. With the brake released, that push can overcome nothing but rolling drag, so many automatics creep slowly forward.

---

Q: The torque converter's lock-up clutch exists to make the two sides grip by friction at all times.
- True
- False
> False. The fluid slip is the converter's whole trick - it replaces the clutch pedal's disconnection. The lock-up clutch acts only at cruising speed, when both wheels already spin at nearly the same speed and slipping serves no purpose; it removes the slip exactly when the fluid link is not needed.

## ShortAnswer

Q: Trace the complete drivetrain from the clutch to the road, naming each part and its one-line job.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Clutch: the switch connecting or separating engine and wheels. Input shaft: delivers engine spin into the gearbox through its one fixed gear. Countershaft: a solid block of fixed gears, always turning with the input, offering one meshed pair per speed. Output shaft gears: free-spinning until the sliding collar locks one on, which selects the ratio. Drive shaft: carries the selected rotation toward the axle. Differential: its final-drive pair trades the last speed for force, then its spider gears split the rotation to two axles while letting them differ in speed. Axles and wheels: deliver the rotation to the road.

</details>

---

Q: A driver releases the clutch on a downshift without touching the accelerator, and the car lurches. Explain the lurch with the two-sides idea, and say what rev matching would have changed.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** With the clutch open, the engine side (flywheel and clutch plate) coasted down toward idle, while the wheels side kept the road's speed - and once the collar locked the lower gear, the locked pair was turning the input shaft with it, forcing it to spin faster than it had in fourth. The new, lower gear demands the engine side spin faster than it now is - so at re-engagement, friction had to violently accelerate the engine side to meet the wheels side, which the driver feels as a lurch, and the clutch's friction material paid for the disagreement in wear. Rev matching would have blipped the accelerator while the clutch was still open, lifting the engine side to the speed the lower gear demands, so the re-engagement would be a gentle settle instead of a forced correction.

</details>

---

Q: A car in fourth gear rolls at a road speed that turns the output side 1,000 turns per minute. The driver downshifts to second, whose pair makes the input shaft turn twice per output turn, while the unpowered flywheel falls toward idle at about 1,000. Walk through the speeds and explain the rev-matching blip with those numbers.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** The wheels keep the road's speed, so the output side stays at 1,000. When the collar locks the second-gear pair, the input shaft is instantly forced from its fourth-gear speed up to 2,000 - two input turns per output turn. Meanwhile the flywheel, with the foot off the gas, is dropping toward idle at about 1,000. At that instant the two sides are 1,000 rpm apart: releasing the clutch would make friction yank the engine side up by a thousand rpm - the lurch. The blip is a quick press of the accelerator while the clutch is still open, raising the flywheel from 1,000 toward the 2,000 the pair demands; the closer the blip brings it, the less friction has to correct, and the gentler the release.

</details>
