# 6.4 The Differential

## The problem the gearbox cannot solve

Trace the chain as it stands: **engine → clutch → gearbox → drive shaft** - one shaft, carrying one rotation, delivering it onward. The wheels, however, are **two** wheels on two ends of an axle, and here is the fact that breaks a simple connection:

**When a car turns, its two wheels must spin at different speeds.** The outer wheel of the turn travels a longer path than the inner wheel - it is farther from the corner's centre - and covering a longer path in the same time requires spinning faster. On any turn, the outer wheel must outrun the inner one.

If the two wheels were rigidly connected to one shaft, they would be forced to spin at the same speed. In a turn, one of them would then be turning too slowly and the other too fast for the path each travels - the tires would scrub, skip and grind across the pavement, fighting the car's own geometry on every corner.

So the last link must do something no gearbox does: take **one** input rotation and deliver it to **two** outputs - while allowing those two outputs to differ in speed at any moment.

## The part: what a differential is

The **differential** is a gear assembly sitting at the middle of the axle, where the drive shaft meets the wheels. Its name describes its function exactly: it allows a *difference* between the two wheels while passing the drive to both.

Mechanically, it works in two stages:

1. **Receiving and redirecting the drive.** The drive shaft's end carries a small gear that meshes with a larger **ring gear** mounted on the differential's housing, turning the drive's direction sideways toward the axles. (This first pair is also the drivetrain's last reduction, called the **final drive**: one more gear pair after the gearbox that further trades engine speed for turning force, using the same small-drives-big logic from file 6.1.)
2. **Splitting the rotation.** Inside the housing, the ring gear's rotation is passed to gears connected to each wheel's axle, with small **spider gears** sitting between them. When both wheels turn at the same speed - driving straight - the whole assembly rotates as one piece and the spiders merely turn with it. When the wheels must differ - any turn - the spider gears rotate on their own axes, **letting one axle turn faster while the other turns slower**, while still passing the drive's force to both.

That is the entire trick, and it is why the part exists: the spiders absorb the speed difference that a rigid axle would have to fight. The outer wheel speeds up, the inner wheel slows down, and the drive reaches both without either tire scrubbing.

## The full chain, complete at last

Every link this course has built, in order, now stands finished:

**Fuel and air → bang → piston → connecting rod → crankshaft → flywheel → clutch → gearbox → drive shaft → differential → axles → wheels.**

Read each station once more for what it contributes: the cylinders make the push (Phases 0-3); the crank converts it to rotation (Phase 0); the flywheel smooths it and starts it (Phase 4); the clutch switches it (Phase 4); the gearbox picks its ratio (Phase 6 so far); the differential splits it between two wheels while letting them disagree. One chain, from a chemical reaction to the contact patch of rubber on the road.

## What this file is not

The differential here is the **open** differential - the basic, standard design. Variations exist (limited-slip differentials that restrict how far the speeds may differ, and locking differentials for off-road use), all of which modify the spider-gear behaviour rather than replace the concept. This file also does not cover front-wheel-drive packaging, where the differential is combined with the gearbox in one housing - the mechanism is identical, only the mounting differs.
