The differential completed the manual drivetrain - one chain, every link named. But the chain so far has a pedal running through it: the driver's left foot decides when engine and wheels connect, and the driver's hand picks every ratio. A large share of the cars on the road have neither. This file explains how they do it - and the part that makes it possible is not a clutch at all.

# 6.5 The Automatic - The Torque Converter

## The same problem, no pedal

Start from the requirement, because it is the manual's requirement exactly. The engine must keep spinning whenever it runs; the wheels must sometimes be stopped. In a manual car, the clutch pedal is what separates the two - press it at a red light and the engine spins free while the car stands. An automatic car has no such pedal, but the engine still needs to keep running at that red light. So some part must do the connecting and disconnecting **by itself**, with no foot asking it to.

That part is the **torque converter**, and it sits where the flywheel and clutch sit in a manual: between the engine and the gearbox. Its name will make sense by the end of this file.

## The converter: two wheels, no contact

A **torque converter** is a round, sealed, doughnut-shaped metal housing, filled with oil, holding two sets of blades that face each other without ever touching:

- The **engine-side wheel** is attached to the engine, turning with the flywheel - it spins whenever the engine spins.
- The **gearbox-side wheel** is attached to the gearbox's input side - the path that leads to the wheels.

Between them is only oil. When the engine spins its wheel, the blades fling the oil outward and toward the other wheel; the moving oil strikes the second wheel's blades and pushes them around. The engine never grips the gearbox the way a clutch plate grips a flywheel. It pushes oil, and the oil pushes the other wheel. That is the whole connection - a fluid one, with no solid contact anywhere.

## What the oil does at idle, and at full throttle

The strength of that oil push depends entirely on how fast the engine's wheel is flinging it, and that single fact produces every behaviour an automatic driver knows.

**At idle with the brake on:** the engine spins slowly, so the oil is flung weakly. The push reaching the gearbox-side wheel is not zero - but it is far too feeble to move a loaded car. The car sits at the red light with the engine running, no pedal needed, because the fluid connection carries almost nothing at low speed. (Lift off the brake at idle and many automatics **creep** forward slowly - that weak push is still enough to overcome nothing but rolling drag.)

**On the gas:** press the accelerator and the engine speeds up, flinging the oil much harder. The push on the gearbox-side wheel grows with it until the force is enough to overcome the car's whole weight - on a slope, too - and the car moves off. The harder the engine works, the firmer the fluid link becomes.

**The slip that replaces the clutch pedal.** Notice what never happens: nothing ever has to be disconnected. The two wheels are simply *allowed* to spin at different speeds - the oil slips between them. With the car held by the brakes, the gearbox-side wheel can stand perfectly still while the engine spins freely; the oil absorbs the entire speed difference. The clutch pedal's whole job - separating two speeds that disagree - is done automatically, continuously, by the fluid itself. In the language of file 4.3, the converter is a clutch whose bite point never ends: some slip is always available whenever the two sides disagree.

## The price of the fluid link - and the lock-up clutch

That convenience costs energy. Oil is a much looser connection than metal pressed on metal: some of the engine's push is lost as the oil swirls and heats, and the driven wheel always turns a little slower than the driving one, because it is being pushed rather than locked on. That permanent slip wastes fuel - the manual's friction grip is, at full engagement, lossless, while the converter's fluid grip never quite is.

Modern converters reclaim most of the loss with a **lock-up clutch** inside the housing: at cruising speed, when the two wheels are already spinning at nearly the same speed and slipping serves no purpose, a friction clutch presses the two sides into solid contact - the fluid link is bypassed exactly when it is not needed. Slow down or pull away, and the lock releases, returning the slip that makes the converter work.

So which design wins? Manuals are traditionally a little more efficient and cheaper to build, and many drivers prefer the control. But modern automatics have closed the gap - their computers pick gears precisely and their lock-up clutches remove most of the slip - and today many match or beat the manuals they replaced.

## The gears that shift themselves

One piece remains, and this course keeps it deliberately abstract. A manual gearbox selects ratios with a sliding collar moved by hand (file 6.2). An automatic does the selecting with **planetary gearsets** - gears arranged around a central sun gear, always meshed, where holding or releasing different members of the arrangement changes the ratio - and the holding is done by hydraulics (oil pressure), commanded by the transmission's computer. The driver's only inputs are the same two every automatic summary reduces to: **how fast the car is going, and how hard the gas is pressed.** From those two facts, the transmission picks the ratio itself. The internal machinery of planetary sets is a mechanical subject of its own - what matters here is the division of labour: the torque converter replaces the clutch pedal, and the self-selecting gearsets replace the gear stick.

## What this file is not

This file does not cover the internals of planetary gearsets - how the sun gear, the carrier and the ring gear produce each individual ratio - which is a mechanical deep-dive beyond this course's scope. It also does not cover the other automatic designs (continuously variable transmissions that slide through infinitely many ratios, or dual-clutch gearboxes that automate the manual's clutch hardware): the torque converter automatic here is the classic and still most common design. And the converter's slip should not be read as a flaw to engineer away entirely - it is a feature (the automatic "clutch") and a cost (the lost efficiency) at the same time, which is why the lock-up clutch exists rather than a solid connection.
