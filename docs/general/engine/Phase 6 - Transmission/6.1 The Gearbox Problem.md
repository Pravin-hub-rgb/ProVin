# 6.1 The Gearbox Problem

## Where we are in the chain

The full chain from Phase 4: **engine → crankshaft → flywheel → clutch → input shaft → gearbox → drive shaft → wheels.** The clutch passes the engine's spin to the input shaft whenever it is engaged. From there, the spin enters the gearbox - and this file explains why something must happen between the input shaft and the wheels, rather than a straight connection.

## The two numbers that disagree

**The engine's comfort range.** A petrol engine does not work well at every speed. Below roughly a thousand turns per minute it stumbles and struggles to idle cleanly; at very high speeds it strains toward its limits. Its useful working range is a band - roughly a couple of thousand turns per minute up to around six thousand. Inside that band it delivers power willingly; outside it, poorly.

**The wheels' demands.** The wheels, meanwhile, need wildly different speeds and forces across a single drive:

- Pulling away from a standstill: the wheels turn **very slowly**, but each turn requires **a lot of turning force** - to accelerate the car's whole weight from zero.
- Cruising on a highway: the wheels turn **fast**, but need **very little force** - only enough to overcome air resistance and rolling drag.
- Climbing a steep slope at low speed: slow wheels, huge force again.

No single fixed connection satisfies both sides. Connect the engine straight to the wheels and either the engine is in its useless low range while the car crawls, or the engine runs out of revs while the car still needs speed. The engine's narrow band and the wheels' wide needs are simply incompatible at a fixed ratio.

## The fix: choose the ratio

The gearbox is a **ratio selector**. Between the engine's spin and the wheels' spin, it inserts a pair of gears whose sizes set the trade between turning force and speed:

- A **low gear** (first, second): the engine side spins many turns for each wheel turn. Result: lots of turning force at the wheels, low wheel speed - made for pulling away and climbing.
- A **high gear** (fourth, fifth, sixth): the engine side spins about the same as - or even less than - the wheels. Result: high speed, low force - made for cruising.

Changing gear changes nothing about the engine; it only swaps which ratio is connecting engine to wheels, keeping the engine inside its comfortable band while the wheels do whatever the road demands. That is the gearbox's entire purpose, and every mechanical detail in the next two files exists to make ratio-swapping possible while driving.

## The gear pair, worked with numbers

Because "gear ratio" is easier to trust with numbers, here is the one worked pair this course reuses, exactly as it was derived:

Two gears meshed together - a **big one with a 5 cm radius** and a **small one with a 1 cm radius**. Their teeth lock together, which forces one shared fact: **the two edges move at the same line speed** where they meet. But the big gear's edge is much longer than the small gear's edge, because a gear's edge length grows with its radius.

Now follow one full rotation of the **big gear**: its long edge rolls along the small gear's edge, and since the small gear's edge is five times shorter, the small gear must complete **five full rotations** to keep up.

So, with direction of drive deciding which trade you get:

- **Small gear drives big gear:** the big gear turns slowly but with a lot of turning force. (Force is multiplied; speed is divided.)
- **Big gear drives small gear:** the small gear spins fast but with little force. (Speed is multiplied; force is divided.)

Neither arrangement creates power - it only trades speed for force, or force for speed, in a fixed proportion set by the size difference.

## First gear, corrected

This is the correction that trips up almost everyone's first attempt at the concept. It is tempting to say "first gear is the small gear". The truth: **first gear is not a gear - it is a pair.** In first gear, the *engine-side* gear is small and the *wheel-side* gear is big: small drives big, slow and strong. The engine side spins about three or more times for every one turn of the wheels' side in a real first gear.

And the same correction applies upward: higher gears are not "bigger gears" - each is a pair whose engine-side gear gets relatively bigger and wheel-side gear relatively smaller as you climb, shrinking the ratio toward one. Fifth gear's ratio may be close to one, or even reversed - engine turning slower than the wheels - which is called overdrive.

## What this file is not

This file establishes *why* ratios are needed and *what a pair does*, but not yet *how a car physically switches between pairs while driving* - the shafts, the fixed and free-spinning gears, and the sliding collar are the next file. It also does not cover what happens to the clutch during a shift; that smoothness problem gets file 6.3 to itself.
