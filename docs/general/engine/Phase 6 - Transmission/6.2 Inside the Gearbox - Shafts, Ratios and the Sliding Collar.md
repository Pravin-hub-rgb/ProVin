# 6.2 Inside the Gearbox - Shafts, Ratios and the Sliding Collar

## Entering the box

The chain resumes exactly where file 4.3 left it: the clutch plate grips the flywheel and is attached to the **input shaft**, which spins whenever the clutch is engaged. That input shaft is the gearbox's front door - everything inside is arranged to offer it a menu of possible ratios to hand its spin to the output.

A note kept from the source discussion, and worth keeping: gearbox layouts vary between designs and eras, and what follows is the common simplified version - the one that makes the mechanism legible. The concepts transfer to real gearboxes even where the details differ.

## The three shafts, in order

**Shaft one: the input shaft, with one fixed gear.** The input shaft carries exactly **one gear, bolted on, that never changes** - it spins whenever the clutch is engaged, at engine speed (through the clutch). This answers a natural first guess: the input side is *not* where the gears switch. It has nothing to switch. Its single fixed gear is always meshed with the next shaft.

**Shaft two: the countershaft, a spinning block of fixed gears.** Below the input shaft runs a second, parallel shaft, the **countershaft**. The input's fixed gear meshes with one gear on it, so it turns whenever the input shaft turns. The countershaft is a solid piece carrying a whole row of **fixed gears of different sizes** - and because they are fixed to the shaft, they all spin together with it, as one block, whenever the input spins. Nothing on this shaft is ever selected or released; it just turns.

**Shaft three: the output shaft, with free-spinning gears.** The third shaft carries its own row of gears, **each one meshed permanently with one of the countershaft's gears**. Here is the trick that makes selection possible: these output gears are **not fixed to the output shaft**. Each spins freely around it - driven by its countershaft partner, but spinning at whatever speed that mesh dictates, while the output shaft itself may be turning at a completely different speed (or not at all, at a standstill).

So the box is full of meshed pairs, all spinning, all ready - and none of them connected to the output that matters yet.

## The sliding collar: how one pair gets chosen

The gear stick's job is to move a **sliding collar** - a sleeve on the output shaft that can slide along it and lock, by internal teeth, onto the hub of exactly **one** of the free-spinning output gears.

The moment the collar locks a gear to the shaft, that gear is no longer free-spinning: it and the shaft must turn together. From that instant, the power path is complete: input shaft → its fixed gear → countershaft block → the chosen countershaft gear → the chosen (now locked) output gear → output shaft → drive shaft → wheels.

Move the stick to another position and the collar releases one gear, slides, and locks another. The pairs themselves never move or change - the mesh was built at the factory. What changes is only **which free gear is locked on**.

Read it once more in the corrected language of file 6.1: the input gear is fixed and never changes; the selection happens on the output side; and each selectable position is a *pair* - the countershaft gear it meshes with and the output gear above it, whose relative sizes set that speed's trade between force and speed. In first gear, the engaged pair is sized so the output turns much slower than the engine - a real first gear turns the engine about three or more times for every turn of the wheels' side. Climbing each position upward, the pairs shrink the difference, until fifth or sixth runs the output nearly at engine speed - or slightly faster, the overdrive noted in file 6.1.

## Why the clutch must be pressed before every shift

The collar now explains a habit every manual driver follows without thinking: pressing the clutch pedal before moving the stick. With the clutch engaged, the engine's force flows through the whole gear train - input gear, countershaft, the locked pair - and the teeth of every meshed gear are pressed hard against each other under load. Sliding the collar across that loaded arrangement means forcing its internal teeth to lock onto a hub that is being pushed by the engine: the teeth fight each other, and the result is the crunching, grinding sound of a shift attempted without the clutch.

Press the pedal first and the input shaft is cut free from the flywheel (file 4.3). Engine force no longer flows through the gears, so the load disappears - and the collar can slide and lock the new gear freely, with nothing squeezing the teeth against each other. The sequence is not a ritual: press to unload, slide to select, release to reload. Real gearboxes add synchronizing hardware so small speed mismatches are forgiven - noted in this file's closing section - but unloading the gears is still the shift's first requirement.

## Why this arrangement, and not gears that swap

Two design points explain themselves once seen. First, every pair is **always meshed** - nothing ever has to slam two spinning gears' teeth together (the starter motor's grinding lesson from file 4.2 applies: teeth meeting at mismatched speeds clash). Selection never moves gear teeth; it only locks or frees a connection. Second, because the countershaft is one solid block, choosing a ratio is mechanically trivial: lock one free gear, and its whole pre-built pair comes alive.

## What this file is not

This file does not cover what the driver must do about *speeds* during a shift - when the clutch is pressed, the engine side and the wheels side are spinning at rates set for different ratios, and reconnecting them needs a speed adjustment. That is rev matching, and it has file 6.3 entirely to itself. It also does not cover the synchronizing parts that help the collar lock smoothly in real gearboxes - the simplified collar here is the concept; real cars add hardware to make the same idea kind to the teeth.
