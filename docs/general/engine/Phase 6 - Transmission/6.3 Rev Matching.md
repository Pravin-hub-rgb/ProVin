The gearbox is built - three shafts, a block of fixed gears, free-spinning outputs, and a collar that selects. But every shift hides one moment of violence that this file defuses: the instant the clutch re-engages and two sides spinning at different speeds are forced into agreement. The technique for making that moment gentle has a name you have likely heard - rev matching - and its logic is entirely a restatement of file 6.2.

# 6.3 Rev Matching

## The mismatch, spelled out

Follow one shift precisely. The clutch is pressed: the engine side - the flywheel and clutch plate - disconnects from the wheels side (output shaft, gearbox internals, wheels). From that moment the two sides spin independently:

- The **wheels side** keeps spinning at whatever speed the road demands - it is locked to the road, and its speed cannot be chosen.
- The **engine side** drops quickly toward idle, because with the clutch open, nothing loads it.

And here is the detail that makes the arithmetic exact: the moment the collar locks the new gear (file 6.2), the input shaft stops belonging to the engine side. The locked pair is driven by the still-rolling wheels, and it turns the countershaft and the input shaft with it - at whatever speed that ratio dictates. So during the shift there are exactly two speed facts to know: the flywheel, falling toward idle, and the input shaft, set by the road speed through the selected pair. The whole technique lives in the gap between those two numbers.

Now release the clutch. At the instant of re-engagement, friction must force the two independent speeds to become one common speed. If they are far apart, that reconciliation is not gentle: the engine is abruptly dragged to match the wheels (or vice versa), and the driver feels it as a **jerk**, while the clutch's friction material pays for the disagreement in wear - the exact toll described in file 4.3.

## The downshift, with numbers

Whether the sides disagree - and by how much - follows from the pair just locked. Take a downshift from fourth to second, with made-up round numbers. The car still rolls, so the output side keeps turning at, say, 1,000 turns per minute. The second-gear pair is sized so the input shaft turns twice for every output turn (file 6.1's small-drives-big direction): the moment the collar locks it, the input shaft is forced up from its old fourth-gear speed to 2,000. Meanwhile the flywheel, unpowered with the foot off the gas, is falling toward idle - call it 1,000. The gap is a full thousand turns per minute: a flywheel at 1,000 against an input shaft at 2,000. Release the clutch there and friction must yank the engine side up by a thousand rpm - the lurch. **Blip the accelerator** - a quick press while the clutch is still open - and the flywheel climbs to meet the 2,000 before the grip arrives; the closer the blip brings it, the gentler the release.

## The upshift, where the two sides already agree

An upshift runs the opposite arithmetic, and it is the easier direction. Second to third: the taller pair forces the input shaft *slower* than it spun a moment ago, and the flywheel, falling toward idle since the foot lifted off, is coming down too. The two descend toward each other and end up fairly close - which is why most drivers release the clutch gently on upshifts and the shift is smooth without any blip. Enthusiasts blip on upshifts as well, chasing perfectly seamless or very fast shifts, but for everyday driving the upshift needs nothing more than a patient release.

## The fix: adjust one side to meet the other

The wheels side cannot be adjusted - it follows the road. So the only side available is the engine's. **Rev matching** is the act of adjusting the engine's speed, with a press of the accelerator while the clutch is still open, so that when the clutch re-engages, **both sides are spinning at similar speeds for the gear being selected**.

Done right, the reconciliation shrinks from a violent drag to a mild settle: the friction has almost nothing to correct, the engagement is smooth, the jerk disappears, and the clutch's wear drops accordingly. The name describes the act literally - you *match the revs* (the engine's turns per minute, the tachometer's number, whose full treatment waits in file 7.3) to the speed the drivetrain needs.

## Where the technique lives

In everyday driving, a driver's clutch work plus the gearbox's own hardware (the synchronizing parts mentioned in file 6.2) make ordinary shifts acceptable without conscious rev matching. The technique becomes deliberate and valued in two places:

- **Skilled manual driving, especially downshifts.** Experienced drivers *blip* the throttle - a quick press of the accelerator while the clutch is open and the new gear is selected - to lift the engine side up to the speed the lower gear demands. This is the classic, audible sign of a driver who knows the machinery.
- **Cars that do it for you.** Many modern manual and automatic performance cars carry electronics that blip the throttle automatically on downshifts - the same physics, executed by the computer.

Either way, the goal is identical: bring the two independent speeds close together *before* friction is asked to merge them.

## The sequence, and where the blip goes

The whole shift has an agreed order, and the order matters:

1. **Lift off the gas.** The engine must stop being powered before it can be disconnected - pressing the clutch while the gas is still down leaves an unloaded engine revving freely.
2. **Press the clutch.** In practice the two happen almost together, gas first by a split second - it feels like one motion.
3. **Move the stick.** The collar slides and locks the new pair - possible only because the clutch un-loaded the gears (file 6.2).
4. **Blip - downshifts only.** A quick press of the accelerator while the clutch is still open, lifting the flywheel toward the speed the pair demands. On an upshift, skip it.
5. **Release the clutch gently.** The grip arrives with both sides already close, so friction has almost nothing to correct.

## What it is not

Rev matching is **not** revving the engine high for its own sake, and not a way to add power. It is a speed-matching courtesy between two sides of one drivetrain, performed at the moment they are about to be reconnected. If the blip is mistimed or the wrong size, it helps nothing - the match must be for the *selected gear's* required engine speed, which is why the technique is a skill: the correct blip depends on road speed, gear, and ratio, together.

And it is not a substitute for the clutch. The clutch still does the connecting; rev matching only removes the speed disagreement that the clutch would otherwise be forced to fix the hard way.

## What this file is not

This file does not cover the racing variant of the technique that also uses the right foot on the brake pedal simultaneously - a coordination skill outside this course's scope. It also does not cover automatic transmissions, which solve the same mismatch problem with a fluid connection instead of a friction clutch - file 6.5, right after the differential closes the manual chain.
