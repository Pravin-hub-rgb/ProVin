The gearbox is built - three shafts, a block of fixed gears, free-spinning outputs, and a collar that selects. But every shift hides one moment of violence that this file defuses: the instant the clutch re-engages and two sides spinning at different speeds are forced into agreement. The technique for making that moment gentle has a name you have likely heard - rev matching - and its logic is entirely a restatement of file 6.2.

# 6.3 Rev Matching

## The mismatch, spelled out

Follow one shift precisely. The clutch is pressed: the engine side (flywheel, clutch plate, input shaft) disconnects from the wheels side (output shaft, gearbox internals, wheels). From that moment, the two sides spin independently:

- The **wheels side** keeps spinning at whatever speed the road demands - it is locked to the road, and its speed cannot be chosen.
- The **engine side** drops quickly toward idle, because with the clutch open, nothing loads it.

Now select the new gear - the collar locks the new pair - and release the clutch. At the instant of re-engagement, friction must force the two independent speeds to become one common speed. If they are far apart, that reconciliation is not gentle: the engine is abruptly dragged to match the wheels (or vice versa), and the driver feels it as a **jerk**, while the clutch's friction material pays for the disagreement in wear - the exact toll described in file 4.3.

Whether the sides disagree depends on the shift. On an **upshift**, the engine side is usually spinning too fast for the new, taller ratio. On a **downshift** - the harsher case - the engine side falls toward idle while the new, lower ratio demands it spin *faster*: re-engage carelessly and the wheels' side lurches the engine upward through the gearbox.

## The fix: adjust one side to meet the other

The wheels side cannot be adjusted - it follows the road. So the only side available is the engine's. **Rev matching** is the act of adjusting the engine's speed, with a press of the accelerator while the clutch is still open, so that when the clutch re-engages, **both sides are spinning at similar speeds for the gear being selected**.

Done right, the reconciliation shrinks from a violent drag to a mild settle: the friction has almost nothing to correct, the engagement is smooth, the jerk disappears, and the clutch's wear drops accordingly. The name describes the act literally - you *match the revs* (the engine's turns per minute, the tachometer's number, whose full treatment waits in file 7.3) to the speed the drivetrain needs.

## Where the technique lives

In everyday driving, a driver's clutch work plus the gearbox's own hardware (the synchronizing parts mentioned in file 6.2) make ordinary shifts acceptable without conscious rev matching. The technique becomes deliberate and valued in two places:

- **Skilled manual driving, especially downshifts.** Experienced drivers *blip* the throttle - a quick press of the accelerator while the clutch is open and the new gear is selected - to lift the engine side up to the speed the lower gear demands. This is the classic, audible sign of a driver who knows the machinery.
- **Cars that do it for you.** Many modern manual and automatic performance cars carry electronics that blip the throttle automatically on downshifts - the same physics, executed by the computer.

Either way, the goal is identical: bring the two independent speeds close together *before* friction is asked to merge them.

## What it is not

Rev matching is **not** revving the engine high for its own sake, and not a way to add power. It is a speed-matching courtesy between two sides of one drivetrain, performed at the moment they are about to be reconnected. If the blip is mistimed or the wrong size, it helps nothing - the match must be for the *selected gear's* required engine speed, which is why the technique is a skill: the correct blip depends on road speed, gear, and ratio, together.

And it is not a substitute for the clutch. The clutch still does the connecting; rev matching only removes the speed disagreement that the clutch would otherwise be forced to fix the hard way.

## What this file is not

This file does not cover the racing variant of the technique that also uses the right foot on the brake pedal simultaneously - a coordination skill outside this course's scope. It also does not cover automatic transmissions, which solve the same mismatch problem with a different connecting device, mentioned only in passing in Phase 7.
