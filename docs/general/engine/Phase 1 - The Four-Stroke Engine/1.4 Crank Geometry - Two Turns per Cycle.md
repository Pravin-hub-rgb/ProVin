The four strokes are walked, but a number is still missing from the story: how much does the crankshaft actually turn during one full cycle? The answer is a piece of pure geometry that flows directly from file 0.3, and it is the reason engine flywheels and multi-cylinder designs exist at all.

# 1.4 Crank Geometry - Two Turns per Cycle

## One stroke equals half a turn

Take the crank throw - the off-center point the connecting rod pushes - and watch where it sits as the piston travels.

- When the piston is at the **very top** of the cylinder, the throw is at the **very top** of its circle around the shaft.
- When the piston is at the **very bottom**, the throw is at the **very bottom** of its circle.

This is not a coincidence; it is forced by the rigid link. The connecting rod physically joins the piston to the throw, so the piston's highest position must line up with the throw's highest position, and the same at the bottom.

Now the geometry: a point moving from the highest position of its circle to the lowest position travels **exactly half of that circle**. So:

- **One stroke = half a turn of the crankshaft.** Down stroke: half a turn. Up stroke: the other half, completing the circle.

## Four strokes, four halves, two turns

Stack it up for the full four-stroke cycle:

- Intake (down): half a turn - turn 1 completed at compression's end? No - carefully: intake + compression are the first two strokes, so they make the **first full turn**.
- Power (down): half a turn.
- Exhaust (up): the final half, closing the **second full turn**.

Four strokes, four half-turns, **two full turns of the crankshaft per cycle**. Intake and compression make the first turn; power and exhaust make the second.

This is not a design choice the engineer made - it is a physical necessity. The piston is rigidly connected to the shaft through the rod and throw, so the shaft *must* turn exactly twice for the piston to complete its four trips. The shaft has no freedom in the matter.

## The quiet-turns problem

Now put the accounting of file 1.2 on top of this geometry. Per cycle - two full shaft turns - the power stroke occupies only one of the four half-turns. For three half-turns out of every four, **nothing pushes the piston at all**: intake, compression and exhaust all require the mechanism to move without a driving force behind them.

So what turns the crankshaft during the quiet half-turns? Two things, working together:

1. **The flywheel.** The power stroke's push spins the shaft, and the heavy flywheel bolted to it resists slowing down. Its stored momentum carries the crank around through the quiet half-turns, including the effortful squeeze of compression.
2. **The other cylinders.** In a multi-cylinder engine, every cylinder is connected to the same crankshaft. While one cylinder sits in its quiet strokes, another is somewhere in its power stroke, and its push turns the shared shaft - dragging the quiet cylinder through its housekeeping.

Notice that these are the same idea at two scales: stored spin from recent bangs keeps the loop alive between pushes. The bang does not directly hand energy to a neighbouring cylinder; it turns the shaft, and the shaft carries *everything* - the flywheel and all the pistons - through the next segment of their cycles.

## Why more cylinders (a preview)

One bang per two turns, all alone, makes a rough, shaky machine - and the only fix inside a single cylinder is a heavier flywheel or a bigger cylinder, both of which make the shaking worse. The real fix was to split the same power across several smaller cylinders firing in turns, so a push is always happening somewhere on the shared shaft. That history - from single-cylinder first engines to fours, sixes and eights - gets its full file in Phase 5 (file 5.1). Here, hold only the mechanism: shared crankshaft, staggered cycles, smoother shaft.
