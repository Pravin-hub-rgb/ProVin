# 1.3 The Four Strokes One by One

## Where to start: a convention, not a fact

A cycle is a loop, so it has no true starting point - you could begin watching anywhere. The convention is to start with **intake**, because that is where a fresh batch of mixture enters. So set the scene: the piston is at the very top of the cylinder, everything is closed, and the previous cycle has just swept the burnt gas out.

## Stroke 1 - Intake (piston down)

The **intake valve** (the opening at the top of the cylinder that lets fresh mixture in) opens. The piston travels down.

Here is the correction, and it matters: **the incoming mixture does not push the piston down. The piston going down is what pulls the mixture in.**

The direction of cause and effect is exactly backwards from the instinct. As the piston descends, it makes more room inside the cylinder, and more room means lower pressure in there. The outside air, at its normal pressure, then pushes its way in to fill the space - and it carries the petrol mist along with it. By the time the piston reaches the bottom, the cylinder is full of fresh mixture, and the intake valve closes.

Two things follow from understanding this direction correctly:

1. The intake stroke consumes energy; it produces none. Something else must be dragging the piston down against mild resistance. In a multi-cylinder engine it is other cylinders' power strokes turning the shared crankshaft; in a single-cylinder engine it is the flywheel's stored momentum. Both are the same idea: the bang of a previous cycle pays for this housekeeping.
2. The engine "breathes by suction" - the filling is not pumped in, it is drawn in by the moving piston.

## Stroke 2 - Compression (piston up)

Both valves are now shut, so the cylinder is sealed with the fresh mixture trapped inside. The piston travels up, and the trapped mixture is squeezed into a small fraction of its former space. This is the **compression stroke**.

Why bother squeezing? Because a squeezed mixture burns far more powerfully than a loose one, for a physical reason: squeezing packs the fuel and air molecules much closer together, so the burn can spread through them much faster, releasing its heat almost all at once instead of trickling. A compressed mixture is also hotter to begin with. The result is a sudden, strong shove instead of a weak fizz.

Note the cost, though. The squeezed gas pushes back hard while being compressed - compression is work done *against* the mixture, not by it. The engine pays for the squeeze with energy stored from the previous bang, via the flywheel and (in multi-cylinder engines) other cylinders' pushes. Nothing in the four-stroke cycle is free except stroke 3.

## Stroke 3 - Power (piston down)

At the very end of compression, with the piston at the top and the mixture packed tight, the **spark plug** (the part that produces an electric spark at the top of the cylinder) fires. The mixture burns almost instantly. The hot gas wants enormously more room, both valves are still shut, and the only movable wall is the piston - so the piston is shoved down with real force.

This is the **power stroke**, the only stroke in the entire cycle that produces energy. Its push spins the crankshaft and tops up the flywheel, which then pays for strokes 1, 2 and 4 of the next cycle. Everything else in the engine exists to keep this stroke fed and cleaned up after.

## Stroke 4 - Exhaust (piston up)

At the very bottom of the power stroke, with the cylinder full of burnt waste gas, the **exhaust valve** (the opening that lets burnt gas out) opens. Note the timing precisely: it opens at the *end* of stroke 3, not the beginning - if it opened earlier, the bang's pressure would leak out the exhaust instead of pushing the piston, wasting the power stroke.

The piston now travels up and shoves the burnt gas out through the open exhaust valve. At the top, the exhaust valve closes, the intake valve opens, and the loop is back at stroke 1.

## The loop in one table

| Stroke | Piston | Valves | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Intake | Down | Intake open | Piston's descent draws in air + petrol mist |
| 2. Compression | Up | Both shut | Trapped mixture squeezed; spark fires at the very end |
| 3. Power | Down | Both shut | Burning gas shoves piston - the only energy-making stroke |
| 4. Exhaust | Up | Exhaust open | Piston sweeps burnt gas out; then intake opens again |

Two full turns of the crankshaft, one bang, and the loop never stops while the engine runs.

5. Which stroke pays for the other three, and through what two channels?

In a multi-cylinder engine, the cylinders take turns firing, so other cylinders' power strokes also push the shared crankshaft and carry a given cylinder's quiet strokes.
