The quiz closed Phase 0 with a complete, self-running steam engine - and also with the reason it could never power a car. This file begins Phase 1 by confronting that reason directly, because the fix it produced is the single most important sentence in the history of the automobile: burn the fuel inside the cylinder itself.

# 1.1 Why Burn Inside the Cylinder

## What the steam engine costs you

Take stock of what a steam vehicle must haul around, before it produces a single push:

- A **boiler** - a large, heavy metal tank.
- A **coal fire** burning under it, with coal stored on board.
- **Water**, also on board, also heavy.
- And, worst of all, **waiting time**: the fire has to heat the water into steam before the engine can do anything at all. You cannot start a steam vehicle now; you start it thirty minutes from now.

For a factory near a coal pile, these are manageable costs. For a vehicle, they are fatal. Every kilogram of boiler, coal and water is weight that must be carried by the very engine it feeds, and the vehicle must sit cold and useless until the steam is ready.

The steam engine's problem is not how it pushes - Phase 0 proved the pushing works beautifully. Its problem is **where the pushing gas is made**: outside, in a separate, heavy, slow heating plant.

## The idea: make the pushing gas at the spot of use

The internal combustion idea removes the middleman. Instead of heating water somewhere else to produce a pushing gas, **burn the fuel inside the cylinder itself**, so the burning directly creates the hot, expanding gas that shoves the piston.

That is the meaning of the name, taken literally: **internal combustion** means the burning happens *internally* - inside the working chamber. Combustion is just the proper word for burning. The steam engine, by contrast, burns its fuel *outside* the cylinder (in the fire under the boiler), which makes it an external combustion engine.

## What burning gives you

Recall the physics from Phase 0, because none of it changes. Burning fuel releases heat very quickly. The hot gas that results wants far more room than the cold mixture it came from. If that hot gas is trapped in a chamber with exactly one movable wall - the piston - it shoves the piston, exactly the way steam did.

Same push. The difference is location: the gas is born inside the chamber, at the exact place and moment it is needed. That single change deletes the boiler, the coal store, the water tank, and the half-hour warm-up. What remains is small, light, and ready within seconds - which is what makes the difference between a stationary factory machine and a vehicle engine.

## The fuel and its partner

The fuel that made this practical was **petrol**, and it never burns alone. What actually sits in the cylinder and burns is a **mixture of air and petrol mist** - petrol broken into tiny droplets mixed through the air. Air matters because burning is a chemical reaction with oxygen, and air is the free, endless supplier of oxygen.

So the new recipe for one push: trap air and petrol mist in the sealed pocket above the piston, light it, and the burning mixture expands hard and shoves the piston down. In this rough form, the description of a car engine's power stroke is already complete.

## What carries over, what gets deleted

The steam engine taught every mechanical idea this course will use, and almost all of it survives the transition:

| Carried over from Phase 0 | Deleted with the boiler |
|---|---|
| Cylinder and piston | Boiler |
| Sealed pocket, push on the movable wall | Coal fire and coal storage |
| Valve-controlled openings | Water tank |
| Connecting rod, crank throw, crankshaft | External heating plant |
| Self-timing loop (0.4's idea) | Half-hour warm-up |

The push is still gas pressure on a piston. The rotation is still made by an off-center crank. The valves still need timing. What changed is only the source of the push - and that one change turns a factory boiler into something you can hold in your hands.

## What this file is not

This is not yet the four-stroke cycle. Burning fuel inside the cylinder solves the boiler problem, but it immediately creates a scheduling problem the steam engine never had: a steam engine receives its ready-made gas from outside, but a cylinder that burns fuel must somehow bring the air and fuel in, prepare them, burn them at the right instant, and eject the burnt waste - all inside the same small chamber. Managing those jobs, one piston trip at a time, is the four-stroke cycle, and it starts in the next file.

Also, this file does not yet explain how the petrol becomes a mist, who lights the fire, or when. Those are Phase 3 subjects.
