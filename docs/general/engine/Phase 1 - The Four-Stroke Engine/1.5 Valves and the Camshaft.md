# 1.5 Valves and the Camshaft

## First, the valve's default state

Both valves in the cylinder - the intake and the exhaust - must be shut almost all the time. They only open for their own moment. So each valve is not held open by anything active: it is held **shut by a spring**, and opened by something that pushes against that spring at the right instant. When the push goes away, the spring snaps the valve shut again.

That arrangement means the whole timing question reduces to one job: **push each valve at exactly the right moment, and never otherwise.**

## The part that does the pushing: the camshaft

A **camshaft** is a long rotating rod with egg-shaped bumps on it, one bump per valve. Each bump is called a **cam lobe**. (Do not confuse this with the crank *throw* - a throw is the offset point the connecting rod grips on the crankshaft; a lobe is the egg shape that pushes a valve.)

As the camshaft spins, a lobe rotates around until its tall side faces the valve - and the lobe pushes the valve open, compressing the spring. As the rotation carries the lobe past, the short side follows, the push disappears, and the spring slams the valve shut. Spin the camshaft and the valves open and close in the rhythm of the bumps passing them.

## The timing is baked into the geometry

Here is where the steam engine's lesson returns. Nothing decides when a lobe pushes its valve - the decision was made once, at manufacturing time, by **where the lobe is welded or machined onto the rod**. The pattern is physically built into the shape and position of the bumps. An engine is not thinking about timing; its timing is a shape rotating in space.

And the camshaft is not left to spin at some free speed of its own. It is connected to the crankshaft by a **timing belt** (or, in some engines, a **timing chain** - the same job done with metal links). This is why the part is called the *timing* belt: its only job is to lock the camshaft's rotation in exact step with the crankshaft's. Because the crankshaft's position *is* the cycle's position (file 1.4), a camshaft locked to it delivers every lobe at exactly the right moment, every cycle, forever.

## Why the camshaft turns at exactly half speed

This is the detail worth deriving, because it is one of those facts that seems arbitrary until you count.

Count what each valve must do across one full cycle: the **intake valve opens once** (during intake) and the **exhaust valve opens once** (during exhaust). Once each, per cycle. Nothing opens twice.

Now count the crankshaft across the same cycle: from file 1.4, it turns **twice**.

So for each valve to open exactly once per cycle, the camshaft must make **exactly one turn per cycle** - if it turned twice, every lobe would pass its valve twice and every valve would open twice per cycle, wrecking the sequence.

That is the whole reason: **one cycle = two crankshaft turns = one camshaft turn**, so the camshaft must rotate at exactly half the crankshaft's speed. The mechanism that enforces it sits where the timing belt meets them: the gear on the camshaft has **twice as many teeth** as the gear on the crankshaft, so the crankshaft has to turn twice for the camshaft to turn once. The half-speed is built into the tooth count.

## The full choreography, now conducted

Put the pieces together with the valve trace from file 1.3:

1. Intake lobe pushes the intake valve open as the piston starts down; spring closes it at the bottom.
2. Both valves shut through compression; the spark fires at the very end.
3. Both valves shut through the power stroke; the exhaust lobe arrives at the very bottom.
4. The exhaust valve stays open through the exhaust stroke; spring closes it at the top; the intake lobe arrives to begin the next cycle.

Two crankshaft turns, one camshaft turn, each valve open once - a repeating machine with no brain, driven entirely by shapes and tooth counts.

One closing fact: the engine in the diagrams you may have seen often shows **two camshafts** - one running the intake valves, one running the exhaust valves. That arrangement has its own name and its own trade-offs, covered with the V engine in file 5.2. And the whole chain now stands complete: fuel burns, the piston is pushed, the connecting rod turns the crank, and the timing belt keeps the camshaft opening the valves in step.

1. Why is each valve held shut by a spring instead of being actively held open or shut?

**Sample Answer:** A valve must be shut almost the entire cycle and open only for its own moment. A spring provides the shut state for free and continuously, so the timing mechanism only needs to push the valve open at the right instant; when the push passes, the spring restores the shut state automatically.

2. What is a cam lobe, and how does it open a valve?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** A cam lobe is the egg-shaped bump on the camshaft, one per valve. As the camshaft spins, the lobe's tall side rotates around and pushes the valve open against its spring; as the lobe rotates past, the push vanishes and the spring snaps the valve shut. Not to be confused with the crank throw, which is the connecting rod's offset point on the crankshaft.

</details>

3. Why must the camshaft rotate at exactly half the crankshaft's speed?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** Each valve must open exactly once per cycle, so the camshaft must make one turn per cycle - any faster and a valve would open twice per cycle. One cycle takes two full crankshaft turns (file 1.4), so the camshaft turns once per two crank turns. The timing belt enforces it with a camshaft gear of twice the teeth.

</details>

4. Where is the valve timing "decided" in a modern engine?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** Nowhere at runtime - it is decided once at manufacturing, by where each cam lobe sits on the camshaft and by the tooth counts of the gears linked by the timing belt. The pattern is baked into the geometry; the rotating shapes deliver it every cycle.

</details>

5. What is the timing belt's job, and why is it named that?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** Its job is to lock the camshaft's rotation in exact step with the crankshaft, so every lobe arrives at its valve at precisely the right moment in the cycle. It is named for that role: it carries the timing. In some engines the same job is done by a timing chain.

</details>
