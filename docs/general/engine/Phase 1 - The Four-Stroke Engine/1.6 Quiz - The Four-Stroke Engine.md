# 1.6 Quiz - The Four-Stroke Engine
## MCQ

Q: Why did burning fuel inside the cylinder make vehicle engines practical where steam engines were not?
A. Burning inside produces a stronger push than steam can
B. It removes the boiler, coal, water and warm-up delay - the pushing gas is made at the spot of use
C. Petrol is cheaper than coal
D. Internal burning lets the engine skip the crankshaft
> B. The steam engine's push worked fine; the fatal cost for a vehicle was making the gas in a separate heavy plant. Internal combustion creates the hot expanding gas directly in the cylinder, deleting the boiler, the fuel and water storage, and the half-hour wait.

---

Q: What actually burns in the cylinder of a petrol engine?
A. Petrol alone, as a liquid
B. A mixture of air and petrol mist
C. Petrol vapour with no air, ignited by the spark
D. Pure oxygen and petrol in equal parts
> B. Burning is a chemical reaction needing oxygen, so what burns is air carrying petrol mist - petrol broken into tiny droplets. Air is the free oxygen supplier.

---

Q: During the intake stroke, what makes the fresh mixture enter the cylinder?
A. The mixture is pushed in ahead of the descending piston
B. The piston's descent creates lower pressure inside, so outside air pushes in carrying the mist
C. A pump in the intake path forces it in
D. The spark's suction pulls it in
> B. Cause and effect run from the piston: it descends, makes more room, drops the cylinder's pressure, and the outside air pushes its way in with the petrol mist. The mixture never pushes the piston down.

---

Q: Why does the engine compress the mixture before lighting it?
A. To make room for the burnt gas later
B. Compressed molecules are packed close, so the burn spreads fast and releases heat all at once - a strong push instead of a weak one
C. Compression cleans the mixture
D. To prevent the spark from firing early
> B. Squeezing packs fuel and air molecules close together so burning races through them almost instantly, and the compressed mixture is hotter to begin with. The push is far stronger than from a loose mixture.

---

Q: When exactly does the exhaust valve open?
A. At the start of the compression stroke
B. At the very end of the power stroke, as the piston reaches the bottom
C. At the same instant as the spark
D. Halfway down the power stroke
> B. It opens at the end of stroke 3. Any earlier and the bang's pressure would leak out the exhaust instead of shoving the piston, wasting the only energy-making stroke.

---

Q: How many full turns does the crankshaft make during one four-stroke cycle, and why?
A. One - because one bang is one push
B. Two - four strokes at half a turn each
C. Four - one turn per stroke
D. Half - the shaft only turns during the power stroke
> B. Each stroke moves the crank throw between opposite extremes of its circle - exactly half a turn. Four half-turns make two full turns, forced by the rigid piston-rod-throw link.

---

Q: Why must the camshaft rotate at half the crankshaft's speed?
A. To reduce wear on the timing belt
B. Each valve must open once per cycle, and one cycle takes two crankshaft turns - so the camshaft must make one turn per two crank turns
C. The camshaft is longer and heavier, so it needs less speed
D. To keep the valves shut during the power stroke
> B. One camshaft turn delivers each lobe once; the cycle spans two crank turns. The camshaft gear has twice as many teeth as the crankshaft gear, enforcing the half speed.

---

## TrueFalse

Q: Opening or closing a valve counts as a stroke.
- True
- False
> False. A stroke is one single-direction trip of the piston - up or down. A valve switching is a quick event at the boundary between strokes.

---

Q: In a multi-cylinder engine, one cylinder's power stroke hands its energy directly to the neighbouring cylinder.
- True
- False
> False. The cylinders share one crankshaft: a bang turns the shaft, and the shaft drags every piston - including the neighbouring ones - through their current jobs. The sharing happens through the shaft.

---

Q: The spark plug fires at the very end of the compression stroke.
- True
- False
> True. The mixture is packed tight and the piston is at the top when the spark fires; the burning and the resulting push constitute the power stroke that follows.

---

Q: The valve timing in a camshaft engine is decided while the engine runs, by a controller.
- True
- False
> False. It is baked in at manufacturing: where each cam lobe sits on the camshaft and the gear tooth counts linked by the timing belt. The rotating geometry delivers the timing every cycle, with no decisions at runtime.

## ShortAnswer

Q: Walk through all four strokes, naming piston direction, valve states, and what happens in each.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Intake: intake valve open, piston travels down, and its descent drops the cylinder's pressure so outside air pushes in carrying petrol mist; at the bottom the intake closes. Compression: both valves shut, piston travels up, squeezing the trapped mixture into a small space; the spark fires at the very end. Power: both valves still shut, the burning mixture expands hard and shoves the piston down - the only energy-making stroke. Exhaust: at the very bottom the exhaust valve opens, the piston travels up shoving the burnt gas out; at the top the exhaust closes, the intake opens, and the loop restarts. Two full crankshaft turns per cycle.

</details>

---

Q: A friend says: "The power stroke powers the whole engine, so the other three strokes are free." What is wrong with that statement?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** The other three strokes are not free - they cost effort. Compression in particular is work done against the trapped mixture, which pushes back hard. The power stroke's push tops up the flywheel (and, in multi-cylinder engines, turns the shared shaft), and that stored spin from recent bangs is what carries the three quiet strokes. Only one stroke in four produces energy; the engine spends part of it running itself.

</details>
