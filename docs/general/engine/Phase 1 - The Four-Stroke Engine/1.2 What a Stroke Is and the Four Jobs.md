The last file ended with a bang on demand: air and petrol mist trapped in the cylinder, lit, and the burning gas shoves the piston. Powerful - but a single bang is not an engine. A cylinder that has just burned its contents is full of burnt waste gas, and it has no fresh fuel, no fresh air, and no room prepared. To run continuously, the chamber must be refilled and emptied, over and over, thousands of times per minute. That scheduling job is what this file and the next two are about.

# 1.2 What a Stroke Is and the Four Jobs

## The word "stroke", defined once and forever

A **stroke** is one single-direction trip of the piston. Down, from top to bottom - that is one stroke. Up, from bottom to top - another stroke.

Two clarifications that prevent later confusion:

1. **Opening or closing a valve is not a stroke.** A valve switching is a quick event that happens at the boundary between strokes; only the piston's trips count.
2. The word applies to any piston machine, including the steam engine of Phase 0 - a stroke is just a trip, wherever it happens.

The piston has exactly two directions, so every engine's life is a sequence of down-strokes and up-strokes. The question that splits engine families apart is: **how many of these trips does one full "make a bang" process require?**

## The four jobs every engine must do

For a cylinder to produce one bang and be ready for the next, four things must happen:

1. **Pull the fresh mixture in.** Air and petrol mist must fill the cylinder.
2. **Squeeze it.** The mixture must be compressed into a small space before burning (why squeezing matters is the next file's business).
3. **Burn it and get pushed.** The mixture is lit, and the expanding gas delivers the push.
4. **Push the burnt gas out.** The waste must leave so the next cycle has room to breathe.

These four jobs are not optional and not reorderable: you cannot burn what you have not pulled in, and you cannot refill until the waste is gone. Every fuel-burning piston engine ever built does exactly these four jobs, every cycle, forever.

## The four-stroke answer: one job per trip

The design that assigned one job to each piston trip is the **four-stroke cycle**:

- Trip 1 (down): pull the mixture in.
- Trip 2 (up): squeeze it.
- Trip 3 (down): burn it and get pushed.
- Trip 4 (up): push the waste out.

Four trips, four jobs, one bang per completed loop. The loop then repeats: fill, squeeze, bang, empty - fill, squeeze, bang, empty.

The name is literal: the cycle takes four strokes. And since each stroke swings the crank throw through half a turn (the geometry from Phase 0), a full cycle takes **two full turns of the crankshaft**.

## The history, briefly

The four-stroke engine in the form that mattered was built by the German engineer **Nikolaus Otto in 1876**. This is worth pinning down, because the natural assumption - that someone first built a simple two-stroke and later refined it into four - is backwards. Otto's four-stroke came first, and the two-stroke arrived about a decade later, in the 1880s, as a deliberate simplification *of* the four-stroke. Phase 2 tells that story properly; for now, hold the direction: four-stroke first, two-stroke later.

Otto's design is the shape of virtually every petrol engine in every car on the road today. When a car brochure says "4-cylinder petrol engine", each of those cylinders is running this exact loop.

## The cost hiding in the plan

Look again at the four trips and notice the accounting problem: **only trip 3 makes power.** Trips 1, 2 and 4 are housekeeping - breathing in, squeezing, breathing out - and they consume effort rather than produce it. One bang has to pay for four piston trips and two full shaft turns.

This single fact drives three entire chapters of this course: why a heavy flywheel is bolted to every engine (Phase 4), why engines got more cylinders (Phase 5), and why the two-stroke design was invented at all (Phase 2). Keep the unfairness of 1-in-4 in mind; it is the motor of the whole story.

<details>
