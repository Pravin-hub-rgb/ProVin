Just like choosing a route in phone Maps after seeing your location: if there is no traffic you pick a direct road, and when congestion appears the goal changes too — taking another route becomes practical to reach the target. In chess too we choose a "route" for our pieces on the board, but we must recalculate after every opponent reply.

In `3.5 Quiz - Opening Fundamentals and Repertoire` you saw the opening as a repeatable starting setup. An opening that has gone beyond theory is not by itself a middlegame plan; now that setup has to be converted into a clear goal and practical moves.

# 4.1 How to Build a Middlegame Plan

After a few opening moves, when development, pawn structure, and king safety matter more, that part is the middlegame. It has no fixed move number — sometimes it starts after the 10th move and sometimes it still looks like simple theory-chasing until the 25th. The real sign is whether your setup is doing a practical job or not.

A **middlegame plan** means "I want this result in this position, so I am thinking these steps." A plan is not a fixed line; it is a reason and a direction. Just like planning a route home, on the board you can choose a reason like bringing your rook to an open file, your knight to a weak square, or your king near safety. Then after the opponent's move that reason can be adjusted a little.

## Safety first, then ambition

In the middlegame, checking king safety first is useful, because if an open line is open it can become an attack immediately. Whether pawns and pieces stand in front of the king, where the opponent's rooks and queen have a direct line, and whether a pattern like a fork attacks one piece from two different sides — these three questions come first. Playing a "creative" move after repeatedly checking the king often opens the position before it wins anything.

After that, look at material and pawns. Material means how many pieces you have on the board, while pawn structure tells which piece will stay strong and which weak in the future. Isolated pawns, doubled pawns, connected pawns, and passed pawns — these terms work only when you understand their simple reason; we will compare them in detail in the next topic. For now it is enough to choose your strongest concrete objective instead of the whole move list.

## Activity means doing work

**Activity** means your piece actively participates, not just sits safely parked. Let an active rook move along files and ranks to find new targets in the position; let a knight land on a center or outpost, a strong square; put pressure with a bishop on diagonals. An outpost is understood simply for now: a square far from the opponent's pawn coverage where your piece cannot be chased away late. When pieces are active, their moves create paths for each other's plans.

A practical scan can run like this: first king safety, then weak squares, then open files and passed-pawn chances, and finally whether you have a material advantage. After that you can think of two or three candidate goals and compare them with the opponent's likely reply. You do not need to find a "winning move" right now; just understand the position's direction. The 1000-rating target values this practical clarity, but no plan guarantees a rating — practice, calculation, and reviewing mistakes also affect the result.

## Look at a plan in three parts

### 1. Goal

Write the goal in one short sentence, such as "weaken the opponent's kingside" or "bring the rook to an open file." Do not make the goal big and vague — a good goal is measurable and connected to a square or piece on the board. "I will win" is not a goal because it does not say by which route.

### 2. Route

Route is the intermediate steps that lead to the goal. If the goal is bringing the rook to an open file, the route may include exchanging the opposing pawn, clearing the file, and creating an entry square for the rook. Check every move on the route with "does this give an option for the next move?"; do not mistake random activity for a plan.

### 3. Recheck

The opponent's move does not immediately cancel your plan, but it can change its direction. If the opponent has covered your weak square or brought a defensive pawn near the king, you can choose a new goal. This flexibility is not grand-master abstraction but practical common sense — when the position's evidence updates, the decision should update too.

## Seeing through one position

Now look at these three steps together on a sample position. If your rook is inactive, your pawns cover the king, and there is a gap in the opponent's queenside pawn structure, then "queenside expansion" can become a goal. In its route first comes an appropriate pawn move, then an open file and rook placement, then pressure on weak squares. If the opponent gives counterplay, choose an alternate plan like activating your king or simplifying.

This kind of checklist gives shape to a 20-second scan in every game. You do not try to solve everything each time; you choose one dominant, concrete reason for your position. For reaching a 1000 rating this practical clarity matters, but no plan guarantees the rating — practice, calculation, and mistake review also affect the result.

Now comparing these concepts at a glance is helpful, so each column answers only the question you ask during a position scan:

| Part of scan | Your question | Practical benefit |
|---|---|---|
| King safety | What direct attack exists right now? | Counterattack or safety before loss |
| Pawns | Which weak or passed pawn is there? | Clear reason for long-term pressure |
| Activity | Which piece can play right now? | Open files, outposts, and targets |
| Goal and route | Where do the next two moves lead? | Focus and faster decisions |

## What It Is NOT

A middlegame plan is **not "memorizing a theory line."** If you only remember moves from an opening book and do not see the reason after them on the board, your preparation has not reached a new position. A plan must be verified on the board after every move.

A plan is **not something to force.** If the opponent is blocking your route and a better defensive choice is available, adapting the plan is the work of maturity. Blindly grabbing material, putting the king in danger, or saying "I am attacking" is not strategy.

A plan is **not a rating guarantee.** It is a clear thinking framework for 45-minute daily practice; you can improve decisions by applying it in Lichess games, but every opponent and every position is different. This course's target is to structure progress, not to promise an outcome.

Now the framework of 4.1 gives you direction; in 4.2 `Pawn Breaks, Open Files aur Space` we will see how pawns are moved to change lines and activity.
