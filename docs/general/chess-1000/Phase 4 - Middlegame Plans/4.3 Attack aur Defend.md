You have sometimes finished only your own work in a group project and left the team stuck on someone else's task. Or when a member was chatting, the others stayed ready and actively supported them. In the same way, on the board an attack is strong when pieces work together toward one target. Defense is also active — it does not just wait, it creates reasons like an entry square, a weak piece, or a counterattack.

In `4.2 Pawn Breaks, Open Files aur Space` you saw structural changes: breaks open lines and targets, space gives pieces active squares, and an open file gives the rook direction. In 4.3 `Attack aur Defend` we now connect these changes to a goal, so you do not just say "attack" or "defense" but choose practical moves.

# 4.3 Attack and Defend

An **attack** means creating coordinated pressure in the opponent's position, where one or more pieces work on a target — king, weak pawn, weak square, trapped piece. Merely bringing a piece forward is not an attack; pressure becomes a plan when that piece's work fits with the other pieces and squares. To understand an attack, seeing target, entry, and support first is useful.

An attack can be more dangerous when the king is in the center, because fewer defenders stand in front of it. Open file, exposed king, and back rank are common conditions of an attack; a fork means one piece attacks two targets at once. You should not attack immediately on seeing one of these conditions empty. On the board you must check the whole chain: is the target vulnerable, will entry be available, and will the defender get time to react?

## The attack chain

The first element is the **target**. For a beginner it is not enough to just say "king"; later you must decide whether an easy target is a weak square around the king, the back rank, a loose pawn, or a trapped piece. The second element is **entry**, that is, from which square the attacker comes in. The third element is **support**, because capturing the supporting piece often makes it unsafe rather than impossible; the defender can be overloaded or pinned, or cannot recapture immediately.

Example: white rook pressure on the open c-file, knight and bishop supporting the target, and weak `c7` square — all of these together make an attack. The test of every move is whether the next attack step is being created, or whether a piece advanced on seeing a target and left its own cover. If pieces apply pressure separately, calling it "all pieces are attacking" is wrong.

## Defense is not only saving the king

**Defense** means making the opponent's attacking resources expensive and maintaining the integrity of your position. It can include covering a weak square, exchanging an attacking piece, blocking a critical file, finding a counterattack, or taking your pieces to safer squares. The strongest defensive move is one that slows the opponent's plan and also builds your practical plan.

Before saying "move the king" every time, ask: after the move did the king only go to a temporary square, or did permanent cover appear behind it? Because trying to push the king one square forward often brings the attack back on the same file. A better move is one that closes entry, exchanges, or forces the opponent onto their own weakness.

## Attack and defense are two paths of the same game

In one position you can both attack and keep a defensive resource. If your rook is putting pressure on a weak square but your knight and king are so exposed that the opponent's pawn break can attack you, insurance for safety is necessary along with the attack. Similarly, if you keep defensive pieces only for cover, the opponent can get free activity.

Understanding counterattack is also necessary, but do not turn it into an "always counterattack" formula. A counterattack is meaningful when it targets your weak piece, gives work to your extra pawns, or puts pressure on the opponent's king in their own position. Your defense does not completely cancel the attack; it takes the fight for control on the board to the next stage.

## Practical attack-defense checklist

To understand an attack on the board, remember this sequence: king safety, target vulnerability, entry square, supporting piece, and defender's escape squares. Then in defense look at: who owns the critical square, which piece can stop the attacker, and whether exchanging improves your position. This checklist is not line-by-line theory; it brings your attention to the concrete board in 20–30 seconds.

| Question | In attack | In defense |
|---|---|---|
| What is the target? | King, weak pawn, or trapped piece | Entry square, defender, or back rank |
| Is a line open? | Open file/diagonal with support | File block or cover move |
| Is there backup? | Second piece holds the target | Counterattacker or exchange |
| What changes? | Forces the weak square | Forces the opponent to replan |

## What It Is NOT

An attack is **not giving check on every move.** Check is a tool to execute a target and attacking plan; checking repeatedly can expose pieces and give the defender counterplay. Still, check can be necessary when it removes a covering piece, forces entry, or immediately creates a threat.

Defense is **not passively standing there.** If moving one square makes it vulnerable again two moves later, that is not delay but giving the opponent free tempo. Better defense permanently changes the position by supporting your pieces, blocking lines, exchanging, or counterattacking.

Attack and defense are **not a rating guarantee or a winning formula.** Material, king location, pawns, and piece placement decide together; so check every position fully. The idea of 4.3 is that instead of saying "the opponent is attacking my king," you can see which target, entry, and support the attack came from.

Now after attack and defense, in 4.4 `Simplify ya Pieces Badhaayein` we will see when increasing material is right and when exchanging extra pieces to make the position easier is better.
