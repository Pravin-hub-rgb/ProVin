When you change an ingredient in a recipe, the same instructions do not work every time; you adjust the next step after seeing taste and texture. Chess planning is the same — the opening setup is a recipe, but after every opponent reply the position's taste can change. So a goal should not be held as one line; it must be tested on the board.

In `4.4 Simplify ya Pieces Badhaayein` we learned how to compare extra material, active placement, and safe trade with each other. In 4.5 `Plan se Position tak` we now work on the final step of planning: how to convert a candidate goal into the actual position and how to update the plan after every opponent reply.

# 4.5 From Plan to Position

The last useful part of middlegame planning is understanding the **position** — that is, what has already been achieved on the current board and what is still only an imagined possibility. Your plan is a future sentence, while the position is current evidence. Comparing the two continuously is what turns a plan into calculation and real decision.

A candidate plan can be checked on the board in three layers. The first layer is the immediate move: is there a legal, purposeful move going toward the goal? The second layer is the opponent's response: after the opponent's best resistance, will your target, file, or king square still be available? The third layer is the end position: will your pieces reach there with actual pressure, material, and activity?

## Write your plan as a sentence

"Queenside expansion" is too broad. You can refine it and write, "I will create queenside space with the c-pawn, challenge the opposing d-pawn, then look for a file or weak square for the rook." In this sentence goal, pawn break, and the role of the final piece are connected. You do not need to write a new essay on every move; noting your goal's three moves or three intermediate steps is enough.

Then check whether that route is available in your current position. If the file only opens when you advance the king, calculate the plan's cost. If the pawn break makes the opponent's strong piece a target, compare it with attack and defense. This is where opening theory ends and actual planning begins.

## Rescan after the opponent's move

The opponent's move does not immediately reject your "correct" plan, but it gives new evidence. If they brought a rook or pawn to a defending square, the weak square's pressure has decreased; you can consider an alternate target or exchange. If they start a counterattack, you can choose a defensive resource to modify your plan, instead of staying stuck on the original opening line.

After every move ask three quick questions: is the king safe? Is the plan's next step still legal and useful? Did the opponent change my position's best target? If all three answers are "yes," your confidence grows; if even one is "no," re-evaluate. This process does not need an engine — it is a decision filter in 1000-level games, not a magic formula.

## Comparing position quality

Do not count only material when looking at a final position. Is the rook active or blocked? Does the king have cover or open squares? Is the weak pawn's defender nearby? Can your pieces defend each other mutually, or must each piece be saved separately? Comparing these practical questions tells you whether a move genuinely made your position better or just increased the move number.

You can use this in a 10-minute study routine. Choose one of your Lichess games, write your first middlegame goal, and note alternative routes with the opponent's responses for 10–15 moves. Using the engine for analysis is optional verification; the first job is to write your reasoning honestly. This lets you see recurring mistakes as patterns — such as ignoring the king every time you see an open file.

## An example: plan, interruption, replan

Suppose your plan is to create weak squares with queenside pawns. Imagine pressure on the c-file after `c5` for White, but the opponent changes the central structure with `...dxc5` and your queenside space decreases. Then it would be wrong to cancel the plan and blindly follow every move; you can continue the attack, simplify, or build a kingside counter-plan.

Replanning does not mean finding a new opening. It means seeing the reason behind the goal that is still valid on the board, then choosing the best route for that reason. This adaptability is valuable for the 1000-rating target, but changing direction repeatedly in one game is also aimless; every switch must have a clear reason.

This process can be seen as a compact decision loop:

| Decision stage | Your question | If the answer is clear |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence | What is protected and weak on the board? | Base read of the position |
| Direction | What is my goal? | Focus of the plan |
| Test | Does the route survive after the best reply? | Practical candidate |
| Decision | What happens to king, material, activity after the move? | One move |
| Review | What new board evidence is there? | Replan or continue |

## What It Is NOT

The work from plan to position is **not a replacement for the engine.** You can compare candidate moves with shallow calculation and basic tactics, but calculation depth still matters in complex positions. Strategy should not replace calculation; both must be used together.

Replanning is **not random mood swings.** Every direction change must come from a weak square, counterattack, piece placement, or material change. Dropping the plan just because you saw an "interesting" move is not flexibility; it is losing focus.

A "winning plan" label is **not a guarantee either.** Tactics, time, and the opponent's choices can change in Lichess games; so evaluate a plan with probability and safety. In the 45-minute daily schedule your goal is one measurable improvement in every game, such as more purposeful moves or fewer king mistakes — not a fixed rating promise.
