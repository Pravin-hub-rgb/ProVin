When a trainer makes a fitness plan, they do not just say "go to the gym every day." They decide weekly targets, recovery, and measurement, so you see results and can adjust the next week's plan. Chess improvement works the same way: connect your 45-minute schedule to measurable actions and honest review, not just motivation.

The feedback from `6.6 Quiz - Analysis, Clock and Tournament Improvement` gave you an honest snapshot of your Phase 6 tools. Now we will arrange those tools into a 10-week personal improvement plan, which also includes a 10-game training block and after it a 10-game capstone.

# 7.1 Personal Improvement Plan

A **personal improvement plan** means a schedule, metrics, and feedback loop written for you. It is not just a wish of "I will become 1000"; it also says what you will do in a 45-minute session, which game you will review, and which result is genuinely evidence of improvement for you.

Set the target directly as "around 1000 in Lichess rapid 10+0," but do not treat this target as a guarantee. Rating changes with opponents, pool, form, time pressure, and luck. For this course, view 20 comparable rated games as a **course checkpoint**; this is not a scientifically validated minimum or a definition of a stable rating. In this checkpoint, competent decisions with around-1000 snapshots, sensible results, and similar mistakes can be evidence of your honest readiness, not a lucky peak.

## First set the baseline

View your current level as a baseline. Look at notes from your last 10 rated 10+0 games, or if you do not have that many, record the next 10 games as a baseline block. In every game note the rating snapshot, result, time-trouble status, your dominant error, and review completion.

The baseline's purpose is not to give you a final number. Its job is to see in which phase your time runs out, which error repeats, and how long you do a complete written review. Just as a doctor looks at baseline habits, not just weight, comparison's starting point is also habits.

**Stable performance** does not mean a temporary rating jump in one game. In this course 20 comparable rated games are a rolling checkpoint sample, not a scientifically validated minimum or fixed definition. Around-1000 snapshots, sensible results, and competent decisions with similar mistakes across multiple comparable games let you form a practical, provisional judgment of your preparation.

## 10-week structure

Think of the plan in three parts: foundation, live integration, and final consistency check. Every week's output will be an observable product, such as reviewed games, a filled log, or a tactical checkpoint, so the plan does not feel complete just by reading pages.

| Week | Roadmap phase | Completion evidence |
|---|---|---|
| W1 | Phase 0 — baseline, plan, study system | Attempt 6 baseline positions and start a 7-day log |
| W2–4 | Phase 1 — board scan, tactics, exercise pack | Scan for forcing moves on available Phase 1 positions, identify patterns, and score 4+ correct in the final 6-position pack |
| W5 | Phase 2 — calculation and position choice | Calculate and compare 3-move lines |
| W6 | Phase 3 — opening fundamentals and repertoire | Choose 2–3 simple opening principles |
| W7 | Phase 4 — middlegame plans | Notes from 10 games reveal one common plan gap |
| W8 | Phase 5 — essential endgames | Study basic king-and-pawn endings and run conversion drills |
| W9 | Phase 6 — analysis, clock, tournament improvement | Play 5 games under time pressure and review them |
| W10 | Phase 7 — 1000 rating project | 20 comparable rated checkpoint games, 30 total logs, final review, next 4-week plan |

Use the table like a timetable, not like a punishment. If an extra loss comes in some week, focus on the next week's checkpoint and quietly make the plan realistic. Do not measure your progress only by weekly rating change.

## Daily 45-minute template

You can keep your 45-minute session in flexible blocks. A practical starting point is 10 minutes recall, 20 minutes practice, 10 minutes review, and 5 minutes logging; if there is a tournament, some of this time can go to an actual game.

In the recall phase, revisit old error triggers and one tactical theme. In the practice phase, use puzzles, opening exploration, endgames, or Lichess games, but note one purpose for every activity. In the review phase, write your move before the engine, and in the last five minutes choose the next assignment.

You will not set a daily rating target. Daily target evidence is: solving one question, reviewing one game, or counting one trigger. The network of these small completions builds repeatable improvement over 10 weeks.

## Track metrics fairly

Keep **no more than five** fields per game: rating snapshot, result, time-trouble flag, primary lesson, and review done or not. Along with these, note a weekly `accuracy of review`, where you write how long it took to find the decisive moment and whether you checked the alternative on the board yourself.

Keep rating in a separate column and keep overall progress fair. Do not look at one block's score, opponent pool, time control, and rating change together. The purpose of metrics is not to compare every day, but to see a realistic pattern over several weeks.

Write one line every week: "My most common mistake this week was ___, next week I will check the ___ trigger." When you read 10 weeks of notes together, you will see which pattern is truly repeating and which was just one game's story.

## Match the plan to your schedule

If you do not have a fixed 45-minute session, adjust the plan into an honest realistic version. A short consistent session can be better than a long interrupted "intensive" session, because the review loop completes. You can note missing time every week and adjust the next assignment.

Do not make rating your daily identity. Your plan measures board decisions, time routines, and review completion; rating is its downstream outcome. This distinction will protect you from breaking the plan after every loss and from overcelebrating every win.

## Next week's work

In the next seven days, start your 10-game baseline log. After every game, in 10–15 minutes write result, time-trouble status, primary error, and next cue; do the deep review with the engine on the third day or within the same routine. Keeping more than five fields is also not practical; keep simplicity.

At the end of the week, do a 45-minute review: which field showed a pattern, when the trigger fired, and what the observable task for next week is. This will become the starting material for `7.2 Ten-Game Training Block`.

## What It Is NOT

The personal plan is **not a rating guarantee.** 1000 is a useful target, but you do not completely control results; the plan improves your decisions, not guarantees an outcome.

The personal plan is **not an excuse to play rated 10+0 every day.** Some days are for review and practice, and these are necessary for progress. Writing a purpose before every game protects consistency from random queueing.

The personal plan is **not just chasing one number.** Taking one lucky peak rating as stability would be wrong; multiple comparable games, decision traces, and recurring-error change are more reliable evidence.
