You do not pass a driving test with one lucky parking maneuver. In the final test you show rules, vehicle control, and examiner instructions together, so every turn is evaluated. Now after `7.2 Ten-Game Training Block` you are giving that final-style evidence: ten more realistic rated games, with review, and one honest readiness decision.

# 7.3 Ten-Game Capstone

A **capstone** means using course tools in one final, integrated assignment. In this project you will play serial 10 comparable rated Lichess rapid 10+0 games, record every game's quality and metrics, and at the end make a provisional readiness decision through written review on whether you want to continue around the 1000 target or need another block.

We will make the capstone practical, not stressful like a "last exam." Games will be realistic, selection fair, and you will handle losses as review input. Do not delete any result to cherry-pick the sample; the complete record is the course checkpoint's evidence.

## Capstone pre-commitment

Before starting, note numbers and realistic dates for 10 games. Keep the same fields for every game: date, rating snapshot, opponent rating, result, time-trouble flag, primary error, written review, and next cue. You can also keep a column "what I practised," so the source of improvement is traceable.

If you play in a tournament, select a suitable rated event as checked in `6.5 Tournament Routine and Recovery`. If you play an online queue, choose a practical time control and result context. Avoid opponent/result-pool gaming; the capstone should be a fair training sample, not rating farming.

## Five-step protocol for every game

Before starting a game, write one primary focus and one clock cue. Focus can be tactical clarity, king safety, piece activity, conversion, or time management. Then make realistic decisions during the game and do not try to write live commentary on every move; let board attention and clock routine be tested.

After the game, write your move and decisive moment, then open the engine. You will repeat the `7.1` and `7.2` workflow: write your move before checking the engine, compare alternatives, find the decisive moment, classify the lesson, and turn it into a small next-week assignment. One capstone game's output is not only the result, but this complete review evidence.

Record recovery after every game too. Evidence of a water break, short walk, and next-game checklist helps explain your sustained routine. After every loss keep one actionable lesson and after every win one realistic next focus.

## Written review of the capstone

After every game, write one paragraph: situation, your move, missed alternative, primary error category, and next cue. After five games, in a midpoint summary count how many games had time trouble, how many decisions were reviewed, and which trigger repeated most. You will not secretly reset the block; missing reviews will also be noted.

At the end of the block, look at the 20 comparable rated games course checkpoint sample. Training block's 10 plus capstone's 10 comparable rated games are part of the course checkpoint, but this 20-game sample is not a scientifically validated minimum. Your readiness decision will remain provisional on these games and written reviews. If some games were unrated, do not add them to the rated sample of the course checkpoint.

## Practical checkpoint for 1000 readiness

Success is not treated as one lucky rating peak here. For this course 20 comparable rated 10+0 games are a **course checkpoint**, not a scientifically validated minimum. In that sample you can see a pattern of around-1000 snapshots, practical tactics, time management, and review quality. "Stable" does not mean every game is identical; these 20 games are not a fixed definition of a stable rating, but a rolling evidence sample.

The 1000 target is honestly useful, but **rating cannot be guaranteed** by any plan, engine, tournament, or course. Opponent strength, time controls, pool composition, form, and random factors affect the result. The capstone's benefit is that you build evidence of your preparation, not a false promise of controlling the outcome.

## Three options for the readiness decision

After the block you can choose one of these three honest labels:

- **Continue around 1000:** The practical pattern in the 20-game course checkpoint is encouraging, common errors are manageable, and the review routine can continue; this decision is provisional.
- **Repeat the 10-game block:** Stability is not there yet, time-trouble rate is high, or written reviews are not regular; then repeat the targeted block.
- **Slow down and rebuild:** Foundational gaps in tactics, king safety, or endgames; adjust the 45-minute plan with smaller fundamentals and review goals.

Justify the decision not by a rating number but by written review and future assignment. If the data is mixed, "repeat" is an honest and useful answer. This does not mean failure, but making the next experiment clearer.

## What It Is NOT

The capstone is **not a guaranteed rating event.** Its benefit is a fair, comparable sample and decision trace; no result can promise 1000 to you.

The capstone is **not a presentation showing only best games.** Losses, time-trouble games, and incomplete reviews should also stay honest in the dataset. Cherry-picking makes self-assessment look better, but does not improve decision quality.

The capstone does **not measure rating as a personality.** You can learn planning skill from your 20-game course checkpoint sample, but defining worth or ability by one number is not healthy. Use the data for next action.
