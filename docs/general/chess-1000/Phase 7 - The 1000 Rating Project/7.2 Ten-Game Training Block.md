# 7.2 Ten-Game Training Block

A **ten-game training block** is the first measured integration test of your plan. In it you will play serial 10 games, ideally comparable Lichess rapid 10+0 games, and after every game write your move, decisive moment, and one lesson. The purpose of every game is not only rating or result, but testing plan tools in real decisions.

If rated games are available in your tournament pool, record these games as rated 10+0. If temporary constraints make games unrated, label them honestly; an unrated sample will not be used as rated readiness evidence. This block's output is data and review discipline, not guaranteed points.

## Write the commitment before the block

Write a small commitment note: "I will play ___ realistic rated 10+0 games in the next ___ days/weeks, and fill minimum review fields after every game." Writing date, tournament, or pairings makes your plan not flexible, but accountable and practical.

Keep the same baseline fields for every game: game number, rating snapshot, opponent rating, result, time-trouble flag, primary error, and review status. This way you do not see every game as a separate anecdote, but as a dataset. Even a small dataset works if it is honest.

## One game's training loop

Before the game, write your phase goal: development, tactical awareness, king safety, or endgame conversion. Do not target improving everything in one game; choose one primary cue and one secondary reminder. This keeps your attention practical and measurable.

After the game, first check the scoreboard result, then update your review log. For every game choose at least one important position where your move could have meaningfully changed the game. In that position write your move and FEN, then open the engine and compare best continuation, alternative, and the opponent's forcing reply.

You will not annotate all 20 moves of every game. Deep review should focus on one decisive moment, one alternative, and one prevention cue. Just as one technique is focused in one practice rep, one learning target should be in one game review.

After five games, do a small midpoint review. You can choose two or three recurring triggers and update cues for the remaining games. Adapting the plan is not cheating; modifying against data is honest training.

## Short format for written review

Use this template for every reviewed game:

- **Decisive moment:** Position's FEN and what changed.
- **My move:** Main choice and visible reason.
- **Better plan:** Engine alternative or opponent's forcing reply.
- **Lesson to error list:** Observable cue for next games.

If you are only writing "position got bad," the review is not complete yet. You need one visible decision on the board, such as "did not count squares near the king" or "did not see central activity after knight capture." Specific words can become triggers in future games.

## How to read metrics

At the end of the block, look at five simple metrics: total reviewed games, time-trouble games, most common error category, range of rating snapshots, and how many assignments carry forward to next week. Write a short explanation with every metric, not just the number.

Read result and rating with context. In one block, opponent pool, tournament duration, and sleep can vary. So do not take one single high rating as "I am ready"; repeated games and written review are more meaningful signals.

## 45-minute schedule

A plan to play one game a day can be realistic, but keep time to consume game review too. You can use 15 minutes pre-game warm-up and goal, 15 minutes game buffer, and 15 minutes initial review. Use remaining time for heavy study or capstone preparation.

If you play in a tournament, run the recovery routine according to `6.5 Tournament Routine and Recovery`. Skipping water, break, and next-game checklist to only chase score will make the block's data noisy. A sustainable routine is one that survives until the end of 10 games.

## What It Is NOT

The ten-game block is **not just playing ten games.** Without review this block produces only volume, not learning. Minimum evidence is one focused position and one lesson after every game.

The ten-game block is **not a controlled experiment in rating** where you can predict every result. Opponent pool and luck influence; you can improve your decision process and collect evidence, not guarantee an outcome.

The ten-game block is **not permission to complete all games by finding easy opponents.** Use realistic rated events and fair selection, treat the opponent as a resource, and avoid result-pool gaming. Keeping difficulty in a useful range is real training.

Now you have data from ten games and the first complete set of recurring lessons. In `7.3 Ten-Game Capstone` you will use this discipline in a final ten-game capstone, then make a provisional readiness decision on the 20 comparable rated games course checkpoint.
