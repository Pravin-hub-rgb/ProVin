# 0.2 Baseline Test

## What baseline means

Baseline means where you stand right now — not an ideal position, and not a test result that tries to prove something about you. It is a snapshot to compare against future practice. So note your attempt on each position first, then open the answer.

This test does not prove anyone weak or strong in chess. Its job is to identify which area currently needs extra attention among checking, capturing, threat creation, and simple checkmate. Just as a doctor does not give a final diagnosis from symptoms alone, a baseline is not your final future rating.

Each board comes with a FEN. **FEN** stands for Forsyth–Edwards Notation, a compact chess-position notation that records pieces, side to move, and some game-state information. You do not need to parse it; in this course use FEN as the exact text record of a board.

## How to take the test

Give each position 2–3 minutes, write the move, and write one reason before opening the answer. "Why did I choose it?" as one line is very important, because learning the pattern of a correct move transfers better than learning the pattern of a wrong move. Open the engine or solution only after the attempt is complete. The sequence starts with simple development and moves toward checks, captures, and back-rank/checkmate decisions.

The boards below are White-perspective: White looks from the bottom side, a1 is the bottom-left, and side to move is clearly written in both the diagram and the FEN. Each exercise has its answer hidden in an accordion below it, so solving first is necessary.

## Position 1 — a practical development move

This is an opening-style position where the task is not tactical calculation but identifying one purposeful move. Your goal is not just to play any legal move; you must explain the move for central control and king safety.

![Phase 0 baseline position 01](/diagrams/chess/phase-0/phase-0-baseline-01.svg)

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `rnbqkbnr/pppp1ppp/8/4p3/4P3/8/PPPP1PPP/RNBQKBNR w KQkq - 0 2`  
**Time limit/task:** 3 minutes. Choose one strong developing move that helps prepare the center and kingside castle, then write its purpose.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and review</summary>

**Best move:** `Nf3`

**Short main line:** `1. Nf3` is enough for this diagnostic; the important point is the purpose, not a long opening theory line.

**Why it works:** The knight leaves the back rank, develops toward the center, and makes kingside castling easier to prepare. It is a practical choice even though several legal developing moves could look attractive.

**Common mistake:** Choosing a pawn move first and then moving the same knight immediately, which spends two tempi. "Tempi" means a useful move that gains a turn without giving the opponent a free reply.

</details>

## Position 2 — check and mate awareness

Now the board is sparse, so the lines around the queen and king are clear. You need to find the forcing move first, then check the opponent's escape squares.

![Phase 0 baseline position 02](/diagrams/chess/phase-0/phase-0-baseline-02.svg)

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `6kr/5ppp/8/3Q4/8/8/8/4K2R w - - 0 1`  
**Time limit/task:** 3 minutes. Find White's strongest checking move, and state whether Black has a legal answer.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and review</summary>

**Best move:** `Qd8#`

**Short main line:** `1. Qd8#` ends the game immediately.

**Why it works:** The queen reaches the eighth rank and checks the king on g8 along the row. Black's own rook on h8 and pawns on f7, g7, and h7 block the king's escape squares, while the check cannot be blocked safely.

**Common mistake:** Playing a safe-looking queen move and forgetting to count the opponent's own pieces as walls. Back-rank ideas often work because the king has fewer safe squares than it first appears.

</details>

## Position 3 — capture before comfort

Here the queen looks visibly exposed, and White has a knight jump option. Your task is not comfortable development, but comparing an immediate material and king-safety gain.

![Phase 0 baseline position 03](/diagrams/chess/phase-0/phase-0-baseline-03.svg)

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `6kr/5q2/8/4N3/8/8/8/4KR2 w - - 0 1`  
**Time limit/task:** 3 minutes. Find the knight move that wins material, and check whether the destination is protected.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and review</summary>

**Best move:** `Nxf7`

**Short main line:** `1. Nxf7` wins the queen; the rook on f1 protects the knight on f7.

**Why it works:** The knight attacks f7 from e5 and captures the black queen. Because the white rook controls f7, Black cannot answer by simply capturing the knight with the king, so the gain is decisive.

**Common mistake:** Seeing the capture but not checking the recapture square. A capture is useful only if the opponent cannot immediately win the capturing piece back.

</details>

## Position 4 — back-rank review

Here Black's king is surrounded by its own pawns, and White's rook has a clear eighth-rank line. First look at the rook's route, then count the king's escape squares.

![Phase 0 baseline position 04](/diagrams/chess/phase-0/phase-0-baseline-04.svg)

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `6k1/5ppp/8/8/8/8/8/R5K1 w - - 0 1`  
**Time limit/task:** 3 minutes. Find a rook move that uses Black's own pawns as a wall.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and review</summary>

**Best move:** `Ra8#`

**Short main line:** `1. Ra8#` checks along the eighth rank and has no legal response.

**Why it works:** The rook travels up the open a-file to a8 and attacks the king on g8. The pawns on f7, g7, and h7 stop the king from stepping off the back rank, so the attack is mate.

**Common mistake:** Looking only at empty squares around the king. In real games a king can be trapped by its own pieces, which is exactly what makes a back-rank attack possible.

</details>

## Position 5 — answer a check

Here side to move is Black and the White queen is giving check. In this exercise the best move means resolving the check immediately and identifying your best legal hope at once.

![Phase 0 baseline position 05](/diagrams/chess/phase-0/phase-0-baseline-05.svg)

**Side to move:** Black  
**FEN:** `7k/r5Q1/5K2/8/8/8/8/8 b - - 0 1`  
**Time limit/task:** 3 minutes. Find the forced response, and verify which pieces can legally reach the checking queen.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and review</summary>

**Best move:** `...Rxg7`

**Short main line:** `...Rxg7` captures the checking queen along the seventh rank.

**Why it works:** The rook travels across the open rank and removes the check. The king cannot capture on g7 because the white king on f6 protects that square.

**Common mistake:** Trying `...Kxg7` and ignoring the protecting white king. A checking move is not automatically safe; after every capture, the opponent's protection must be checked.

</details>

## Position 6 — finish the simple mate

This position is deliberately sparse so you can clearly see the three parts of mate: what gives check, what blocks escape squares, and what protects the queen after a capture. After writing the move, mentally check every king square.

![Phase 0 baseline position 06](/diagrams/chess/phase-0/phase-0-baseline-06.svg)

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `k7/8/1QK5/8/8/8/8/8 w - - 0 1`  
**Time limit/task:** 3 minutes. Find a queen move that gives checkmate in one.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and review</summary>

**Best move:** `Qb7#`

**Short main line:** `1. Qb7#` ends the game.

**Why it works:** The queen on b7 checks the king on a8 and covers the escape squares a7 and b8. Black cannot capture on b7 because the white king on c6 protects the queen.

**Common mistake:** Calling any queen check a mate without checking the king's other squares. Mate requires that every king escape, block, and capture has been answered.

</details>

## Phase readiness checkpoint — baseline quick read

Now that all positions are attempted, give each attempt 0, 1, or 2 points: 0 means I did not see the move, 1 means the move was wrong but I understood the reason later, and 2 means both move and reason were there. This score is not a judgment; it only shows which family of drills deserves time next.

| Score | Reading | Next action |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–4 | Gap in safety, capture, or check recognition | Do the board-scan warm-up daily and revisit 3 positions of the weak pattern |
| 5–8 | Expected mixed baseline for a beginner | Keep the 0.3 routine and note one repeated mistake per game |
| 9–12 | Good start in tactical awareness | Identify patterns faster in Phase 1 and increase speed in practical games |

If there is basic uncertainty about legal movement or king safety in any position, you can revisit piece-movement basics right here; this course will not repeat those beginner lessons. After that, still reinforce these baseline positions' attempts and Phase 0 quiz concepts through notes and practice, because a baseline is useful only when you compare decisions with measurable habits.

## What It Is NOT

The baseline is not a pass-fail exam that proves you "good" or "bad" at chess. It is only a starting snapshot, so the score should not be read as a prediction of future rating.

Opening the engine's answer before attempting is not a baseline either. If the answer is already known, it is a memory test, not pattern recognition; that is exactly why we hide the answer at the end of each position.
