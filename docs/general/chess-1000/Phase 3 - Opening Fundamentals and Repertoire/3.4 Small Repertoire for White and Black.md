When `3.3 Pawn Structures aur Traps` taught you to notice pawn shape and early tactical patterns, now those observations need to be organized for your actual games. This does not mean memorizing many openings; it means keeping a small, understandable selection you can confidently open in 10+0.

Just like not carrying everything in your daily bag — phone, wallet, keys, and a snack are enough — in chess you do not need to carry every possible opening. One first move for your side, a simple plan against the opponent's common reply, and one fallback are enough; handle the rest with principles.

# 3.4 Small Repertoire for White and Black

**Repertoire** is your set of selected opening choices. A small repertoire helps both memory and decision speed: when the opponent plays a familiar reply, you play according to plan instead of theory. This is practical for 10+0, but it does not mean you will guarantee a win against every opponent.

## Selection rules for a small repertoire

Before choosing an opening, ask four practical questions: can I understand its basic plan at once, are common replies recognizable on the board, do pieces develop with my safety, and can I revisit it in a 10–15 minute study session? Engine result, GM name, or fashionable trend alone are not reasons.

A reliable repertoire keeps few moves but knows them with flexibility. This does not mean copying a fixed engine line; against an unexpected opponent move, return to principles of development, center, king safety, and structure. `3.2`'s three filters become that safety net.

## A small starter choice for White

My default recommendation is **White: `1. e4` and the Italian family**. Understand the first few moves like this: `1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4`, then purpose-driven moves like `4. d3` or `4. O-O`. Note the goal on the board: pieces become active, White influences the center, and the king consolidates away from early pressure.

If `1. d4` feels more comfortable, you can make `1. d4 d5 2. c4` your White primary. Its plan is the same: take center and development together; you do not need to memorize Queen's Gambit deep theory now. Just choose one White primary this week; do not open two options in parallel.

## A small starter choice for Black

My default recommendation is **Black: `1...e5` against `1. e4`**. In the basic replies keep `2...Nf6`, `2...Bc5`, and after `2...Nc6 3. Bb5` keep `3...a6`. Write a reason after every move: the knight develops, the bishop comes to an active square, and the king gets a safer setup for future castling.

If `1...d5` against `1. d4` feels more familiar, you can make that your Black primary. For it, plan development of knights and bishops and early king safety; you do not need to memorize book lines of every variant. This choice is valid according to your preference and practice comfort.

## Choose exactly one per colour

The final selection rule is simple: one White primary and one Black primary, then one notebook page with their common replies. I suggest `1. e4` for White and `1...e5` for Black because simple goals are visible for 10+0; you can choose an alternative, but you must be able to explain the choice.

Write these four lines on each card: "Why do I play this first move?", "What is the opponent's common reply?", "What is the plan for the next two moves?", "Which principle will I use on an unexpected move?" This way the repertoire is not memorized but rehearsed. Revisit the card 10–15 minutes each week, then consciously follow the same plan in one Lichess game.

## What to memorize, what not

What to memorize: the starting move, one or two common replies, and the plan after them. Deep side lines, engine variations, and every possible trick are optional for now. First understand each move's board-level reason and safety consequence; if you later want to expand the repertoire, add one additional line, not a whole library.

## What It Is NOT

A **small repertoire** does not mean blindly repeating one opening. It means keeping limited, well-understood choices and switching to principles afterward.

An **opening choice** is not a guarantee. No line will win every game; in 10+0 familiarity improves decision time and confidence, but the result still depends on the opponent and execution.
