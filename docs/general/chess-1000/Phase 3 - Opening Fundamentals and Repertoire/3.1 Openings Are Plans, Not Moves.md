In the opening too you do not need to calculate every move; the first job is to understand the board's plan, so your chosen moves stay compatible with each other.

Just like choosing a destination before using GPS in a new city, in a chess opening first think about where you want to go: attack quickly by controlling the center, play calmly by developing pieces, or counterattack on the opposite side after castling. Without a destination every move feels like a separate option; with a plan the moves do one practical job together.

# 3.1 Openings Are Plans, Not Moves

An **opening** is the initial sequence in which you prepare your pieces for the battlefield, build control of the center, and keep the king away from early danger. An **opening plan** is a practical purpose that gives direction to your next two moves, that is your next two decisions. **Repertoire** simply means a selected set of opening choices ready for you.

Memorizing an opening and understanding it are different. A memorizing player copies every move after `1. e4` like a fixed sequence; an understanding player sees what changed in center, development, king safety, or space from that move. In 10+0 a practical, repeatable plan matters more than a stack of fixed theory.

## Three immediate jobs of the opening

The first job is **development**: bringing knights and bishops to squares where they can work actively, and supporting the rook after the opening. The second job is **center control**: building control over central files and ranks; the center does not need a pawn standing on it, influencing it is also useful. The third job is **king safety**: keeping the king away from early attack, so castling or an equivalent safe setup comes early.

It is not always possible to achieve all three in one move. So whenever a move appears, ask yourself: "Does this move improve development, center, king safety, or future flexibility?" If the answer is one clear job, the move's practical purpose is clear.

## Write a plan sentence

Before memorizing an opening, write a **plan sentence**. It should contain your immediate goal, the role of your next two moves, and a basic handle on the opponent's likely response. Here "next two moves" means your next two decisions, not one White move and one Black move. An example sentence could be: "White plays `1. e4`, then `2. Nf3`, then `3. Bc4`, and after development castles kingside; Black can challenge the center."

A plan sentence is not a fixed truth. If the opponent does not play `2...Nc6`, your bishop route may change, as long as the larger goals of development and king safety stay. This flexibility comes before deep opening theory.

## 45-minute routine

Do not turn opening study into a cram session. Set up one opening position for 10 minutes, write two possible plans for 15 minutes, test moves on the Lichess analysis board for 10 minutes, and write one "if opponent does this, I do that" line in your notes for 10 minutes. The rating target is a direction, not a guarantee; repeatable study and real games both give feedback.

## What It Is NOT

An **opening plan** does not mean an exact move sequence or a book line for every opponent move. A plan guides decisions that match the position's goals.

**Repertoire** does not mean "memorize all famous openings." A small repertoire means understanding fewer options, practicing a few clear responses, and returning to principles in the rest of the situations.

## Checkpoint: your opening sentence

Right now choose one candidate opening family for White and one for Black, but do not try to memorize full theory. Write one sentence for each color: "I want to achieve this goal, this opening move is the next step, and after the likely response I will maintain which value among development, center, king safety, or future flexibility?" Read that sentence again; if you cannot remember the exact reply to every opponent move, you are memorizing a sequence rather than a plan.
