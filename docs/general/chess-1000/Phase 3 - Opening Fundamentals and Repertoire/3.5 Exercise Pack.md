# 3.5 Exercise Pack

These six positions cover different stages of the opening: first opening-plan choice, castling, a standard response, a pawn-structure decision, a defensive development move, and an early trap. Each answer has move, short line, reason, and common mistake — so the pattern becomes a practical decision, not just a theory sentence.

## Position 1 — First opening-plan move

This is a starting position, so no tactical calculation is needed. White must choose a first opening-plan move and show its reason on the board: how center claim or development will support the future king-safety setup. Before the move ask which knight is still left undeveloped.

![Chess position 1: first opening-plan move](/diagrams/chess/phase-3/phase-3-opening-01.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `rnbqkbnr/pppppppp/8/8/8/8/PPPPPPPP/RNBQKBNR w KQkq - 0 1`

**Time limit / task:** 45 seconds. Think of simple options like `1. e4` and `1. Nf3`, choose one primary White move, and write its development and center reason.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `1. e4!`

**Short line:** `1. e4 e5 2. Nf3`

**Reasoning:** White plays `e4` for center influence, and after Black's common reply the knight comes to an active square with `Nf3`. `1. Nf3` can also be a reasonable starter, but in the recommended `1. e4` plan the goal is to build center and development together. This position's goal is not just pushing a pawn but building a future plan.

**Common mistake:** Treating `e4` as the "best opening move" and ignoring development and king safety afterward. The first move's reason must connect to your next two opening moves.

</details>

## Position 2 — Castle before danger

This position comes after `1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4`. White has development options, but the king is still in the center and Black's future attack time can grow. Think of castling not just as notation, but as the king's safe future square and rook coordination.

![Chess position 2: castle before danger](/diagrams/chess/phase-3/phase-3-opening-02.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `r1bqkbnr/pppp1ppp/2n5/4p3/2B5/5N2/PPPP1PPP/RNBQK2R w KQkq - 3 4`

**Time limit / task:** 90 seconds. Compare candidates like `O-O`, `d3`, and `Nc3`; choose one move on the basis of king safety and future activity.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `4. O-O!`

**Short line:** `4. O-O Nf6`

**Reasoning:** The bishop on c4 and knight on f3 are already developed. White still has castling right with king on e1 and rook on h1, the f1/g1 squares between them are empty, the king on e1 is not in check, and e1, f1, and g1 are not attacked squares. So `O-O` is legal and consolidates the king early off the board. After castling the rook on f1 can cooperate with center activity and king safety on g1. `d3` is also a structural move, but for the current king-safety goal castling is clearer.

**Common mistake:** Pushing a central pawn on every move and delaying `O-O` too long for the king. King safety is not a one-time checkbox but an ongoing part of the opening loop.

</details>

## Position 3 — Practical response to 3. Bb5

This position comes after the first move of the Ruy Lopez: `1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5`. Black does not need to open a grand theory book now; with a simple pawn response the bishop can be kept active on `Ba4`. The move's reason is future activity and safe development more than capture.

![Chess position 3: Ruy Lopez pawn response](/diagrams/chess/phase-3/phase-3-opening-03.svg)

**Side to move:** Black

**FEN:** `r1bqkbnr/pppp1ppp/2n5/1B2p3/4P3/5N2/PPPP1PPP/RNBQK2R b KQkq - 3 3`

**Time limit / task:** 90 seconds. Compare the immediate board purposes of `...a6`, `...Nf6`, and `...d6`; choose one practical response and one reason.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `3...a6`

**Short line:** `3...a6 4. Ba4 Nf6`

**Reasoning:** `...a6` gives a practical option to make the bishop retreat to `Ba4`, where it stays on an active diagonal. At the same time the black pawn can capture the bishop with `...axb5`, so White must move the bishop soon. After the line Black can also develop with `...Nf6`; full Ruy Lopez theory is not needed.

**Common mistake:** Copying `...a6` only as a "Ruy Lopez response" label without thinking about the bishop retreat or future knight development. A move is understood when its next purpose is visible on the board.

</details>

## Position 4 — Develop with the structure in mind

This position comes after `1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6`. White has open-ish central choices and developed knights; the task is to choose a rapid opening principle, not a deep engine line. After looking at a candidate, ask whether it improves piece activity and what Black's next center challenge will do in the future.

![Chess position 4: develop after the central exchange](/diagrams/chess/phase-3/phase-3-opening-04.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `rnbqkb1r/pp2pppp/3p1n2/8/3N4/2N5/PP3PPP/R1BQKB1R w KQkq - 0 6`

**Time limit / task:** 2 minutes. Compare `Bc4`, `Be3`, and any simple knight move; note one development plan and one common consequence of structure.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `6. Bc4!`

**Short line:** `6. Bc4 e5`

**Reasoning:** The bishop from f1 comes to an active diagonal on c4 and gets future activity near the f7 target. `...e5` can be Black's central challenge, but White first sent an undeveloped bishop to a purposeful square; this shows the pieces' work on the board. `Be3` can also be a solid choice, but `Bc4` gives a simple plan of central aiming and development.

**Common mistake:** Choosing a central break like `d5` on every move while a bishop is still on the bench. Sending an undeveloped piece to an active square can itself be a strong structural plan.

</details>

## Position 5 — Develop a defender, not a panic move

This position comes after `1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6`. Black must settle an undeveloped bishop instead of chasing an aggressive attack; `...Be7` is a simple, repeatable principle-based move. In this exercise check the order of defensive development and king safety, not deep Queen's Indian theory.

![Chess position 5: defensive development](/diagrams/chess/phase-3/phase-3-opening-05.svg)

**Side to move:** Black

**FEN:** `rnbqkb1r/pp3ppp/4pn2/8/2PP4/2N5/PP2PPPP/R1B1KB1R b KQkq - 2 3`

**Time limit / task:** 90 seconds. Compare the short purposes of `...Be7`, `...dxc4`, and `...O-O`; choose one defensive development move.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `3...Be7!`

**Short line:** `3...Be7 4. Bg5 O-O`

**Reasoning:** The bishop from f8 settles on e7 as an undeveloped defender and clears f8 to prepare for castling. `...Nf6` already cleared g8; the king on e8 is not in check and f8/g8 are not attacked, so `O-O` is legal. `...dxc4` is also a sound central choice, but `...Be7` gives a clear reason of defensive development and future king-safety setup.

**Common mistake:** Treating `...dxc4` as "best" immediately because it captures, and ignoring bishop development. `...dxc4` can also be a sound central choice; the lesson here is to compare on the board what immediate capture, defensive development, and future break each create as future value.

</details>

## Position 6 — Trap or just a position?

The last position comes after `1. e4 e5 2. Bc4 Nc6 3. Qh5 Nf6`. White is getting a tempting queen capture, but the answer is strong only when check, protection, and king escape are verified. This is a trap-recognition exercise, not a claim that `3. Qh5` is the best opening move in every game.

![Chess position 6: scholar's mate pattern](/diagrams/chess/phase-3/phase-3-opening-06.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `r1bqkb1r/pppp1ppp/2n2n2/4p2Q/2B1P3/8/PPPP1PPP/RNB1K1NR w KQkq - 4 4`

**Time limit / task:** 2 minutes. Test the queen capture with check and mate notation; then state how Black could have avoided the scholar's-mate pattern immediately with `3...g6`. Also explain the common beginner error.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `4. Qxf7#`

**Short line:** `4. Qxf7#`

**Reasoning:** The queen from h5 captures the f7 pawn along the diagonal and gives check to the king on e8. The bishop on c4 protects f7, and the black king's escape squares are either occupied or under queen control. So `...Nf6` did not prevent the queen capture but completed the trap. With `3...g6` the black g-pawn would attack queen h5; after `Qxg6` Black could play `...fxg6` and capture the queen for free, so the immediate mate pattern would not arise. Understanding the pattern's reason matters more.

**Common mistake:** Choosing the queen capture only because it looks like a free piece, without verifying protection and king escape. This exercise's lesson is right here: confirm a trap by separating attractive move from real tactic.

</details>

## What It Is NOT

"Best move" in this pack does not mean the only theoretically perfect move in every position; it means minimum clarity and one board-level opening reason. Examples like `Bc4`, `...a6`, and `Qxf7#` illustrate their plan; they do not promise a fixed rating result.

Memorizing opening principles is not enough. If you cannot state a move's reason, do not copy it; build your explanation from development, center, safety, or structure evidence by looking at the diagram again.

## Readiness checkpoint

After attempting positions, write `✓` or `needs review` next to each answer. If you can also state a reason of development, center, king safety, or structure along with the sequence, the checkpoint is passed. Memorizing only the move is not part of Phase 3's goal; writing the reason in your own words is necessary.
