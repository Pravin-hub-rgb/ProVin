When `3.1 Openings Are Plans, Not Moves` gave you the language of a plan instead of a fixed move list, now those three core pieces of the plan need to be seen on the board. These three pieces will tell you why a move is useful, not just because it "appeared in the opening."

Just like building a house requires attention to foundation, load-bearing walls, and a safe entry point, in chess development, center, and king safety do that structural work. Once you understand them, opening moves start to feel like a checklist.

# 3.2 Development, Centre and King Safety

**Development** means bringing your knights, bishops, and rooks to active squares where they can work soon. Simply pushing pawns is not development; the active placement of your minor pieces, the knights and bishops, counts. **Centre** is the influence over the central area of the board — mainly the d and e files and ranks near the four-square center. **King safety** means creating a secure position for the king away from early attack.

## Development: engaging pieces

Think of development like bringing team players from the bench onto the court. A knight reaching the center can create targets on pawns and king; a bishop on an open diagonal can control important squares. The difference is not just the piece's presence but the work it can do later.

Ask on every move, "Is some undeveloped piece still on the bench?" If yes, prioritizing development is practical. After `1. e4`, `2. Nf3` feels natural because the knight becomes active and the path for future castling stays clear. Not every opening demands the exact same move, but the principle of development stays flexible.

## Centre: more than pawns, influence

Do not measure the center by only two central pawns standing there. Center control means attack, influence, and future entry possibilities on central squares. White's d4 or e4 pawns, along with the lines of knights and bishops, can naturally support central space.

Often advancing a central pawn comes before development and king safety. In 10+0 a center move like `e4` or `d4` can be a good starting idea, but development must be completed afterward. If the pawn advanced and the king stayed open, the center gave you space but also sent a bill for safety.

## King safety: castle early, imagine clearly

Do not keep the king in the center for long while the opponent's pieces attack central and open files. **Castling** repositions king and rook in one move, but it is legal only under four conditions: king and chosen rook must still have castling rights, the squares between them must be empty, the king must not currently be in check, and the king must not pass through or land on a square attacked by an opponent's piece — all three of king's current, transit, and destination squares must be unattacked.

Do not treat castling as a "magic shield." The three squares being unattacked is a legality condition; after castling, open files, enemy bishop diagonals, or weak pawns can still create future danger. After safe castling you must focus on pawn structure and piece coordination; safety is not a one-time move but a continuing habit.

## A practical opening loop

Repeat three questions on every position: which piece will develop, which central square will be influenced, and what is the king's next safe square? Even if one move does not perfectly fit all three, identify its primary purpose. This shifts you from "looks good" to "this move does development, center, or safety work."

Work with annotations on the board: one arrow for development, one cross for an unsafe king line, one note for "center." Annotate the first two moves of every opening book, then check again after one simple opponent reply. This exercise will appear on positions in `3.5 Exercise Pack`.

## What It Is NOT

**Center** does not mean only pushing central pawns. Influence, open lines, and future piece entry also count in center control; sometimes a pawn advanced at the wrong time can spoil center control.

**King safety** does not mean always keeping the king on the back rank. The practical test of safety is: is the pressure on the king's surroundings and retreat squares manageable? Balancing development and safety is part of the plan.

## Checkpoint: three goals, one sentence

Revisit your last Lichess opening and write three columns for its first 10 moves: development, center, king safety. Put only one or two board examples in each column, not full theory. Mark any move that serves no purpose for "explain or replace."
