If you understand the king's forward route and the defending king's timing, some positions start to feel very simple.

# 5.2 King-Pawn Endings

A **king-pawn ending** is an endgame position where one side typically has a king and one or more pawns, while the opponent has a king and fewer or zero pawns. The immediate goal of a king-pawn ending is not always to make a queen; first defend the pawn, take the king with it, and stop the opponent's king from advancing. The order of these three matters a lot in practical rook-pawn and central-pawn positions.

A central pawn, like `d4` or `e5`, is flexible for the king. The king can go in front of it to control weak squares, and pawn pushes like d4-d5 give steps of flexibility. A rook pawn means a pawn on an edge file, like white's a- or h-file pawn; it can be more dangerous near the edge, but the black king must carefully watch the rook file and promotion route. Before converting a rook pawn with deep theory, understanding the basic practical rule is necessary.

## Understanding the rule of the square simply

The **rule of the square** is a visual timing test: if the defending king can reach the square ahead of the pawn before it advances, it can stop the pawn. Build an imaginary square from the pawn's current file and rank, and see how many moves the king needs to reach that square. If the king arrives first, the promotion claim can be weak; if the pawn forces the king on its side, the path opens.

This rule does not give permission to "run the king forward." The pawn normally moves one square per move (verify the double-step on the first move), and the king must maintain its safety; so along with the square test, also check captures, opposing pawns, and back-rank squares. Its main job now is to quickly evaluate promotion, not deep calculations.

## Taking the king with the pawn

A beginner-friendly rule is to bring the king in front of or beside the pawn before pushing it. When the king is ahead, it controls the pawn's next square, stops the opposing king from reaching a weak square, and keeps support ready for future promotion. If the king moves too far ahead, the pawn can be left unprotected in front of the opponent's king; if the king stays too far behind, tempo is lost.

Advancing a central pawn is often useful because it changes the king's route and safe squares. But push timing matters: the pawn's destination should not be in the opposing king's control, and your king should be able to support that square. On a rook pawn some squares ahead are dangerous for the king, so "one extra move is always helpful" is not true.

## Race and supporting squares

To simplify the race question, track the pawn, note the defending king's move count, and visualize your king's correct destination. If the opponent's king is far from the pawn and your king can come forward, a clear promotion path can arise. If the opponent's king already stands in front of the pawn, you must find an opportunity through attack, exchange, or king maneuver — not blind pawn pushes.

### A practical position's example

Example: white king on e5, pawn on e4, black king on e8, White to move. A king move like `Kd5` is a practical step of cooperation and support with the pawn, while blindly pushing the pawn without king coordination can be a weak move. The sequence can change after checking exact distance and the defending king's replies on the board.

## King-pawn practical decisions

Do not reduce the decision to the shortcut "king forward, pawn forward"; see it in three layers. First king safety and captures; second the defending king's distance and opposition; third the pawn's promotion route and rook/piece entry. Pushing a central pawn more can be useful, while a rook pawn's side squares need careful checking. Similarly, if the opponent's king is far, race tempo is available, but if king support is missing the pawn can be weak.

| Question | If yes | If no |
|---|---|---|
| Is the king supporting the pawn? | Pawn can advance | King's route must change |
| Can the defending king arrive first? | Calculate the race | Build a combined pawn-and-king plan |
| Is the pawn central or a rook pawn? | Apply the general idea | Carefully check side-specific squares |
| Is the promotion route open? | Evaluate the promotion push | Look for opponent's king, block, or rook plan |

## What It Is NOT

King-pawn ending is **not "push the pawn, make a queen, done."** If king support is missing, the defending king can advance, or the promotion square is under rook/blockade control, an extra push can lose tempo. The basic reasoning is to see the king, captures, and future of the destination after every pawn move.

The rule of the square is **not the final verdict for every position.** It is a rough timing test; active pieces, opposing pawns, stalemate, and rook checks can change it. You do not need to memorize deep rook-pawn patterns and detailed opposition rules now — the next topic will give a basic map of opposition.

King activity does **not give permission to blindly run the king forward.** The king must go to a useful square, not just away from the pawn. This phase's target is not a guaranteed rating but repeatable decision improvement: the king's route, pawn timing, and the defending king's counter-plan.
