# 5.5 Trapped Pieces and Common Endgame Traps

A **trapped piece** is one whose safe squares are decreasing, so much so that its mobility becomes limited. Being trapped does not necessarily mean "captured now"; the piece can be temporarily blocked, the defender can exchange it, or it can get out with rook/king support. So first count mobility, then after forcing capture see the resulting position.

In a beginner scan, ask for every piece: where is the piece now, how many legal safe moves does it have, and is the opponent controlling that square? If only one exit square remains and that square is attacked, the piece can practically be trapped. Then decide whether to capture it immediately, exchange its defender, or increase pressure another way on the board.

## The stalemate trap

**Stalemate** is a draw that happens when the side to move's king is not in check but has no legal move. By contrast, if there is no legal reply after a check, it is checkmate. In the endgame the aggressive side can accidentally create a stalemate by cornering the king with rook or pawn pushes. To avoid this, learn to count whether the opponent's king has a legal move, check, or capture resource after every forcing move.

Stalemate is a draw, not a win. If your plan is "completely freeze the king," check an extra move or alternative target before the last move. Watching the king's cornering, promotion path, and opponent's other pieces together is practical protection.

## Back-rank trap

A back-rank trap is understood when the king has its own pawns in front and the opposing rook/queen can check from behind on the rank. Pushing pawns to make the king "safer" is tempting, but those pawns can block escape squares. In an endgame scan, look at the king's front pawns and the opposing rook/queen's line together.

## King greed and the board after exchange

There is a right reason to take the king to a central square, but not to blindly push it forward for a greedy capture. After queen-pawn, rook-pawn, or bishop-pawn promotion a counterattack can appear, and the king's active position can suddenly become defensive. With a king move ask: am I capturing a piece, or giving the opponent a free piece/promotion?

Re-scanning the board after an exchange also stops common traps. You exchanged a defensive knight, but now an open file or back-rank pattern has appeared in front of the king. Simplification is helpful only when the position is clean with the remaining pieces, not just because one piece is gone.

## Forcing a trapped piece

If an opponent's piece is trapped, you do not need to capture it immediately; first increase control over its escape square. Change the rook's rank, bring the king closer, or block the square with a pawn push. When every escape is blocked, assess capture or exchange options. A common error is sending the queen on an unnecessarily long route after seeing a trapped piece — calculate the closest supporting piece and fastest route.

This scan can be used after every move in the endgame. Since fewer pieces remain, one mistake's material effect is larger and the threat can increase as the defending king's location changes. Do not separate the safety checklist from the plan; this is practical junior improvement.

## What It Is NOT

A trapped piece is **not already captured.** Mobility can be temporarily limited, and the piece can get out with a defender. So along with the "trapped" label, verify legal escape, capture, and exchange lines.

Stalemate is **not automatically your win.** If the opponent has no legal move, the game is a draw, no matter how active your pieces look. A back-rank trap is also a pattern, not the final result of every endgame; you must see king, pawns, rook/queen, and escapes on the board.

King activity is **not a license for greed.** Bringing the king to a useful square is useful, but ignoring a promotion threat and taking a central capture can ruin the position. In the endgame, before writing "king move," you should also think of the opponent's counter-plan.
