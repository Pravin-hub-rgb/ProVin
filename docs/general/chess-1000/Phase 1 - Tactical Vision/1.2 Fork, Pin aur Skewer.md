Just like a truck on the road can block two lanes at once when a car stops in front of it, in chess one piece can block another important piece along a line. `1.1 Board Scan aur Blunder Check.md` taught you to find immediate checks and captures; now we name the tactical lines that give the opponent two choices at once. Seeing these patterns separately first, then comparing them on the board, is the practical work of this topic.

# 1.2 Fork, Pin and Skewer

## Fork

A **fork** means your one move attacks two different valuable targets, such as king and queen. In a forcing move the opponent usually saves one target, so the other becomes a free material or positional gift. Forks appear in common knight and queen attack patterns; a rook can also fork king and a valuable piece along a line.

The analogy is a road crossroad: just as one vehicle can block two different lanes at the same moment, a tactical move can target two different values on one line. To find a fork on the board, first note the opponent's valuable pieces, then see whether your piece can reach a common attack square between them.

A fork can give both material and initiative. The tradeoff is that the opponent usually responds to one target, so you must count protection and escape before capturing.

## Pin

A **pin** happens when an opponent's piece stands in line and moving it would expose an attack on the king. In an absolute pin, moving the piece becomes illegal because the king would be in check. In a relative pin, the piece can move, but the attacker can capture the protected piece and win material. So do not shortcut a pin as "the move is illegal" or "always safe"; the real rule is king safety.

Just as moving a car parked in front of a building could block the road's exit, moving a pinned piece can uncover an attack. To verify a pin on the board, remove the capture, check, or attacking piece along the line; if the king is the target, the risk of the opponent's move has increased.

To win material from a pin you must attack the pinned piece, but if it is defended you must also count the defender after capturing. So finding a pin is useful, but treating every pin as an automatic material gift is a beginner trap.

## Skewer

A **skewer** is almost the mirror of a pin: a valuable piece, usually the queen, stands in line ahead of the king, and removing the capturing piece opens the line to the king. So in a skewer you first force the valuable piece away, then attack the king.

Just as removing an important signboard opens the window behind it, in a skewer the queen's departure opens a direct attack on the king. Find this pattern along the line of a queen, rook, or bishop; you must also check the blocker between the source piece and the target piece.

A skewer's strength depends on the capturing piece and its protection. If the capturing piece is protected and the king can capture, the skewer does not guarantee material gain; so verifying the short main line and response is necessary.

## Comparing the three patterns

All three patterns have one line and two important pieces, but the sequence differs. Use the comparison table only after reading all three definitions, so you do not memorize labels.

| Pattern | What happens | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| Fork | One move attacks two targets | King, queen, rook, or material can be won for free |
| Pin | A piece moves and exposes the king | In an absolute pin the king-exposing move is illegal; in a relative pin it can be legal but often costs material |
| Skewer | A valuable piece is removed from the line, exposing the king | After the queen or piece, the king gets a direct attack |

## Exercise — pin and removal

Here the white rook, black rook, and king are on the same rank. First identify which piece is blocking the line, then look at the check after the capture.

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `Rrk5/8/2N5/8/8/8/8/4K3 w - - 0 1`  
**Time limit/task:** 2 minutes. Find the rook capture that exploits the black rook's role between the white rook and king.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and review</summary>

**Best move:** `Rxb8+`

**Short main line:** `1. Rxb8+ Kd7`

**Why it works:** The black rook on b8 is pinned between the white rook and king on c8. White removes it, so the attack reaches the king and the pinned rook is lost; the white knight on c6 protects b8, so Black cannot recapture with the king.

**Common mistake:** Trying to calculate a long capture sequence while missing the immediate adjacent capture. A pin is easiest to understand when you first remove the blocker and then check whether the captured square is protected.

</details>

## Exercise — skewer and exposure

In this position the rook, queen, and king are on one file. You must capture the queen and then attack the king behind it, so first identify the blocker.

![Phase 1 tactic position 03](/diagrams/chess/phase-1/phase-1-tactic-03.svg)

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `3k4/8/3q4/8/8/8/8/3R3K w - - 0 1`  
**Time limit/task:** 2 minutes. Find the rook capture that removes the queen from the king's line.

<details>
<summary>Show answer and review</summary>

**Best move:** `Rxd6+`

**Short main line:** `1. Rxd6+ Kc7`

**Why it works:** The queen on d6 blocks the rook's file attack on the king. After `Rxd6+`, the queen is gone and the rook checks the king directly, which is the skewer pattern.

**Common mistake:** Looking for a knight fork first and ignoring the rook's line. A skewer can be created by any line piece when the right blocker is removed.

</details>

## What It Is NOT

Fork, pin, and skewer are not three separate visual tags to slap on every position. Their use is meaningful only when the consequence for king safety, material, or initiative after the move is verified.

Do not always treat a pin as "any move of the pinned piece is illegal." In an absolute pin the piece cannot make a king-exposing move. In a relative pin such a move can be legal, but capturing the protected piece causes material or positional loss.

2. How would you explain a pin as "exposing the king"?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** In an absolute pin the pinned piece cannot make a king-exposing move. In a relative pin the piece can make such a move and the king stays safe, but the attacker can capture the protected piece and win material, so material loss is common.

</details>

3. Why can the white rook pin the black rook in exercise 02?

<details>
<summary>Show Answer</summary>

**Sample Answer:** The white rook on a8 is on the same line as the black king on c8, with the black rook on b8 as the blocker in between. The black rook cannot make a king-exposing move, so White can capture it and exploit the pin. The knight on c6 protects b8, so recapture with the king is also illegal.

</details>
