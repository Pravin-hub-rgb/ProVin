You do not need to calculate all of them like an engine; the first job is to pick the moves on which later work will depend. Learning this practical filter saves time in 10+0.

# 2.1 How Candidate Moves Are Made

In chess, **calculation** simply means looking at your move and understanding what the opponent's most important reply would be. A **candidate move** is one that fits both your position's plan and the board's geometry. Keeping 5–10 realistic candidates in your head is enough for a beginner; listing every possible move is not necessary.

On the board, use a familiar routine like watching traffic. First look at the immediate danger ahead, then look at the path in front of your pawns and pieces, then think about a move that creates a future threat. You do not need to memorize this order separately; it becomes natural with practice.

## Three filters first

The first filter is **check**. If a move gives check, the opponent must give a legal reply, so your mind can focus on that move. The second filter is **capture**, but not every capture is automatically good; calculate whether your piece survives after the capture.

The third filter is **threat**. A threat means your move creates the possibility of a capture, check, or piece entry later. If one move clearly stands out through all three filters, calculate a short line on it; do not immediately discard the other candidates, just give them priority.

## A practical 30-second routine

When a position appears, first scan your king. Is the king facing any immediate attack? If yes, escaping or blocking is the first task. If not, note your available checks, captures, and attacks.

Now compare two or three candidates on the board. For each candidate ask only three questions: what is the opponent's best reply, where does the successful piece go after my move, and did I put my king or any critical piece in extra danger? After that, go one or two forcing moves deep on the best-looking candidate.

You can write a practical line like this:

`1. Qxf7+ Kd8 2. ...`

Here `Qxf7+` is both check and capture, so the opponent's legal replies are limited. `...` means you are not calculating the whole game right now; you are reopening the position for the next decision. In 10+0 this is enough time when a clear tactical moment appears on the board.

## How to keep the candidate list short

Do not try to see all directions at once, like seeing every light at once. For five candidates keep a mental tick-mark note: `Check`, `Capture`, `Threat`, `Safe`, `Improve`. This increases focus and keeps calculation from becoming a random tree.

Remember the reason for discarding a move too. If `f3` opens the bishop but leaves the king unsafe, that move leaves the candidate list for now. A **quiet move** means a move that gives neither check nor capture; still, its immediate positional effect can be important. Comparing the future value of a quiet move is a detail we will see in `2.4 Quiet Moves Ka Evaluation`.

## What It Is NOT

**Candidate moves** does not mean "whatever moves are legal." Not every legal move deserves serious consideration; a candidate move must be relevant to the board's current need.

**Calculation** also does not mean you must remember every possible reply up to 15 plies, that is about 15 individual moves by both players. For this course calculation is based on short, practical lines: move, opponent reply, immediate result, and a basic safety check.

In the next chapter we will look at forcing lines in detail. For now, ask yourself one question while solving a position: "Did I choose two or three strong candidates, or did I give every legal move equal weight at once?" If the second answer comes, make the calculation a bit more focused.
