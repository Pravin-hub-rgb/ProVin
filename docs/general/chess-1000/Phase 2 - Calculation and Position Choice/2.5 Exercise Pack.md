When you learned in `2.4 Quiet Moves Ka Evaluation` to compare candidate moves with a future board snapshot, now the exercise pack will apply those same skills to small legal positions. There will be no engine-style long calculation in each position; you must write the candidate, opponent reply, safety, and a practical evaluation.

Solve each position for 60–120 seconds. Position 6 gets two minutes, so the pack's upper time limit is 120 seconds. First note your best move, then think of one opponent reply after the move, and only then open the answer accordion below. That way seeing the answer becomes active practice, not pattern recognition — the exact reason.

Each position's FEN is the text description of the board shown from White's perspective in the diagram. Read FEN like this: piece placement, side to move, castling, en passant, and move counters. For now keep focus on piece placement; you do not need to remember counters for the exercise.

# 2.5 Exercise Pack

The positions in this pack get gradually harder. The first two have a clear tactic, the middle two need the candidate checked with safety, and the last two need evaluating material after a fork or choosing a quiet endgame move. This progression makes calculation into practical decision-making, not an engine line.

## Position 1 — Protected checkmate

This position is a beginner's first forcing-move test. White has a protected queen move visible, but first you must verify whether the black king has any escape square after the queen capture. Look at the diagram, write your move first, then verify checkmate against the king's possible escape squares.

![Chess position 1: protected queen mate](/diagrams/chess/phase-2/phase-2-calculation-01.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `3rk3/5p2/4P1Q1/8/8/8/6K1/8 w - - 0 1`

**Time limit / task:** 60 seconds. Write two best-move candidates, check all possible opponent replies, and give a one-sentence reason with the best move.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `1. Qxf7#`

**Short line:** `1. Qxf7#`

**Reasoning:** The queen from g6 captures the f7 pawn and gives a diagonal check to the king on e8. The white pawn on e6 protects the queen, so the king cannot play `Kxf7`. The black rook on d8 stands on one potential escape square, while the queen controls d7, e7, and f8; the rook cannot capture or block the check. This is an example of verifying a forcing line with safety first.

**Common mistake:** Treating the occupied d8 square as a king escape. The king cannot move there, and the adjacent diagonal check cannot be interposed with the rook.

</details>

## Position 2 — Knight fork

Now the forcing move targets the opponent's king and an important piece together. In this position first build a candidate list, then look at Black's replies after `check`. Understand the fork like this: one piece attacks two important targets in one move.

![Chess position 2: knight fork](/diagrams/chess/phase-2/phase-2-calculation-02.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `2r1k3/p4p2/8/8/2N5/8/6K1/8 w - - 0 1`

**Time limit / task:** 90 seconds. Enumerate Black's four legal replies `Kd8`, `Kd7`, `Ke7`, and `Kf8` after `Nd6+`. State whether the rook is captured immediately or Black recaptures the knight with the king, giving only an exchange result.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `1. Nd6+`

**Short line:** `1. Nd6+ Kf8 2. Nxc8`

**Reasoning:** The knight on d6 gives check to the king on e8 and also attacks the rook on c8. Check restricts the opponent's replies to four king moves. After `Kd8` or `Kd7`, `2. Nxc8 Kxc8` gives Black the rook in exchange for the knight, while after `Ke7` or `Kf8` the rook is captured for free. Comparing the material result of every forcing reply is practical calculation for a beginner.

**Common mistake:** Seeing `Nd6+` and clicking immediately without noticing the rook can be saved by the opponent. Playing without verifying both check and fork leaves evaluation incomplete.

</details>

## Position 3 — King safety first

In this position White must face a check initially. The point of the exercise is not a candidate move's "looks good" feeling, but solving the immediate king danger. After the check you may see a block, but after the block the rook can capture your piece.

![Chess position 3: king safety check](/diagrams/chess/phase-2/phase-2-calculation-03.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `4r2k/8/8/Q7/8/8/8/4K3 w - - 0 1`

**Time limit / task:** 60 seconds. Scan four legal king escapes `Kd1`, `Kd2`, `Kf1`, and `Kf2`, choose one practical best move, and write the consequence of one tempting block. `Ke2` is a wrong option because e2 lies on the rook's open checking line.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `1. Kd1!`

**Short line:** `1. Kd1 Rd8+ 2. Kc1`

**Reasoning:** The king on e1 is in check from the rook on the open e-file. `Kd1` takes the king off the file and gives a central, usable square; when the rook plays `Rd8+`, the king can go to the safe area with `Kc1`. `Qe5` also looks like a legal block, but the rook can play `Rxe5+` and take the queen — this is the practical job of the safety check.

**Common mistake:** Choosing `Qe5` because it blocks the line. A block is helpful only when the blocker is protected and can survive the opponent's later counterattack.

</details>

## Position 4 — Removing a pinned piece

In this position there is not a black rook and king on the bishop's diagonal, but a knight blocker with an important relationship. You must evaluate the bishop's move giving check together with the protected piece and king safety. This position makes a forcing capture something to be understood with its follow-up, not just noticed.

![Chess position 4: bishop removes a blocker](/diagrams/chess/phase-2/phase-2-calculation-04.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `1r2k3/8/2n5/1B6/8/8/6K1/8 w - - 0 1`

**Time limit / task:** 90 seconds. Identify the bishop's forcing candidates, enumerate Black's legal king replies `Kd8`, `Ke7`, `Kf7`, and `Kf8` after `Bxc6+`, and write the material result after the knight is captured.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `1. Bxc6+`

**Short line:** `1. Bxc6+ Kf8`

**Reasoning:** The bishop on b5 was on the c6-d7-e8 diagonal, but the black knight on c6 was blocking that line. `Bxc6+` captures the blocker and gives check to the king on e8. Black's replies are `Kd8`, `Ke7`, `Kf7`, and `Kf8`; none can capture the bishop. The material gain is clear, and your bishop comes to a central, active square.

**Common mistake:** Playing a quiet move like `Ba6` and giving the knight away for free. `Bd7` is not legal here because the black knight on c6 blocks the bishop's path from b5 to d7. Even if a quiet candidate is legal, `Bxc6+` gives immediate gain.

</details>

## Position 5 — Two targets, one move

Now the target is not a rook but a black queen. This time you already know the knight fork pattern, so the new part of the exercise is evaluating candidate safety and the follow-up capture without over-calculation. Every opponent reply creates a different future position; do not spend equal time on all of them.

![Chess position 5: knight forks king and queen](/diagrams/chess/phase-2/phase-2-calculation-05.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `2q1k3/8/8/8/2N5/8/6K1/8 w - - 0 1`

**Time limit / task:** 90 seconds. Identify the forking move with the names of both targets, check Black's `Kd8`, `Kd7`, `Ke7`, and `Kf8` replies, and explain the recapture possibility after the queen capture.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `1. Nd6+`

**Short line:** `1. Nd6+ Kf8 2. Nxc8`

**Reasoning:** The knight on d6 attacks both the black king on e8 and the queen on c8. After `Kd8` or `Kd7`, `2. Nxc8 Kxc8` gives Black the queen in exchange for the knight. After `Ke7` or `Kf8`, Black cannot save the queen because the king cannot recapture on c8. Combining check and fork is useful, but counting the final material result of every reply is necessary.

**Common mistake:** Seeing `Nd6+` and treating every king reply as the same result. `Kd8` and `Kd7` give an extra recapture step, while `Ke7` and `Kf8` do not.

</details>

## Position 6 — Quiet king move

The last position is quiet: no check or capture. Here you must evaluate the pawn's key square, opposition (both kings standing face to face with one square between them), and future approach. The key squares of pawn c5 are b6, c6, and d6. Deep pawn-endgame formulas are not needed now; just see which next square makes the future better.

![Chess position 6: king reaches a key square](/diagrams/chess/phase-2/phase-2-calculation-06.svg)

**Side to move:** White

**FEN:** `2k5/8/2K5/2P5/8/8/8/8 w - - 0 1`

**Time limit / task:** 2 minutes. Compare four legal king moves `Kb5`, `Kb6`, `Kd5`, and `Kd6`, write the plan that brings the pawn to a key square with `Kd6`, and also verify the black king's immediate proximity.

<details>
<summary>Answer and reasoning</summary>

**Best move:** `1. Kd6!`

**Short line:** `1. Kd6 Kd8 2. c6`

**Reasoning:** The key squares of pawn c5 are b6, c6, and d6, and the white king is already on c6. `Kd6` keeps the king among these key squares, protects the pawn, and also creates legal distance from the black king. Treating `Kb5` or `Kd5` as an immediate "safe" retreat would be wrong, because along with safety the key-square distance must also be evaluated. Full endgame theory is not needed.

**Common mistake:** Treating a king retreat like `Kb5` or `Kd5` as immediately "safe." Safety and key square plus pawn distance must both be evaluated; one retreat can slow the future.

</details>

## What It Is NOT

The exercise pack's answer is not an engine certificate; it only shows a practical next move and its reason. Answers like `Qxf7#`, `Nd6+`, or `Kd6` are this position's clearest learning example, not a compulsory move in every future game.

The checkpoint score is also not a rating guarantee. If some positions were wrong, repeat the practice of writing reasons; if right, still write a short evaluation note after each move in the next real game.

## Readiness checkpoint

When all positions are attempted, score yourself before looking at the answer sheet. Five or six correct candidates and reasons mean you are ready for Phase 3's opening principles; three or four mean redo positions 1–4; two or fewer mean revisit the short examples of `2.1` through `2.4`. This is not a rating guarantee, just a practical checkpoint for starting the next phase.
