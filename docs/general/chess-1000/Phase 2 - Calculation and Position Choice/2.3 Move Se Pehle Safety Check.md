When you saw the opponent's short replies after check or capture in `2.2 Forcing Lines`, now an important safety net needs to be added. Every forcing move can look attractive, but before clicking a move on the board your own king and exposed pieces must also be protected.

Just like when crossing a road you do not only look at the green light but also at your lane, oncoming traffic, and your destination, in a chess move look at your piece's destination and the opponent's counterattack. This three-second habit prevents big mistakes in rapid games.

# 2.3 Safety Check Before the Move

A **safety check** means verifying before playing a move that the opponent cannot immediately punish your new position. Here "punish" means loss of material, king, or an important positional asset. A safety check is not a replacement for calculation; it gives calculation order and discipline.

## Check 1: Your king

First look at your king's current safety and possible destination. If there is a check, pin, or open file in front of the king, do not play the move blindly. A **pin** means a piece stands in front of the king and moving it would leave the king attacked. So do not just look at the pinned piece; also think about what happens to the king after it moves.

If your move takes the king to an attacked square, count the opponent's replies. It is not enough to think "the king looks close"; from the board's perspective that square must also be safe, or there must be a clear escape or response.

## Check 2: The piece's new destination

Look again at the destination of the piece that is moving. Is that square in an enemy piece's direct capture range? Is the piece's original control area being lost after the move? Has the piece become blocked and stopped contributing practically?

This naturally gives the concept of a **hanging piece**. A hanging piece is one that is about to be captured for free by the opponent because its defender, control, or retreat square is missing. While calculating, keep not only a list of your material but also a list of your pieces' protection.

## Check 3: Counterattack and discovered idea

After thinking of your move, ask once from the opponent's side: "What is the answer to what I am doing?" If the opponent's reply is a check, capture, or attack on your king, evaluate the line now. This is not generic "what if" thinking; it is looking at the position immediately from the opponent's perspective.

Then think of the basic idea of a **discovered attack**, where moving one piece suddenly opens another piece's line. We will not cover this in detail yet; for the next topic it is enough to scan new lines, pins, and attacks after your move.

## A 10-second safety script

Repeat three small questions on every move:

1. What immediate danger is near the king?
2. Is the moving piece protected and is its new square safe?
3. What is one forcing counterattack by the opponent?

If all three answers are clear, keep the move in the candidate list. If one answer is unclear, touch the piece on the board or mentally move it and check again; taking this time is smarter for a beginner.

Do not overthink the safety check. Its job is not to turn every move into an engine-level proof, but to avoid an obvious blunder — such as giving your queen away for free, taking the king to an open file, or moving a defender and leaving a piece undefended.

## What It Is NOT

A **safety check** does not mean "full engine analysis of the whole position before every move." It is a targeted scan: king, moved piece, and immediate counterattack.

Seeing a **check** does not mean only looking at your own pieces. The opponent's forcing replies are also checks, captures, or potential threats. Ignoring them makes the safety check incomplete.

## Checkpoint for a real game

After a rapid game, mark in your notes the moves where the opponent immediately captured material. Next to each mark write: was the king unsafe, was a piece being given away for free, or was the counterattack not calculated? When a pattern appears, your safety habit becomes measurable.
