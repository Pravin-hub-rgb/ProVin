Just like when you watch a gym video, the trainer does not make you do hundreds of reps by saying "keep the hand a little straighter." They give one important signal about your current movement, and you apply that signal in your next set. A chess engine works the same way, but only when you make it a coach for your thinking, not a replacement for your choice.

In `6.1 Game Analysis Without Engine` we learned that writing your move before the engine, comparing alternatives, and finding the decisive moment is necessary. Now we add trust, but trust is useful only when you compare it with your own thinking. That is why in this lesson we will do coach-like review on Lichess.

# 6.2 Using the Engine Like a Coach

A **chess engine** is a computer program that looks at many legal moves and evaluates the position. **Evaluation** is the engine's qualitative or numerical judgment; in this chapter we will not treat the exact number as truth, only understand whether the position is good or bad for a side.

It is also helpful to understand the engine like Google. You ask a question, get many answers, but you decide with your experience which answer fits the situation. The engine shows a line, but your tactical aim, practical understanding, and the position on the board decide whether following that line is right.

## Before calling the engine

On Lichess you can open your game in analysis or Study view and add computer analysis. The exact button name can change, so instead of a screenshot of the interface I will teach the order of the work.

1. Open a recent game and pause at an important position.
2. Write the FEN and your move on the board, then check the position again from the restart.
3. Write two candidate lines for yourself, not only the engine's best line.
4. Now turn on engine analysis.
5. Compare the engine's recommended line, your candidate lines, and the opponent's forcing replies.
6. Before selecting one line, write the reason: "I want this because..."

On Lichess you can use analysis speed, **depth** control, or multiple-lines feature. **Depth** means how many moves ahead the engine has calculated; more depth usually means more calculation, but increasing depth is not necessary in every position. Your goal is not to copy the top line but to make your thought process readable.

## Read PV like a line

In the engine report you see **PV**, or **principal variation**. This means the engine's preferred sequence, like a first move and the replies after it. You can understand PV like a recipe that worked in one particular position; the same recipe is not guaranteed on the next board.

When you read PV, ask three questions. First: at which move did the position's quality actually start to worsen? Second: which forcing line did the engine allow to be ignored, and why? Third: what practical opportunity was there for you that you missed?

When engine evaluation increases, do not immediately say "I was wrong." An evaluation change is a useful signal, but context matters too. If you chose the best theoretical move under low clock and lost the game, the lesson can be "not better decision, focus on time and practicality."

## Understanding the alternative, not blindly copying

When you keep your candidate move different from the engine's best move, first compare what the immediate consequence of both moves is. Did your move save material? Does it give the opponent a forcing check? Did you improve piece activity or just target an empty square?

In Lichess review you cannot directly draw `+/-` arrows or circles on your move, but you can write arrow-like shorthand in notes: "e5→d7, recapture force." The benefit is that you can explain your own choice later. Blindly copying one line is not training, just transcription.

## How to make a coach note

Compress every important review into these four lines:

- **Situation:** The position's type, such as hanging piece, open king, or bad pawn structure.
- **My thought:** Why you chose that move, without the engine.
- **Coach point:** Which threat or resource the alternative missed.
- **Next cue:** Which observable reminder you will use in next games.

Suppose you placed your rook on b7 and the opponent attacked it from the other side. A useful coach note is not just "rook on b7 was wrong"; the useful version is "before opening the file, I will move the rook for safety or trade, or calculate the immediate tactic." Such a note can change the next decision.

## 45-minute practical routine

You can do 10 minutes of independent review, 15 minutes calculating two candidate branches, and 15 minutes making a coach note from engine comparison. Keep the remaining 5 minutes to decide the next assignment, so the session stays actionable even after the board is closed.

Analyzing one game three times can be helpful, but browsing superficial percent bars of ten games in one day is not. You must write your thought, alternative, and trigger; the engine's green number is secondary.

## What It Is NOT

The engine is **not a replacement for your thinking.** It gives a strong signal of the position, but you must explain where the signal came from and what practical meaning it has in a real game.

The engine is **not human understanding of every position.** It does not fully capture opening lines, time trouble, practical piece activity, and the opponent's psychology. So "the engine said best" is a reason, not the final sentence.

The engine **does not prove every disagreement a blunder.** Do not judge the magnitude of an evaluation change without context; the red arrows of a queen sacrifice and a quiet pawn move can mean different things.

Now the engine can give your mistake a name, but the work of improvement is understanding that mistake's **category** and its warning sign beforehand. In the next lesson we will build a practical routine to fix recurring blunders.
