You have probably watched a cricket replay: the scoreboard does not directly tell what happened, but slow motion shows that one delivery became the moment that changed the match. The same happens in chess. Looking at moves you can think "why did I go wrong?", but the real work is identifying that one moment when the board's direction changed.

In `5.6 Quiz - Essential Endgames` you learned how king and pawn basic ideas can be decisive. But there is still an important gap: recognizing those ideas only in quiz positions does nothing if you cannot see them in your actual game. So now we connect theory to your decisions.

# 6.1 Game Analysis Without Engine

**Game analysis** means revisiting a finished game and understanding who built the position, which choice changed its direction, and what you can do differently next time. It is not just repeating live calculation. You pause at slow speed before an important move and look at your reasoning.

Just as GPS does not just show your current location but records the route to show why you missed a junction, your Lichess game record is also such a record. This chapter's aim is to turn every loss from an emotional sentence "I lost" into a small, repeatable decision task.

## Write your move first

The first rule of analysis is: leave the engine and solve the problem in your own words first. For every important position, write the FEN, **Forsyth–Edwards Notation**. FEN packs board arrangement, side to move, castling rights, and some other necessary information into one compact line; you do not need to fill every square manually each time.

Now write your candidate move before playing it on the board. Just writing "I played `Nxd5`" is not enough; also write what you calculated, what attack you expected, and what your plan was after the opponent's best reply. This way the engine judges your answer, not replaces your mind.

This order is important. If the engine stays open all the time, you will start copying its best move and your thinking will never develop. Just as knowing the weight beforehand changes form in the gym, your own first move becomes the base of your training.

## How to read your game on Lichess

On Lichess's game page you can open your game and take it to analysis or study view. The exact label of the UI can change, but the job is the same: replay your game slowly and pause when an important `You` decision is coming.

After that, follow this small workflow:

1. Write the position with FEN and important pieces.
2. Think of your next 3–5 candidate moves and note them in one line.
3. Play your preferred move on the board, but do not complete calculation yet.
4. Restart the position and look at your calculation again.
5. Note one line each about the opponent's forcing reply, piece values, king safety, and pawn structure.
6. Only now pause to see engine feedback.

This sequence protects you from making a "mistake gallery." You will not write commentary on every move, only understand the positions where the decision could have advanced. Here **ply** means one individual move, that is one chess move by one player. Your job is not to solve every ply; your job is to find and understand the **decisive moment**, the moment where direction changed.

## Reading one position in three notebook lines

Suppose you resigned, but you think maybe your knight came late. It is not enough to just write on the board: "I mistakenly placed the wrong knight." Also write that "the knight was needed to cover the king," "the opponent had a center counterattack plan," and "next game I will check king safety before opening the queenside early."

Now restart that position and compare alternatives. Maybe the knight was good immediately too, but some retreat move made your long-term plan more practical. Your job is not to copy one engine best line; your job is to understand what the costs of **your choice and the other choice** were.

You can give a simple label:

- **Tactical miss** — a capture, check, fork, pin, or discovered attack was missed.
- **Strategic unclear** — the move was legal, but the purpose of pawn structure, piece activity, or king plan was not understood.
- **Time problem** — the position was difficult, but the clock took away time to think clearly.
- **Endgame conversion** — there was a material advantage, but the result was not secured with technique or plan.

Comparing these labels later with the engine is helpful. If your label turns out wrong, that information is also valuable: it means you missed an important feature of the position, not just the move's result.

## Build your personal error list

After every analyzed game, build an **error list**. It should not contain the whole game, only two or three recurring mistakes. With each mistake write a short trigger, such as "gave the queen away for free while opening the king" or "chased an unclear piece under low clock."

Turn every entry into a small assignment for next week. If your trigger is "did not calculate captures," set a task to write the forced reply after every capture in the next three games. The assignment should be small and observable; not something like "play better."

## 45-minute analysis session

Analysis does not mean spending a whole day with the engine. In a practical session, 10 minutes finding the decisive moment sitting in front of a recent game, 20 minutes calculating two alternative branches yourself, and 15 minutes updating your error list is enough.

If you cannot do it in 45 minutes, complete the review even with less. A well-written note is more useful than three hours of scattered engine browsing. You need evidence of decisions, not the theater of results.

## What It Is NOT

Game analysis is **not a competition to beat the engine on every move.** You do not need the perfect move in every position; you need to understand your recurring costly mistakes and their observable signs beforehand.

Game analysis is **not a past-tense report of rating.** Rating is a noisy signal, and one loss does not always mean your understanding worsened. What happened on the board is more reliable evidence.

Game analysis is **not just looking at results.** Even in a winning game you can learn from a moment where you wasted a chance, and in a losing game you can find a good defensive move.

Now that you can safely compare with the engine after your game, the next lesson will teach using the engine like a coach. A coach's job is not to replace your choice, but to show where your plan was weak.
