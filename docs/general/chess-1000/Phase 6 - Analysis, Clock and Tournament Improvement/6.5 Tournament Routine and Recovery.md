On exam day, starting the second subject immediately after the first is not productive for every learner. After some water, stretch, food, and a chain reset, the next attempt goes better. Rated chess tournaments are the same: starting the next game immediately after one result is not a smart routine if body and mind are not given a little time to recover.

In `6.4 10+0 Clock Routine` we understood time stages and practical selection inside one game. Now that discipline is needed at event level, where you recover after a session's result and start the next game with the right mindset.

# 6.5 Tournament Routine and Recovery

A **tournament** is an organized set of many rated games where opponents, time control, and rounds follow a schedule. Your goal is not to win every round, but to test your preparation in real conditions and take useful information for the next round.

Before joining an event on Lichess, it is smart to check time control, rated or unrated status, duration, and pool on the event page. Inside the event you will choose fair training samples instead of protecting your rating, so every round gives you genuine practice.

## How to choose a suitable rated event

10+0 rapid events can be a practical starting point for your current target, but "suitable" does not mean only matching the time control. The event's estimated duration should fit your day's budget, and you should keep some time for analysis after the result.

Set realistic expectations by looking at the event pool. You can learn from opponents near your current level, but avoiding every weaker opponent is also not training. Some stronger games will show you uncomfortable ideas, while some familiar games will clearly expose your recurring mistakes.

Before entering, think of a fallback. If the event runs unexpectedly long, a network issue comes, or your schedule changes, what will you do? Withdrawing just because "mood is off today" is not healthy, but blindly ignoring your health, sleep, and real-life commitments is not tournament routine either.

## Warm-up before the event

15–20 minutes before the event, do a short warm-up on Lichess: moves of a familiar opening, some simple tactics, and a quick clock-awareness exercise. The purpose is not to read a new giant opening chapter; the purpose is to activate your hands, eyes, and decision routine.

You can write one line before every game: "Today I must clearly state my plan, not play random moves aggressively." Your goal is not to beat every opponent, but to maintain attention quality. Just as a warm-up readies the body, a fixed pre-game checklist keeps decisions clean.

## Check the result after every round

Immediately after every game, note the result, opponent rating, time control, and your rough feeling. You do not need to check every move immediately in the engine; first give the result context and give your body a chance to recover.

Before the next game, drink water, take a screen break, go to the bathroom, and take five deep breaths. This small reset routine preserves your focused attention. Then use a fixed pre-game checklist, not a new "last minute" plan.

## Learn from loss, do not punish

In loss review do not start with "I am a weak player." First go to the decisive moment, write your move and the opponent's forcing reply, then compare alternatives. Target extracting only one or two actionable lessons from every loss, so notes stay actionable and emotional baggage does not grow.

Avoid result-pool gaming. This means selecting opponents only according to your rating or expected result to farm easy points, manipulating weaker pools, or leaving the event if a favorable result does not come. Choose fair rated events, play against realistic opponents, and treat the opponent as a learning resource.

One game's rating is never the complete story. Pool strength, time pressure, form, and luck all mix into the result, so judging personal worth from one isolated result is not healthy. At the end of the event look at a broader record like a 20-game sample, not one lucky peak.

## Recovery after the event

Joining another tournament queue immediately after an event ends bypasses recovery. Food, water, light movement, and sleep support your decision quality, because 10+0 is also mentally demanding. Take at least one break, then look at results and make a written summary.

You can review quality before score: how many games went into time trouble, what type the decisive errors were, and in how many games you clearly followed your plan. Then note the trend by looking at the rating snapshot. This helps you select the next event, because you will know in which type of pressure your preparation was better.

## 45-minute recovery routine

You can split a session into three parts: review, rest, and next step. 15 minutes written review of one loss, 10 minutes hydration and walk, and 20 minutes preparation for the next event. If you are outside a tournament, 20 minutes of tactics or position work is also useful.

Recovery helps only when you do not avoid it. After every event write "one task for tomorrow," such as "count king danger" or "use a simple plan for the first ten moves." This task becomes the bridge to the next event.

## What It Is NOT

Tournament routine is **not pressure to win every round.** If you treat every result like a final exam, focus can end after one loss. Every round is a sample of information and practice.

Tournament routine is **not opponent shopping or result-pool gaming.** Searching for favorable opponents for easy points is not fair training, and farming rating only from results is not sustainable progress. Choose realistic events, fair play, and useful review.

Tournament routine is **not ending rest.** Some people lose focus when they understand recovery's value, but mental energy is necessary in 10+0 too. Keeping your schedule realistic is long-term discipline.
