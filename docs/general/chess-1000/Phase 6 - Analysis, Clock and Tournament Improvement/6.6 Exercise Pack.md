In practice cricket you do not get to practice only one shot; you rotate through multiple shots so one moment's success is not mistaken for whole-game readiness. Here too, instead of solving one decision repeatedly, we are giving six different decision points. Treat each position like your own game: first think and write your move, then look at the hidden answer.

In `6.5 Tournament Routine and Recovery` you learned that after real games pause, result context, and honest loss review are necessary. This exercise pack gives that routine a safe training space, where making a mistake is part of practice and every answer comes with a next-week lesson.

# 6.6 Exercise Pack

An **exercise pack** means deliberate practice on small, focused positions, instead of random moves to raise a score. The positions here are constructed training positions, but every prompt asks you to replay a decision point from your own game. You do not need a perfect answer; you need to compare your reasoning.

For each position follow this order: read the FEN, confirm side to move, play your move on the board and write it, then think of the opponent's best response and note an alternative, and only at the very end open the details. **Write your move before checking the engine** is the central rule here, because the first purpose is not to copy the answer but to see your decision process.

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `6k1/5ppp/8/8/8/8/8/R5K1 w - - 0 1`

Imagine the opponent's pawns have come in front of the king and your rook is freeing a file. **Attempt task:** write your move, then explain how the escape squares close and through which line the rook delivers.

<details>
<summary>Show best move and lesson</summary>

**Best move/continuation:** `Ra8#`. The rook goes to a8 along the a-file and gives check on the back rank; f8 and h8 are attacked by the rook, while f7, g7, and h7 are blocked by Black's own pawns. Black has no legal move to interpose this check.

**Engine-style evaluation reasoning:** The qualitative engine reading is "forced mate for White." The exact number is not needed; the decisive signal is the rook's rank delivery and Black's blocked escape squares. You had to calculate the opponent's back-rank idea before your move.

**Lesson to add to the error list:** "Before moving the rook from an open file, check the opponent's king's escape squares."

## 2. Rook geometry and false mate claim

**Side to move:** Black  
**FEN:** `r5k1/5ppp/8/8/8/8/5PPP/4R1K1 b - - 0 1`

Black's rook may suggest a tempting idea of going to a1, but the white rook on e1 blocks the check line. **Attempt task:** choose a legal move and verify the capture ahead — is the rook's path clear, is there a capture on the destination, and does the blocker stop the check line?

<details>
<summary>Show practical move and lesson</summary>

**Practical legal move:** `Kf8`. The black king on g8 can safely go to f8; this is not check or checkmate. The rook on a8 cannot move diagonally, and even after `Ra1` the white rook on e1 blocks the first-rank line, so there is no immediate Black mate in this position.

**Engine-style evaluation reasoning:** The qualitative reading is "equal practical move; no immediate Black check." You did not need an exact evaluation number, but rook geometry, blocker, and SAN notation verification. Before labeling a capture or mating move, you had to see both destination and check line.

**Lesson to add to the error list:** "Before labeling a capture or mate, verify the rook's legal path, the piece on the destination, and the check's blockers."

## 3. Opening a line for the queen

![White-to-move queen back-rank decision point](/diagrams/chess/phase-6/phase-6-analysis-03.svg)

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `6k1/5ppp/8/8/8/8/4QPPP/6K1 w - - 0 1`

Here you have a queen, but the queen's best move may not be on the left side of the board; it can happen by shifting straight along the rank. **Attempt task:** write the queen's possible checks with the line, and identify which legal escape of Black's king has been blocked.

<details>
<summary>Show best move and lesson</summary>

**Best move/continuation:** `Qe8#`. The queen goes from e2 to e8 along the vertical line and checks the king on g8 along the rank. f8 and h8 are covered by the queen's rank line, and f7, g7, h7 are not letting Black's king escape because of its own pawns.

**Engine-style evaluation reasoning:** The qualitative engine-style reading is "direct mate once the open e-file is found." You did not need a long calculation list, just compare the queen's destination, rank control, and escape squares. This move created a direct result without capturing a piece.

**Lesson to add to the error list:** "Before moving a piece on an open file, check queen and rook rank/file delivery."

</details>

A central knight stands on the board, but before capturing it you must also look at surrounding pieces. **Attempt task:** write all capture sources of the `d5` knight, then choose where to keep the piece active after grabbing material.

<details>
<summary>Show best move and lesson</summary>

**Best move/continuation:** `Nxd5`, and one possible continuation is bringing the knight to central and protected activity after `...Kf8`. The knight from c3 captures Black's knight on d5; the target is occupied on d5 and the move looks legal. `Kf8` is not forced, but it is one legal practical reply example in this position.

**Engine-style evaluation reasoning:** The qualitative reading is "White wins material while keeping a central piece," not a promise of immediate mate. You had to see the trade-off between material gain and piece activity, not an exact evaluation number. If you ignored pawn structure or king safety, your comparison would stay incomplete.

**Lesson to add to the error list:** "Before counting free material, note capture's piece activity and counterattack."

## 5. King and queen final conversion

![White-to-move king-and-queen conversion](/diagrams/chess/phase-6/phase-6-analysis-05.svg)

**Side to move:** White  
**FEN:** `7k/5K2/6Q1/8/8/8/8/8 w - - 0 1`

Now fewer pieces are on the board, so every square's role has changed. **Attempt task:** write the queen's options near Black's king, and before your preferred move count the opponent's legal escape squares.

<details>
<summary>Show best move and lesson</summary>

**Best move/continuation:** `Qg7#`. The queen goes from g6 to g7 and gives a diagonal check to the king on h8. h7 is covered by the queen, g8 is controlled by the white king on f7, and Black has no piece to block the check.

**Engine-style evaluation reasoning:** The qualitative engine-style reading is "queen and king produce a forced mate." Instead of an exact evaluation number, note the pattern of coordinated control by king and queen. Despite fewer pieces, coordination was decisive.

**Lesson to add to the error list:** "In the endgame, when piece count is low, recalculate king and sliding piece coordination."

</details>

## 6. Continuing queen capture to mate

![Black-to-move mating capture decision point](/diagrams/chess/phase-6/phase-6-analysis-06.svg)

**Side to move:** Black  
**FEN:** `q6k/8/8/8/8/8/5PPP/R5K1 b - - 0 1`

An attacked rook has appeared in front of you in an attack, but only capturing is not enough; after the capture you must also see the king's exit map. **Attempt task:** write Black's rook capture options, then note one line on White king's legal squares after them.

<details>
<summary>Show best move and lesson</summary>

**Best move/continuation:** `Qxa1#`. The queen goes from a8 to a1, captures the white rook, and gives check to the king on g1 along the first rank. White has no interposing piece, f1 and h1 are in the queen's line, and f2, g2, h2 are blocked by its own pawns.

**Engine-style evaluation reasoning:** The qualitative engine reading is "capture and checkmate is the clearest continuation." Before material gain you had to count rank control and the opponent's king's escape squares. This is necessary because a simple capture can be not just a free-point distraction but a complete winning move.

**Lesson to add to the error list:** "Before capturing an attacked piece, verify rank/file control and the opponent king's escape squares."

</details>

## Readiness checkpoint

Now open all six positions again with details closed, without the engine. On each position you should get three things: your first move, the opponent's forcing reply, and one small lesson you will add to your error list.

Practical signals of passing the checkpoint can be:

- In at least four of six you understand the decisive moment without hints.
- In every answer you wrote your move on the board and at least one alternative.
- Instead of an exact evaluation number, you gave a qualitative reason, such as forced mate, material gain, or safer king position.
- You extracted one specific next-game cue from every position, not a vague message like "think better."
- After finishing these exercises in a 10+0 routine you are ready to play one rated game too.

If on more than three positions you could not build any direction without the engine's answer, do not think the course failed. It means tactical vocabulary and pattern recognition need a little more review; that information is the input for your next block.

## What It Is NOT

The exercise pack is **not a rating forecast.** The best answers in six positions measure your tactical recall and explanation, but do not predict your next opponents, time pressure, and result. The purpose is not to pressure for a score or six out of six, but to practice a repeatable decision loop.

The exercise pack is **not a replacement for the engine.** Writing your move, counting forcing replies, and comparing alternatives are necessary steps; the engine gives feedback after these steps. If you copy every position's answer and move ahead, the exercise's learning result decreases.

The exercise pack is **not work to reconstruct the complete history of every real game.** Treating a position like your own game is a useful lens, but a practice position is a deliberate example. The real value is converting the source move into the error list, not in the position's score.

## A 45-minute pack session

In the first 15 minutes attempt two positions and only note your moves. In the middle 15 minutes do the remaining four positions, and in the last 15 minutes update your reason and error list by comparing with the engine. Open the engine after the attempt, so the exercise becomes feedback, not a shortcut.

It is not necessary to make a "full review of the loss" for every position. Keeping the tactical question concise and writing source position, missed question, and prevention cue is enough. Your goal is not six perfect answers but one repeatable decision loop.

Now your Phase 6 drill set is ready. After this, check your understanding with `6.6 Quiz - Analysis, Clock and Tournament Improvement`. After that, in `7.1 Personal Improvement Plan` you will convert this exercise routine into your 10-week schedule's 45-minute daily practice, then check your readiness with a 10-game training block and 10-game capstone.
