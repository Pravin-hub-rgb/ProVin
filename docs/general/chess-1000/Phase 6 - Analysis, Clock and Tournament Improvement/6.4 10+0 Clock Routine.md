At a train station the announcement says "train leaves in ten minutes," and hearing it you adjust your packing, platform, and next move. You do not need to remember a fixed schedule for every second; you only need to know which task comes first in which phase. A chess clock is the same kind of external signal, but it does not mean keeping the same number of seconds on every move.

In `6.3 Blunders ko Fix karna` we saw that tactical, king-safety, structure, and time-pressure errors are different. In 10+0 distinguishing these errors is necessary, because you have a different kind of time in the opening, middlegame, and endgame.

# 6.4 10+0 Clock Routine

On Lichess, **10+0** means each player gets 10 minutes at the start of the game and no fixed increment is added per move. This is not "10 minutes per move." The side-to-move's clock is already running when the game starts; while you are thinking your clock is already running, and after your move the opponent's clock starts. So your 10-minute clock must be managed across however many moves the game has — there is no universal 40-move requirement. The question is not `seconds per move` but **when to finish which job**.

10+0 is fast chess, but there is a difference between chess and a sprint. A sprint needs full effort; in 10+0 you will not get full thinking time for every move, so important positions need extra care. Watching the clock improves your calculation, yes, but playing every move in panic is not improvement either.

## Look at the clock in three phases

At the start of the game you need to think two complete opening moves, but not read a whole book chapter on every possible line. Note a simple route for development, castling, and central pawn structure, then adjust the route after seeing the opponent's development. This way you avoid finishing the whole opening chapter and save something for the middlegame too.

In the middlegame, choose a position with one clear question: is the center strong, which piece will attack, or what is the king's weak point? Evaluating every position fully wastes too much time for 10+0. Write your goal on the board, and as soon as you find a forcing move, capture, or threat, push calculation there.

In the endgame the plan for rook, queen, knight, or bishop should be clear. Note a simple purpose for promoting pawns, exchanging pieces, or bringing the rook to an active square. When the plan is clear, you can break calculation into small, disconnected branches because you are not trying to solve every possible variation.

## No universal seconds-per-move rule

Online advice often gives a recipe of "this many seconds per move." That number depends on your opponent, position, internet speed, and training level, so there is no universal rule that fits all players. A better choice is to notice your own clock pattern and understand the board's urgent moments.

In one study session you can play three 10+0 games and see where you use time and where you lose it. Just note numbers, not prescriptions: "6 minutes left after the opening" or "every move was expensive in the last ten moves." These observations can become checkpoints for you.

On Lichess the side-to-move's clock runs as soon as the game starts. While you are thinking your clock keeps running; after your move the opponent's clock starts. So click confidently after thinking a move, but shortlist candidate moves before getting stuck on the position. Accidentally clicking a wrong move on the board is also a time cost, so both analysis habit and interface discipline matter.

## How to recognize time trouble in 10+0

**Time trouble** is the stage when you have very little realistic time left for calculation. It is not just the clock's red number; the simplicity of your calculation, the clarity of the board, and the pressure of selection also increase.

When the position looks complicated, first order the immediate threats. Look at check, capture, and block together, then resolve them one by one. Instead of trying to solve every possible rook-endgame branch, you can identify one simple, practical choice.

If you are learning advanced terms like **pass** or **zugzwang**, keep them for now with a simple working definition: pass means not making a move when making one worsens the position; zugzwang means the player must move, but every legal move makes their position a little worse. Detailed study of these terms comes in advanced topics, but the 10+0 principle is: it is not necessary to make a move every time.

## Lichess practice routine

You can build a practical version of your 45-minute session like this: 15 minutes simple tactics or position work, 15 minutes two or three 10+0 games, and 15 minutes clock-aware review. After every game write where time went, on which move anxiety came, and which rule was remembered while identifying a forcing move.

In review do not immediately run the engine to get the "correct move." Write your move and first choice, then compare with the engine. This way you do not just learn to survive time pressure, but to choose decisions that are practical in your actual position.

## What It Is NOT

10+0 routine is **not a fixed seconds-per-move rule.** No universal number fits your position and opponent; personal observation and stage awareness help more.

10+0 routine is **not an excuse to play every move quickly.** Playing fast can be useful, but speed without understanding rushed capture, king danger, or pawn move is not improvement.

10+0 routine is **not freedom to ignore time and calculate.** The clock gives you limited research time, so giving every new question equal priority is not necessary. Learn to consciously defer other branches by choosing a simple goal.

Now you know how to manage the clock inside one game. In the next lesson we will see how to plan recovery across multiple games of a tournament and after a real result, because focusing on the board alone is not enough for real rated improvement.
