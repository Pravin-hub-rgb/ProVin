A small mistake can become a big problem, but it can be fixed if you find that moment and make a trigger for next time. # 6.3 Fixing Blunders

A **blunder** is a move that made your position so bad that you faced clear risk of material, check, or mate — such as a free piece, mate given, or a simple tactic allowed. It does not always mean "the engine's biggest red arrow"; sometimes a practical move is a blunder when you had no reason for it.

Treating a blunder as blame is not healthy. The statement "I was slow" is useful only when it produces a next-game testable action, such as "I will count immediate checks after the opponent's development move." Without an action, confession feels good today but does not change behavior in the next game.

## First see the cause, not the mistake's name

In analysis we separate three things: position, move, and cause. Position is the facts on the board; move is the choice you made; cause is the missing check or thinking error that made the choice bad.

You can place a mistake in these simple families:

- **Tactic miss** — capture, check, fork, pin, or discovered attack was not searched.
- **Loose piece** — your piece was not defended, but you assumed protection.
- **King danger** — check, pawn storm, or open line was underestimated.
- **Structure and plan** — the reason for pawn move, exchange, or piece activity was not clear.
- **Clock pressure** — the position was difficult, but all time went into one unclear choice.

In one game one mistake can fall into two families. For example you left a knight loose and did not defend it under time pressure; in that case a combined cause note is more useful. But creating categories should not become a new distraction. Every mistake must be understood with cause and next cue.

## The blunder-fixing cycle on Lichess

Open your recent rated game and choose one red position. You do not need the "biggest mistake," but the most actionable one, because large volume teaches less and an actionable mistake gives more correction.

Run the cycle like this:

1. Pause at the position, write the FEN and your move.
2. State what threat you expected.
3. Try to think the opponent's forcing reply yourself after restarting your move.
4. Now compare the engine's best continuation and one alternative.
5. Place the blunder in a cause category.
6. Write one small next-week assignment, such as "in the next three games, count the defender before moving a loose piece."

Writing the move before checking the engine is an important guardrail of this cycle. It lets you improve your calculation and not build a habit of only revealing answers. The line you saw yourself can become a pattern later.

## One practical cue for each category

To fix a tactic miss, write the opponent's simplest reply after every capturing or checking candidate. Learning to check the forced sequence before piece values is necessary, but writing it on the board visibly changes your calculation habit.

To fix a loose piece, count defenders, blockers, and capture squares before moving. One defender is not enough if it can move after your next capture; so the word "defended" must be thought through a little.

To fix king danger, look at the opponent's pawn advance, open file, and discovered attack together. King safety check is not one line but a lens of the position; your king moves only when there is a practical reason for protection, squares, and counterplay.

To fix structure and plan, after every pawn move do not just count squares; write the future purpose. A question like "the pawn move opens, but which piece will come to that file?" makes the future plan visible.

To fix clock pressure, before opening an uncertain calculation in unlimited detail, switch quickly. Choosing a simple, forcing, or checking move and resetting the position can also be a strong decision.

## Recovering after a blunder

Resigning immediately after a blunder is not the only option. First check immediate forcing moves: is there a check, capture, block, or threat that gives you one more minute? Save your king, regain the piece, or in the worst case simplify the position and create draw chances.

After recovery, ask three questions: "what is the best move now?", "which mistake will I not repeat?", and "can the result still improve?" This brings you back into your thinking after a loss, instead of only looking at the scoreboard.

## Next week's assignment

Choose only three triggers for your error list. For each trigger keep an observable count, such as "loose pieces in three games," and write a prevention cue. Then review those games and see whether the cue helped stop the mistake.

Keep the assignment small in your daily routine. A three-minute note is also useful if it captures the exact position, exact missed question, and next move. You need repeatable evidence, not a long wish-list.

## What It Is NOT

Fixing blunders is **not making every loss catastrophic.** Every game's result is a sample, and the improvement signal comes from board decisions. One loss does not mean "I am weak," only that one case for the next review has been found.

Fixing blunders is **not just changing engine percentages.** If your score improved but the same tactical pattern remained, do not read a small number improvement as readiness. Give more time to behavior and decision quality.

Fixing blunders is **not justifying every mistake against the engine.** Sometimes your move is the best practical move, yet the opponent built a better position. Keep the difference between "a better move was available but I did not choose it" and "the move was fine, the result was bad" in an honest note.

Now that your blunder patterns are clear, combining them with the 10+0 clock is necessary. Because that tactical attention works fully in a training session, but when half a second is left in a real game, a routine is needed.
