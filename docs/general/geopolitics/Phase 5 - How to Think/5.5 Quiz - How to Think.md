# 5.5 Quiz - How to Think
## MCQ

Q: What did the Stanford research on fact-checking find?
A. Students could not reach the right answer
B. Students often got the right answer but relied on the document's appearance and their own memory rather than checking outside sources
C. Fact-checking websites made students worse
D. Graduate students were no better than high school students
> B. Many students reached a correct verdict while having no idea how — they trusted the document's look and their own memory. The ones who both got it right and could explain why had used lateral reading: leaving the source to see what others said about it.

---

Q: What is lateral reading?
A. Reading a document from left to right carefully
B. Checking the source's funding and authors
C. Leaving the source and searching what others say about the claim
D. Reading several documents in parallel
> C. Lateral reading means opening a new tab to find out what other sources say about the claim. It works because careful reading judges a document in isolation — exactly what a well-designed document is optimised for — while lateral reading judges it against everything else.

---

Q: Why does careful reading fail as a defence against manipulation?
A. People are not good at reading
B. Because a well-crafted document is optimised to survive careful reading, and manipulative documents are designed to do exactly that
C. Because careful reading is too slow
D. Because documents are usually too short
> B. Careful reading evaluates the evidence and reasoning in front of you. A manipulative document is specifically constructed to withstand that evaluation, which is why it works. Lateral reading asks a question the document cannot control: what does everyone else say?

---

Q: What is the first step in the three-minute fact-checking method?
A. Check the source's funding
B. Stop and write down what you already believe before reading
C. Search the claim in quotes
D. Read the corrections
> B. Ten seconds, and it is essential. Without it you cannot tell whether you were convinced by evidence or merely agreed with what you already thought. Everything else in the method depends on having a "before" to compare against.

---

Q: Why is "just check the facts" inadequate advice?
A. It is too slow
B. Because most distortion is spin, in which every statement is true and the misleading effect is in selection and emphasis
C. Facts are always accurate
D. It only works for written sources
> B. Spin consists of individually true statements, so every fact-check passes while the overall impression is false. You can verify every sentence and still be thoroughly misled.

---

Q: Which is the single most important idea in file 5.3?
A. Labelling technique
B. Base rates — that news frequency is not event frequency
C. The truth sandwich
D. Unnamed sources
> B. Base rate neglect is the error that catches even careful readers, because it is not in any individual fact — it is in the aggregate. Roughly 350 Americans die in air crashes a year versus 650,000 from heart disease, and air crashes get hundreds of times the coverage.

---

Q: Why is base rate neglect particularly dangerous?
A. It makes you read too much
B. Because the error is not in any individual fact, so fact-checking does not correct it — it is an error of aggregate judgement
C. It only affects statistics
D. It is easy to teach
> B. You can fact-check every claim in an article and still draw entirely the wrong conclusion about how common or dangerous something is, because the error lives in what you conclude from the facts rather than in the facts themselves.

---

Q: What is the truth sandwich technique?
A. Placing a misleading claim between two true statements so the whole thing feels verified
B. Presenting two sides of a debate
C. Citing two reliable sources
D. Quoting a document twice
> A. Because the surrounding statements are true, the middle item inherits their credibility. Most people do not check the middle item against the same standard as the outer ones.

---

Q: Why is repetition such an effective manipulation?
A. Because it is always a conspiracy
B. Because human memory has a strong sense of what you have heard before and a weak sense of how many times — so the most repeated claim feels most true regardless of support
C. Because journalists repeat themselves
D. Because repetition indicates importance
> B. This is not usually a conspiracy. The mental shortcut is simply that familiarity feels like accuracy. It means the claim you have heard most is generally the most repeated one, not the best-supported one.

---

Q: Why is being moderate not automatically a virtue?
A. Because moderates are usually uninformed
B. Because the middle ground is not automatically accurate — on smoking and lung cancer, decades of evidence did not produce a genuine two-sided debate
C. Because moderation means cowardice
D. Because extremes are more interesting
> B. Being moderate is a temperament; being accurate is a goal, and they come apart. Treating "smoking causes cancer" and "smoking might cause cancer" as two sides would have been simply wrong, and would have cost lives.

---

Q: What is false balance?
A. Giving both sides equal airtime
B. Treating unequals as equals — presenting two positions as equally credible when the evidence supports one
C. Being indecisive
D. Reporting both sides of a war
> B. It is often committed by people who believe they are being fair, which is what makes it dangerous. Saying "some disagree" on a settled empirical question is not balance, it is misleading while appearing neutral.

---

Q: What are the three questions that distinguish legitimate balance from false balance?
A: Is there genuine disagreement among people who are both qualified? Is the disagreement about evidence or values? What is the quality of the sources on each side?
B. Who benefits? What are the consequences? How loud is it?
C. Is it recent? Is it well-funded? Is it documented?
D. Which country? Which leader? Which ideology?
> A. The key test is whether qualified people genuinely disagree — and whether the disagreement is about evidence, which resolves over time, or about values, which does not. "Many people say" is not evidence; neither is passion or repetition.

---

Q: What is the "am I the audience?" question, and why is it productive?
A. Whether the article is written for beginners
B. Whether a message is aimed at outsiders to influence them, or at a domestic audience — because a great deal of what looks like foreign-policy news is domestic politics aimed at a foreign audience
C. Whether you were mentioned
D. Whether it is paywalled
> B. Governments constantly produce messages aimed at other countries to influence them, and these get reported in domestic media as though they were settled policy. Recognising the difference between a stated position and an actual decision is one of the most useful things a reader can learn.

---

Q: What is the "would I feel this strongly if the names were swapped" test?
A. A test of whether you are emotionally reactive to the countries involved rather than to the event
B. A test of political bias
C. A way to detect propaganda
D. A test of memory
> A. If you would be calm about the same event happening to a country you dislike, your reaction is about the country. If you would be equally angry either way, your reaction is about the event and is probably justified.

---

Q: What did research on prebunking find?
A. That warning people about manipulation techniques makes them harder to manipulate later
B. That warnings are counterproductive
C. That only experts can be manipulated
D. That prebunking works only for political content
> A. People told in advance to watch for false balance and unnamed sources were measurably harder to manipulate afterwards. This is exactly what Phase 5 has been doing to you, and it argues for spending ten minutes learning how narratives about a new topic are manipulated before reading any of them.

---

Q: What is the single criterion that matters most when evaluating a think tank?
A. Its reputation
B. Whether it publishes named authors with disclosed funding and releases primary data you can independently check
C. How often it is cited
D. Whether its staff are academics
> B. A think tank that releases data you can verify is worth more than a famous one that publishes only conclusions. Reputation is a reasonable prior, but checking the actual criterion is better than trusting a prior.

---

Q: Why are YouTube videos good for geopolitics but unreliable for facts?
A. They are usually made by non-experts
B. Maps in motion explain geography far better than text, but videos are made to be watched to the end, which creates engagement incentives rather than accuracy incentives
C. They are too long
D. They are often paid for
> B. The rule that follows: use a video to understand a topic, never to verify a specific fact. For a figure, a date, or exact wording, you need a written source you can quote.

---

Q: Which source would be best for the actual global military expenditure figures?
A. A newspaper editorial
B. A think tank's opinion piece
C. SIPRI's published database
D. A YouTube explainer
> C. SIPRI publishes primary data rather than conclusions, which is why primary data is the most important criterion for evaluating any source. A database you can independently check beats a famous name publishing only analysis.

---

Q: What did research on thinking versus writing summaries find?
A. Thinking is more effective than writing
B. People believed thinking taught them more and remembered less — the activity that feels most like learning teaches least
C. Writing and thinking are equivalent
D. Writing only helps for factual material
> B. Re-reading and thinking feel productive because they are familiar and comfortable. Writing forces retrieval, which is what makes memory durable — and it forces you to state claims, which is where you discover you cannot state them.

---

Q: Why is the prediction line the most valuable part of the weekly log?
A. Because you become a better forecaster
B. Because it makes you find out how you are systematically wrong — and systematic errors are correctable while random ones are not
C. Because it is fun
D. Because you can share it
> B. Write the prediction with a confidence rating before you know the outcome. Over twenty entries you will find patterns — overpredicting escalation, underpredicting boring continuity, confusing loud stories for important ones — and those are the errors you can actually fix.

---

Q: Why is calibration more valuable than being right?
A. Because it is easier
B. Because being right by luck does not help next time, but knowing your confidence level tells you when to slow down
C. Because it makes you look intelligent
D. Because it is measurable
> B. In a subject where most public commentary is confidently wrong, a calibrated person knows which questions they cannot answer and slows down on them. That transfers better than any specific piece of knowledge.

---

Q: What are the three systematic errors you are likely to find in your own predictions?
A. Overpredicting escalation, underpredicting boring continuity, and confusing loud stories with important ones
B. Being too optimistic, too pessimistic, and too slow
C. Forgetting dates, misreading maps, and losing notes
D. Reading too little, reading too much, and reading the wrong sources
> A. Almost every standoff looks like it is heading somewhere serious and then de-escalates, because escalation is expensive for both sides. Most of the future is continuity, not drama. And you will predict for events that got coverage and that did not matter.

---

Q: What should you do after missing a week of the log?
A. Do three weeks of work on Sunday to catch up
B. Do the smallest possible version the next week — one event, five questions, five minutes
C. Skip the month
D. Restart the habit from scratch with a new format
> B. Trying to repay a missed week by doing three weeks at once is the most common way a habit dies. The smallest version that still counts keeps the habit alive, and a habit that survives is worth more than a heroic version you abandon.

---

Q: Why should the weekly log be private?
A. Because it is not good enough to publish
B. Because writing something you intend to publish changes how you write it, and the useful version is the unpolished one where you are allowed to be confused
> B. The confusion is the part worth finding. Making it publishable suppresses exactly the uncertainty you are trying to identify, which is what makes the habit work.

---

## TrueFalse

Q: Most false claims you encounter are deliberate disinformation.
- True
- False
> False. Misinformation — falsehood without intent — is far more common. Someone repeats something false believing it is true, often from a source that got it wrong first. Disinformation is real but comparatively rare.

---

Q: Spin can be defeated by fact-checking.
- True
- False
> False. Spin consists of individually true statements, so every fact-check passes. The distortion is in selection, emphasis and omission — which is why "check the facts" does not solve it.

---

Q: Being aware of propaganda techniques makes you immune to propaganda.
- True
- False
> False. Knowing the name of a manipulation does not stop it — it makes you feel protected, which is worse. The only defence is slowing down. Immunity requires constant vigilance, not knowledge.

---

Q: Repetition of a claim usually indicates a deliberate conspiracy to plant it.
- True
- False
> False. Sometimes a claim is repeated because it is genuinely important. Jumping to "deliberate planting" is itself a form of paranoia, and it discredits the scepticism you are trying to practise.

---

Q: The middle ground between two positions is usually the safest one to hold.
- True
- False
> False. The middle ground is not automatically accurate. On empirical questions the evidence often points clearly one way, and hedging is just being wrong more slowly.

---

Q: Treating "Israel's right to exist" and an armed group's claim as two sides of one question is a form of balance.
- True
- False
> False. That is false balance — it chooses a framing while appearing to avoid choosing one. It is a serious failure, and it is usually committed by people who believe they are being neutral.

---

Q: News coverage tracks human cost closely.
- True
- False
> False. Coverage tracks geopolitical importance far more closely. Wars involving many powerful states get more coverage per death than wars involving fewer. Both need reporting, but they are not the same thing.

---

Q: An Indian reader in a written article aims to inform, not influence.
- True
- False
> False. Much of what looks like foreign-policy news is domestic politics aimed at a foreign audience, and it is reported in domestic media as though it were settled policy. The audience question catches this.

---

Q: A headline is a summary of the article beneath it.
- True
- False
> False. A headline is an argument for why you should read the article, written by people whose job is engagement. It must be short, interesting and emotional — and none of those requirements is accuracy.

---

Q: If you cannot steelman an opposing view, that proves it is wrong.
- True
- False
> False. It proves you have not understood it, or you are avoiding it. Only after you have genuinely tried to state the strongest version and failed do you have earned that conclusion.

---

Q: A source being government-funded is reason to dismiss it entirely.
- True
- False
> False. Undisclosed funding is a reason for caution, not dismissal. A government-funded think tank may be perfectly accurate — you evaluate the claim, not the funding.

---

Q: Lateral reading means reading several sources side by side.
- True
- False
> False. It means leaving the source entirely and opening a new tab to find out what others say about the claim. The distinguishing feature is that you switch context rather than consume in parallel.

---

Q: You can become immune to manipulation by learning the techniques.
- True
- False
> False. You can become measurably harder to manipulate — prebunking research supports this — but not immune. Confidence of immunity is itself the main risk factor, because it reduces vigilance.

---

Q: Writing about material teaches you more than thinking about it.
- True
- False
> True. People consistently rate thinking as more effective and consistently remember less. Writing forces retrieval and forces you to state claims, which is where you discover you cannot state them.

---

Q: Calibration means being right more often.
- True
- False
> False. Calibration means your confidence matches your accuracy. It is a separate skill from being right, and it transfers better — because being right by luck does not help next time, but knowing when to slow down does.

---

Q: Making a prediction without a confidence rating is still useful.
- True
- False
> False. A bare prediction feels like a fact, so you learn nothing whether you are right or wrong. With a rating, six months later you can see that your 7s are often wrong — a specific, correctable pattern.

---

Q: Missing a week of the log means you should restart the habit from scratch.
- True
- False
> False. Do the smallest possible version next week. Restarting with a new format each time is the most reliable way to never have the habit at all.

---

## ShortAnswer

Q: You read an article claiming "India has been completely surrounded by Chinese military assets." Apply file 5.1 and tell me what you would do.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would first strip the framing: the literal claim is that China operates military assets in India's neighbourhood, which may be true, plus "completely surrounded," which is a claim about coverage that needs testing. Then I would ask where specifically — which facilities, in which countries, and military or commercial? The String of Pearls files make clear that most Chinese assets in the region are commercial ports, and that only one is a military base, in Djibouti. Then who are the actors that are missing — Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Bangladesh, all of whom granted or host these facilities for their own reasons. Then what each wants. And then what I would need to know, which is the specific classification of each facility. Almost certainly the residual claim after all that is real but much narrower than the headline.

</details>

---

Q: Someone argues "the war in Gaza and the war in Ukraine are the same, both are wars." Use file 5.2 to respond.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would not accept the framing, and I would also not simply dismiss it — there is a legitimate comparison being attempted. The productive move is to say what specifically is being compared. Both are wars, yes. But they differ in every dimension that usually determines scale and coverage: number of states involved, involvement of great powers, war aims, and most importantly the ratio between deaths and international coverage. The Gaza war has produced far more coverage per death than Ukraine, and the reason is geopolitical involvement rather than human cost. So the framing is a real comparison, but it needs to be made precisely — and if it is used to deny that they differ in significant ways, then it is false balance, because it treats two situations as equivalent when the relevant measures are not.

</details>

---

Q: Explain why someone can understand a country's actions completely and still think they were wrong — and why this matters.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because understanding why and judging whether are separate cognitive operations. You can trace a country's action to its interests, domestic politics and constraints with total precision, and conclude it was unjustified — those two conclusions live in different parts of the mind and do not need to reconcile. It matters because people who cannot separate them end up in one of two bad positions: defending everything their own side does, or defending nothing at all. Both are failures of contact with reality. The skill is to hold the accurate explanation and the moral judgement at the same time without one contaminating the other.

</details>

---

Q: You have kept a weekly log for three months and your predictions were wrong most of the time. What does that actually tell you?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** It tells you two different things depending on how you wrote them. If you rated confidence, it tells you your confidence is miscalibrated in a specific direction — and the direction is the useful part. Most people discover they overpredict escalation and underpredict boring continuity, and that loud stories get predicted and quiet important developments do not. If you did not rate confidence, the log has told you almost nothing, because you cannot distinguish a lucky guess from good judgement. That is the entire argument for including the number: without it, failure to predict is indistinguishable from not having tried to predict.

</details>

---

Q: Why does a journalist who genuinely wants accuracy still produce misleading work?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because accuracy and clarity compete under deadline pressure, and clarity usually wins. Every individual statement in a piece of spin is true — the distortion is in selection, emphasis and omission, none of which is a factual error. A writer choosing between four true facts has to include three, and which one is cut is where framing happens. That is not bad faith; it is the structure of the medium. It is also why "check the facts" is inadequate, and why the defence has to be at the level of what was included rather than whether what was said was true.

</details>
