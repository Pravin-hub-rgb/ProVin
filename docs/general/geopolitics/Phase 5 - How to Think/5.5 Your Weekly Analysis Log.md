Four files of technique. You can read carefully, hold both views, spot framing, and check facts.

**And in three weeks you will have forgotten most of it, because skills that are not used decay.**

This final file is about the habit that makes it stick. And it contains one technique for getting better at prediction that no other file in this course covers — and that is the thing I would most want you to take from this entire phase.

# 5.5 Your Weekly Analysis Log

## Why reading is not enough

**Let me start with a claim about how memory works, because it explains everything else in this file.**

**Writing something down makes it far more durable than reading it, or even thinking it.** This is well established. But the effect is much larger than most people expect.

**Here is the specific finding, and it matters for this course.**

**When people summarise material in their own words, they remember it far better than when they re-read it — even when the re-reading feels more productive.** Re-reading feels like learning because the material is familiar and the pages turn. **Writing forces retrieval, and retrieval is what makes memory durable.**

**Which means: everything in the last four phases will largely disappear unless you write it down.**

**But that is not the main argument for writing. The main argument is that writing is thinking.**

**There is a large and consistent body of research on this, and the finding is striking.**

**People consistently rate themselves as having learned more from simply *thinking* about material than from writing summaries of it.** And when their understanding is later tested, that confidence turns out to be wrong — **people who only thought remember substantially less than people who wrote, despite believing the opposite.**

**The uncomfortable version: the activity that feels most like learning is often the one that teaches you the least.**

**Writing is what converts reading into knowing. And in this subject specifically, writing forces you to state claims, which is where you discover that you cannot state them.**

## What the log actually is

**This is deliberately small. The failure mode of habits like this is that you design something ambitious and abandon it in three weeks.**

**So: one event a week. Twenty minutes. Written, not thought.**

**Here is the format. Copy it and use it as-is.**

**1. The event.** One or two sentences. No adjectives — just what happened, where and when.

**2. The five questions from file 5.1.** What is literally claimed? Where specifically? Who are all the actors? What does each want? What am I missing?

**3. The base rate.** How often does this kind of thing happen? **If it happens constantly, a headline about it is not news.**

**4. The opposing view.** One paragraph. In the steelman's strongest form, with no contempt words.

**5. What I don't know.** At least two things. **This is the most valuable line in the whole thing**, because it converts a vague feeling of uncertainty into a specific question.

**6. My prediction.** What will happen in three months, and how confident am I, 1–10? **Written before you know the outcome.**

**Line 6 is where the real skill is, and it is the next section.**

## The prediction, and why it is the most valuable thing here

**Almost nobody does this, and it is the single highest-return habit in this course.**

**Write down what you think will happen, with a confidence number. Then check what actually happened in three months.**

**Do this for twenty events and something will happen to you that is genuinely striking.**

**You will find that your confident predictions are often wrong. And you will find that the errors are systematic — not random.**

**And that is enormously valuable, because systematic errors are correctable, and random errors are not.**

**Here are the errors you will most likely find in yourself.**

**Error one: you overpredict escalation.**

Almost every India-China or India-Pakistan standoff looks like it is heading somewhere serious. **And then, most of the time, both sides de-escalate, because escalation is expensive for both.**

**If your predictions consistently assume things get worse, you will be surprised repeatedly in the same direction — and you will learn that the base rate for escalation is much lower than it feels.**

**Error two: you underpredict boring continuity.**

**Trade will continue. Elections will happen. Parties will win and lose. Diplomacy will limp along without resolution.** Most of the future is this.

**If you predicted dramatic events and got boring ones right, your instincts are calibrated wrongly and you need to know that.**

**Error three: you confuse a loud story with an important one.**

**This is file 5.3's base rate problem, but applied to yourself.** **You will predict outcomes for events that got a lot of coverage and that turned out not to matter.** And you will fail to predict the boring developments that did.

**And here is the most important point of all.**

**Calibration — knowing how confident you should be — transfers better than any specific piece of knowledge.**

**A person who is well-calibrated knows when they are out of their depth and slows down. An overconfident person does not.**

**And in a subject where most public commentary is confidently wrong, being calibrated is worth more than being right.** Being right by luck does not help you next time. Being calibrated means you will be slow on the questions you cannot answer — **which is most of the interesting ones.**

## The weekly review

**Once a month, do something different. Take three months of entries and look for patterns.**

**Ask four questions.**

**One: which of my predictions was most wrong?** Look at it and work out why. **Usually it is because you assumed a pattern rather than checking a base rate.**

**Two: which sources predicted well, and which predicted badly?** You will find this is more stable than you expect — **certain sources are consistently good at forecasting and certain ones are consistently entertainment.** That discovery is permanent and it changes how you read.

**Three: which of my five questions did I skip most often?** If it is always "who are all the actors," that is your specific blind spot.

**Four: did my views change, and did I notice it at the time?**

**That last one is Phase 0's third progress signal, and it is still the one that matters most.** A framework that never makes you update anything is not a framework. It is a belief.

## Making it survive a bad week

**Here is the honest bit, because habits fail and you should know why in advance.**

**You will miss a week. Possibly several.** Everyone does.

**And there is a specific way people handle missing a week that destroys the habit: trying to make up for it.**

**Do not.** The chess course made this point about rating, and it applies here exactly. **Missing one week and then trying to do three weeks at once on Sunday evening is the single most common way a habit dies.**

**If you miss a week, do the smallest possible version the next week. One event, five questions, five minutes.** Not three events and a review.

**And there is a version of this habit that is so small it is nearly impossible to fail: one sentence a day.** "Today I learned that India and China have no agreed border in Sikkim's sector." **That is it. One sentence, in your notes app, most days.**

**That version takes thirty seconds and it will do more for you than a heroic version you abandon.**

## Why this works, when reading clearly did not

**Let me explain the mechanism, because it explains everything in this file.**

**Every piece of content you consume that you do not process is discarded.** This is not a discipline problem. It is how memory works — unrehearsed material decays rapidly.

**So the total amount you retain from any amount of reading is close to the total you wrote down and used.**

**Which means the constraint on this course is not comprehension. It is usage.** And usage is entirely under your control, and entirely independent of how much you read.

**This is genuinely good news, and it is worth sitting with.**

**Because it means the course does not require you to remember much of it. It requires you to use a little of it, repeatedly, on real events.**

**Fifteen minutes a week. That is the whole requirement.**

## What this file is not

**The log is not a diary, and it is not a prediction market.** It does not need to be neat, long, or kept forever. **Its value is entirely in the doing, not the record.**

**And prediction is not about being right.** It is about finding out how you are systematically wrong, because those are the errors you can actually fix. **A log where every prediction came true would tell you almost nothing about your judgement.**

**Nor is this about building a public record.** This should be private. Writing something you intend to publish changes how you write it, and the useful version is the unpolished one — **where you are allowed to be confused, because the confusion is the part worth finding.**
