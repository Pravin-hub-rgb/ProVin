The last file was about holding two views. This is about something sneakier — **framing that makes a claim seem true before you have evaluated it.**

And it contains the single most important idea in this phase.

# 5.3 Propaganda, Spin and Base Rates

## First, a distinction most people never make

**"Propaganda" has become a word that means "anything I disagree with."** That usage has made the term useless.

**So let me define four different things, because they need different responses.**

**1. Disinformation — deliberate falsehood.**
Someone knows it is false and says it anyway, intending to deceive. **This is rare.** It is also real, and it is usually state or campaign-run. **It requires effort and is relatively easy to catch if you know to look.**

**2. Misinformation — falsehood without intent.**
Someone repeats something false believing it is true. **This is far more common than disinformation**, and it is not anyone's fault. **Most false claims you will encounter are honest mistakes, often repeated from a source that got it wrong first.**

**3. Spin — true but framed.**
**This is the dominant form, and it is the one worth worrying about.** Nothing is false. Everything is selected, emphasised and omitted in ways that produce a specific impression.

**4. Domestic messaging — a government talking to its own people.**
**This is not necessarily deception.** Governments genuinely have to explain their actions to voters, and they will present the favourable framing. **The question is whether the framing is misleading in a way that would matter to an outsider deciding what to think.**

**Here is why the distinction matters: disinformation is rare enough that if you can defeat it with reasonable effort, you have solved almost nothing.** Almost all the distortion you will meet is spin — which is much harder to catch, because every individual statement is true.

**And this is why "just check the facts" is inadequate advice.** You can fact-check a sentence and still be thoroughly misled.

## Spin: the specific techniques

**Here is a working list. Learn to recognise them and you will see them constantly.**

**Technique one: the technique of the bare accusation.**
**A claim is stated without evidence, and the framing invites you to supply the emotion yourself.** "Some analysts believe the situation in the region is unsustainable." Which analysts? What is unsustainable? **The vagueness is doing all the work.** You fill the gap with your own fear.

**Technique two: labelling.**
**A person or organisation is given an adjective, and the adjective substitutes for evidence.** "Discredited experts say..." "So-called experts..." **Note that "so-called" is doing the arguing.** If the expert's argument were strong, the label would not help.

**Technique three: the unnamed source that is always reliable.**
**"Senior officials said..." "It is widely known that..." "Sources close to the matter say..."**

**These phrases are sometimes legitimate.** Officials really do brief on background. **But notice that this phrase has no failure mode built in** — a claim from an unnamed source can be repeated forever without ever being checked, because there is nothing to check.

**Technique four: statistics without base rates.**
This is the big one, and it has its own section below.

**Technique five: false balance.**
Covered in file 5.2. **Presenting two positions as equally credible when the evidence is not symmetric.**

**Technique six: the fallacy of the middle ground.**
**The assumption that the truth is always in the middle.**

**This one deserves care because it feels like moderation.** But consider: years of evidence on smoking and lung cancer did not produce a debate between "smoking causes cancer" and "smoking might cause cancer." **The middle ground there is not careful — it is just wrong, and being moderate about it would have cost many lives.**

**Being moderate is not a virtue. Being accurate is.**

**Technique seven: the bystander effect of not checking.**
**The most effective manipulation is not a lie. It is repetition.** Something said enough times, by enough people, in enough places, starts to feel true — regardless of whether it ever was.

**This is not conspiracy. It is how human memory works.** You have a strong sense of what you have heard before and a weak sense of how many times. **Which means the claim you have heard most is not the best-supported claim. It is the most repeated one.**

**Technique eight: asking a question you already know the answer to.**
"A failure of diplomacy — or does China care at all?" **The second half is not a genuine question. It is a rhetorical device that pre-loads the conclusion while appearing to ask.**

**Technique nine: the truth sandwich.**
**A false or misleading claim placed between two true statements.** Because the surrounding statements are true, the whole thing feels verified. **Most people do not consciously check the middle item against the same standard as the outer ones.**

## Now the important idea: base rates

**If you learn one thing from this file, learn this.**

**Base rates are the frequency with which things actually happen. And most of us are badly miscalibrated about them, in a specific and consistent direction.**

**Here is the classic example, and it is genuinely shocking.**

**In the United States, in an average year, roughly 350 people die in air crashes. Roughly 37,000 die in motor vehicle accidents. Roughly 650,000 die from heart disease.**

**Now: which of these do you think gets the most news coverage?**

**Air crashes. By a huge margin. Hundreds of times.**

**Why?** Because air crashes are rare, dramatic, and involve many identifiable people in one event. **Heart disease kills a million people a year, one at a time, invisibly.**

**And here is where this becomes dangerous rather than merely interesting.**

**Because if an air crash happens, it is front-page news. Which means that when you see news about air crashes, you should mentally note: this is a rare event that I am hearing about *because* it is rare.**

**This is the inversion that catches careful people.** A careful reader checks facts, checks sources, checks for framing — **and still gets this completely wrong, because the error is not in any individual fact. It is in the aggregate.**

**The practical consequence: news frequency is not event frequency. And basing your sense of what is dangerous or important on what you read is a systematic error.**

**Let me apply it to something you will care about more.**

**How often does India-China violence occur?** Rarely. The last fatal border incident was 2020. **How often is there a border standoff?** Frequently — several times a year, and it has been going on for decades.

**How often is there terrorist violence in Kashmir?** Frequently enough to be normal, and it has been continuous for decades.

**How often do people die of air crashes in India?** Rarely. Maybe a few hundred a year, often in crashes that kill a few people.

**How often does someone die of a snakebite in rural India?** **Far, far more often. Thousands a year.**

**And here is the trap: the snakebite statistics are almost invisible, because it happens to individuals, continuously, with no news value at all.**

**So: news tells you what is dramatic. Statistics tell you what is dangerous. You need both, and they are not the same thing.**

**One more application, and this is the most politically useful one.**

**How many people were killed in the Israel-Palestine conflict, versus how many were killed in the Russia-Ukraine conflict?** Both large. Both covered. **But the ratio between the two conflicts' death tolls is not the ratio between their coverage, and it is not the ratio between how many countries consider themselves involved.**

**The reason is that wars involving many powerful states get more coverage per death than wars involving fewer.**

**Media coverage tracks geopolitical importance far more closely than it tracks human cost.** That is not a conspiracy — it is what coverage is for. But you need to know it to read properly.

## A practical checklist

**Put this together into something usable.**

**When you read anything geopolitical, ask:**

1. **Is this true?** Check the fact. Not the framing — the fact.
2. **Is this unusual?** How often does this happen? If it is happening constantly, a headline about it is not news.
3. **What is the base rate?** How often does this kind of thing happen, and how many people are involved?
4. **Who benefits if I believe this?** And is that the same as who benefits if it is true?
5. **What is the strongest opposing view?** Can I state it?
6. **Am I the audience?** Is this aimed at outsiders, or at a domestic audience with different interests? **A great deal of what looks like foreign-policy news is domestic politics aimed at a foreign audience.**

**Question 6 is the one people skip, and it is extremely productive.** Because governments do constantly produce messages aimed at other countries to influence them, and these messages are reported in domestic media as though they were policy.

## What this file is not

**Spin is not lying.** Most of what you read is true. **The distortion is in the selection — what is included, what is left out, and what order it appears in.** And "spin" as a word has become an accusation, which means it now tells you nothing about whether the content is misleading.

**Nor is repetition necessarily a conspiracy.** Sometimes a claim is repeated because it is important, not because someone wants to plant it. **The mental shortcut — that anything repeated must be deliberate — is itself a form of paranoia, and it discredits the scepticism you are trying to practise.**

**Nor is "it's just a base rate problem" an excuse to ignore things.** Base rates tell you what is common. **They do not tell you what matters.** A rare event with huge consequences deserves attention precisely because it is rare.

**And most importantly: you will not become immune by knowing these techniques.** Knowing the name of a manipulation does not stop it. **The only defence is slowing down, which file 5.1 built and which this file makes harder — because now you know what you are defending against.**
