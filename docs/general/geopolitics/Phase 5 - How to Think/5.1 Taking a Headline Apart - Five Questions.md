# 5.1 Taking a Headline Apart - Five Questions

## Why headlines are written the way they are

**Before the technique, understand the problem.**

**A headline is not a summary of a story. It is an argument for why you should read the story.** And headlines are written by people whose job is engagement.

**Which means the headline is the most distorted part of the article, and the article is usually the most distorted part of the event.**

**This is not cynicism. It is a design feature of how news works.** Headlines must be short, must be interesting, and must make you feel something. **The three requirements are: short, interesting, emotional. None of them is accurate.**

**So: the headline is the least reliable part of the source, and it is the part you see most.** That is the trap, and learning to see it is most of the skill.

## A worked example, start to finish

**Let me take a plausible headline and take it apart in front of you.**

**The headline:**

> **"China builds new naval base in the Indian Ocean, India helpless"**

**Read that headline and notice what just happened.** You now believe several things you were not told:

1. China has built a **naval base**.
2. It is in the **Indian Ocean**.
3. It is **new**.
4. India is **helpless**.

**Now let me take it apart.**

**Question 1 — What is actually claimed?**

**Strip the framing. What is the literal assertion?** "China has built a naval base in the Indian Ocean, and India cannot do anything about it."

**Two claims. Both testable. Neither obviously true.**

**Note what happened in the stripping.** The word "naval" does a lot of work here. **China has built ports** in the Indian Ocean — that is documented. **A naval base is a different thing**, with different military meaning.

**Question 2 — Where exactly?**

Phase 1 said you should be able to place things on a map. **"The Indian Ocean" is not a place.** It is forty million square kilometres.

**So: which port?** If it is Gwadar, that is Pakistan-administered Kashmir, adjacent to the Strait of Hormuz, and it has been operational since 2007 — **not new.** If it is Hambantota, that is Sri Lanka, under Chinese operation since 2010, on a 99-year lease — **not new.** If it is Kyaukpyu, that is Myanmar, still under construction — **not naval, and not operational.**

**"New" is doing enormous work in that headline, and it may be false.**

**Question 3 — Who are all the actors?**

**The headline names two countries. How many are actually involved?**

If it is Gwadar: Pakistan is a party, because it is Pakistan-administered territory and Pakistan granted it. And Pakistan is a significant state with its own interests that may not align with either China or India.

**If it is Hambantota: Sri Lanka is a party**, and Sri Lanka signed a 99-year lease to avoid a debt crisis. **Saying "China builds a base in the Indian Ocean" erases the Sri Lankan government entirely**, which is the country actually on whose territory it sits.

**Question 4 — What does each want?**

**China:** access for its landlocked western provinces, and overland trade routes that do not pass through Indian waters. It is not primarily about projecting naval power.

**India:** to be the dominant power in its own ocean, and to prevent anyone else's military infrastructure from becoming dominant there.

**Sri Lanka (if relevant):** to get out of a debt crisis, and to avoid choosing between India and China.

**Now the key insight.** Once you state each party's interest, the headline's word "helpless" becomes obviously wrong. **India is not helpless — India has signed access agreements in Oman, Madagascar, Seychelles and elsewhere precisely because it is doing something.** The headline claims helplessness; the actual situation is competition.

**Question 5 — What is missing?**

This is the most valuable question and the one nobody asks.

**What would I need to know to judge this properly?**

- **What kind of facility is it?** Commercial port, dual-use, or military?
- **Which country granted permission, and what did they get in return?**
- **What does India's own public naval posture say about this — not just Indian commentary?**
- **Is there an alternative account of the same facts?**

**And now the crucial point: what has the headline told me about any of this? Nothing.**

**In four minutes you have gone from "China is winning and India is helpless" to "this is a competitive situation in which specific things are happening for specific reasons, and I do not yet know enough to judge it."**

**That is what this file is for.**

## The five questions, properly stated

**Here is the framework, clean, for use on anything.**

**Question 1 — What is literally being claimed?**
Strip the framing words. What would a careful version of this claim look like? **If you cannot state it neutrally, you have not understood it yet.**

**Question 2 — Where, specifically?**
Get the location down to a specific place. **If it cannot be placed, it cannot be evaluated.** And check the date — "new", "just", "now" are the words that most need checking.

**Question 3 — Who are all the actors?**
**Every party with a genuine interest, including ones the story does not mention.** Missing actors are usually the most important ones.

**Question 4 — What does each of them want?**
This is Phase 3's interest framework applied to one story. **If you cannot state every party's interest, you do not understand the story.**

**Question 5 — What would I need to know to judge this?**
What information is missing? **And where would I find it?**

**The value of that last question is that it converts frustration into a task.** Instead of "I don't know enough" you get "I need to check whether this is a port or a military facility" — which is a specific, answerable question.

## The ten-second version

**Realistically you will not do all five questions on every headline.** So here is the compressed version, for when you have thirty seconds.

**Ask three things:**

1. **Where is this happening?** If you cannot place it on a map, you do not understand it yet.
2. **Who benefits if this is true?** If the answer is "whoever is complaining about it", be suspicious.
3. **What is the strongest version of the opposing view?** If you cannot state it, you have not thought about it.

**That third question is the one that does the most work**, because it is the hardest, and because it is the one that most reliably exposes a framing.

**And there is a fourth question for anything that makes you angry.**

4. **Would I feel this strongly if the countries' names were swapped?**

**This is the most reliable test of emotional reaction there is.** If you would be calm about the same event happening to a country you dislike, then your reaction is about the country, not the event. **If you would be equally angry either way, your reaction is about the event, and probably justified.**

## Three specific habits that catch most mistakes

**Habit one: check the date.**

**A huge proportion of misleading headlines are old news with a new urgency.** "India warns China" might be a 2019 statement resurfaces because of an unrelated event this week.

**Before you feel anything, find out when this actually happened.** If it cannot be found, that is itself information.

**Habit two: find the original document.**

**If a claim quotes a document — a statement, a report, a treaty, a satellite image — go and find the document.** Not the news article about it. The actual thing.

**This is habit three of file 5.4 in miniature, and it catches a huge fraction of distortion** because a news article reliably changes the tone of a document even when it does not change the facts.

**Habit three: read the comment section skeptically, and the corrections section happily.**

If an article has a corrections or clarification note near the end, **read it first.** Outlets that correct themselves are usually the more reliable ones.

**And about comments:** they are a sample of who is angry, not of what is true. **Use them to find stories, not to judge them.**

## What this file is not

**Taking a headline apart is not the same as believing the opposite.** The most common result of this exercise is not "the other side is right" — it is "this is more complicated and I did not know that."

**Nor does asking these questions make you immune to being fooled.** It makes you slower, which is the point. **Being slow about a claim is the entire defence**, because most manipulation depends on you reacting fast.

**And none of this means the original claim was false.** "China builds a new naval base in the Indian Ocean" might well be true. **The question is whether you believe it because you verified it, or because it confirmed what you already suspected.**

## Bridge to the next file

You now have a way to take any headline apart.

**But there is a harder skill than that, and it is the one that separates people who read a lot from people who actually understand something.**

**Knowing that a claim is framed is easy. Being able to state the opposing view accurately — well enough that someone who holds it would recognise it — is genuinely difficult.**

**Most people cannot do it. Not because they are stupid, but because they have never tried.**

That is `5.2 Reading Both Sides Properly`.
