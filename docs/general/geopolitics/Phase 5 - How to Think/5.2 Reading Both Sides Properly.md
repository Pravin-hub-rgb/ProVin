The last file gave you a way to take a headline apart. This is the harder skill.

**Knowing a claim is framed is easy. Being able to state the opposing view accurately — well enough that someone who holds it would recognise it — is genuinely difficult.**

Most people cannot do it. Not because they are stupid, but because they have never tried.

# 5.2 Reading Both Sides Properly

## The distinction that most people never make

**Start here, because it is the whole file.**

**There are two completely different things people mean by "both sides."**

**The first is "I take a middle position."** This is almost always a mistake, because it produces a position nobody actually holds.

**The second is "I can state both positions accurately, including the one I reject."** This is a skill, and it is different from being moderate.

**Here is the crucial distinction:**

**"I understand why they did it" and "I think they were right" are two entirely separate claims.**

**A person can have total clarity on why a country acted — the interests, the domestic politics, the constraints — and conclude it was wrong. Those two things live in different parts of the mind and rarely collide.**

**The people who fail at this are not biased in the direction of their own side. They are biased in a subtler way: they think understanding something means accepting it.**

## The Steelman rule

**There is a specific technique here, and it has a name: the steelman.**

**To steelman a position means to state it in its strongest, most serious form — not the version that makes it look foolish.**

**This is the opposite of a "strawman,"** which is what most people do when they discuss something they disagree with: they construct a weak version of the other view and then knock it down.

**Why does steelmanning matter so much?** Because it is a test on *you*, not on them.

**If you cannot state the other side's best case, then one of three things is true:**

1. **You have not understood it.** You have a caricature, not a position.
2. **You are avoiding it.** It is uncomfortable, so you have not actually looked.
3. **It is genuinely wrong.** In which case you now have strong evidence of that, because you tried your best to defend it and failed.

**Note that the third option is now well-earned. Before, you had an assumption.**

## How to steelman, practically

**Here is a procedure that works.**

**Step one: write the other side's argument without using any word that signals contempt.**

**No "obviously," no "of course," no "merely," no "so-called."** Those words are how contempt enters, and once contempt is in, you have stopped trying.

**Step two: include their strongest fact, not their weakest.**

If you are arguing India's position and the strongest fact against India is something specific — you decide which. **If you deliberately pick a weaker counter-argument, you have not steelmanned, you have strawmanned with extra steps.**

**Step three: state it in their vocabulary where you can.**

**This is subtle and it matters.** If someone frames a dispute as "self-determination" and you translate it to "separatism" in your own head before responding, **you have already lost the argument, because you have answered a different claim than the one they made.**

**Step four: say it out loud, in writing, and check whether it feels wrong.**

**It will feel wrong.** That feeling is not information about the argument. **That feeling is the point of the exercise** — it is the sensation of taking seriously something you had dismissed.

**The people who do this well are not the ones who feel comfortable. They are the ones who can act while feeling uncomfortable.**

## Worked example: the Pakistan view of Kashmir

**Let me do this properly, because doing it badly is the failure this file is about.**

**What is the strongest Pakistani position on Kashmir?**

**Stated neutrally, it goes like this:**

The princely state of Jammu and Kashmir had a Muslim majority. **Its ruler acceded to India in October 1947 under circumstances that were disputed even at the time.** The instrument of accession was signed while tribal forces had crossed into Kashmir, and there is genuine disagreement about the sequence.

**After that, an armistice was signed in 1949 which left the region divided.** The United Nations then passed Resolution 8 in 1948 calling for a plebiscite on the future of the princely state, to be held once the fighting stopped. **That resolution has never been implemented.**

Pakistan's position is that a plebiscite is required, that the Line of Control is not a border, and that until it is held the matter is not settled.

**Now — is this the position of every Pakistani, and do I endorse it?** No. Plenty of Pakistanis hold other views. And there are serious problems with the position, which I will come to.

**But that is the steelman. And notice something important happened:** to state it accurately, I had to take seriously that the dispute is not simply about who shot first in 1947. **There is a live, unfulfilled international commitment at the bottom of it.**

**That is what steelmanning does. It finds the thing you were not looking at.**

**And now, honestly: where does the Pakistani position fail?**

- **It relies on treating the 1947 accession as invalid, while the overwhelming majority of states treat it as valid.** That is a serious problem — a position that requires most of the world to be wrong.
- **The plebiscite's feasibility has changed radically.** Seventy years have passed, two generations have grown up under the existing arrangement, and the demographic composition of the region has changed.
- **It offers no workable alternative.** A plebiscite in Kashmir today, in a region where India administers the larger part and the population has changed, is not a simple procedure.

**And that is the honest assessment: the position is coherent and has a real historical grievance, and it has a serious structural problem.** Both of those are true, and holding both is what reading both sides properly means.

## "Both sides" is not always the right frame

**Now the harder and more important part of this file, and it cuts against everything above.**

**Sometimes the evidence really is lopsided, and "both sides" is a way of avoiding that.**

**This has a name: false balance.** Presenting two positions as equally credible when the evidence supports one.

**A serious example, and one where saying "both sides" is genuinely evasive.**

If you are asked "what is the scientific consensus on vaccines causing autism?" and you answer "well, there are some who disagree," **you have not been balanced. You have been misleading, and you have done it in the name of neutrality.**

**Because neutrality has a meaning, and it is not "always give equal weight to claims."** It is "do not have an interest in the outcome."

**And you can have no interest in the outcome while still recognising that one side is better supported by evidence.**

**So how do you tell the difference between legitimate balance and false balance?**

Ask these three questions.

**Question one: is there genuine disagreement among people who are both qualified?**

**On vaccines and autism, the people on one side are not qualified and the people on the other side are. That is not disagreement — it is one side being wrong.**

**On Kashmir, Pakistani and Indian claims about 1947 involve historians with genuine expertise who disagree. That is real disagreement.**

**Question two: is the disagreement about evidence, or about values?**

**If it is about evidence, disagreement resolves over time as evidence accumulates. If it is about values — about how much a nation may prioritise security over self-determination — then the disagreement will not resolve, and that is not a failure.**

**Question three: what is the quality of the sources on each side?**

**"Many people say" is not evidence.** Neither is volume, nor passion, nor repetition. **Check whether each side is pointing at evidence or at other people's opinions of evidence.**

**And here is the honest uncomfortable line:** **you will rarely see false balance applied to the facts you personally hold.** If you believe vaccines work, you do not want "both sides" on that. **So notice that the instinct to balance applies unevenly — which means it is usually serving something other than truth.**

## The one-sentence test

**There is a very simple way to test whether you can genuinely read both sides of something. Try this.**

**Write, in one sentence, the strongest argument against your own view on the issue you feel most strongly about.**

**Then read it back and ask: would someone who holds that view recognise it as fair?**

**If yes — you can read both sides.**

**If no — and this is what almost everyone finds at first — then you have absorbed a position rather than reasoned your way to it.** And that is worth knowing about yourself, whatever you decide to do about it.

## What this file is not

**Reading both sides is not being moderate.** The midpoint between two positions is often wrong, because the evidence is not symmetric. **Fairness is not splitting the difference.**

**Nor is it a substitute for having a view.** You should end up with a view. **The point is that the view should survive steelmanning the other side — not that you should have no view.**

**And understanding is not agreement.** You can explain a country's actions with total precision and still think it was wrong, and you should be able to. **People who cannot do this end up either defending everything their side does, or defending nothing.**

**The most important thing this file is not: false balance is a real and serious failure, and it is usually committed by people who think they are being fair.** Treating "Israel's right to exist" and an armed group's claim as two sides of one question is not balance. It is choosing a framing, while appearing to avoid choosing one.
