# 5.4 Sources and Fact-Checking

## The finding that should change how you read things

**In 2016, researchers at Stanford gave high school, college and graduate students a set of documents, along with professional fact-checking websites, and asked them to verify claims.**

**The result was genuinely surprising.**

**Many students got the answer right — and still could not tell you *how* they knew it.**

**They would reach a correct verdict while relying entirely on the document's appearance and their own memory, rather than by checking against outside sources.**

**And the students who did it well — the ones who got the answer right *and* could explain how — had done something specific. They had stopped reading the document and gone and looked elsewhere. They checked what other sources said about the same claim.**

**That technique has a name: lateral reading.** And it is the most practically useful thing in this file.

**Lateral reading means: when a claim appears in a source, you leave that source and open a new tab to find out what others say about it.**

**Why does this work so well when careful reading does not?**

**Because careful reading is judging the document in front of you — its evidence, its reasoning, its tone.** And that is exactly the thing a well-designed document is optimised for.

**Lateral reading is judging the document by its relationship to everything else.** Which is a completely different task, and one that a manipulative document cannot control.

## A four-step method

**Here is the practical version, and it takes about three minutes per source.**

**Step one — Stop.** Before you read a claim, decide what you actually believe about it. **Write it down.** This takes ten seconds and it is essential. **Without it you cannot tell whether you were convinced or merely agreeing.**

**Step two — Find the source's interest.** Before the content, look at who is speaking and who funds them.

- Is it a government with a policy to defend?
- Is it an organisation funded by a country with an interest in the outcome?
- Does the page disclose funding at all?
- **Is it the outlet's first claim on the topic, or its hundredth?**

**Step three — Go lateral.** Open a new tab. Search the claim itself, in quotes. **Then search the organisation's name plus criticism.** Read what the sceptics say, not just what the supporters say.

**Step four — Read with a specific question.** Not "is this true" but something narrower — "what happened, where, and who was involved?" **Specific questions get answered; vague ones get absorbed.**

**That is the whole method. Three minutes. It is more effective than almost anything else you can do, and almost nobody does it.**

## Where to look, specifically

**You do not need dozens of sources. You need a few reliable ones, checked consistently.**

**On wire services and international news, the most reliable by a wide margin:**

- **Reuters** — the most systematically neutral wire service, and the reason most international news organisations use it. **Start here when you want a fact.**
- **Associated Press** — similar, and strong on domestic US and non-Western coverage.
- **BBC** — reliable, and notably careful about attributing contested claims.
- **Financial Times** — excellent on economics, energy and China, and unusually well-sourced.
- **Nikkei Asia** — the best business journalism on China and East Asia, and independent of both.
- **Caixin** — independent Chinese business journalism, genuinely good and genuinely risky.
- **Rest of World** — technology outside the West, and a model of careful original reporting.
- **The Economist** — good, and openly ideologically leaning. **Read it knowing that, which is why it is worth reading.**

**On India specifically, and honestly:**

- **The Hindu** and **Indian Express** — serious, editorially distinct from each other.
- **Scroll.in** — left-leaning, detailed, and strong on social issues.
- **The Wire** — genuinely high quality, and the best Indian outlet on security, policing and Kashmir.
- **Mint** and **Business Standard** — solid economics coverage.

**On the think tanks — and this category needs care.**

**A think tank is not a neutral institution. They all have positions and funders.** The useful move is not to avoid them but to know whose they are.

**Indian:**
- **Observer Research Foundation (ORF)** — Indian, international focus, publishes named authors.
- **Manohar Parrikar Institute for Defence Studies and Analyses (IDSA)** — Indian, defence focus, formerly the Institute for Defence Studies and Analyses.
- **Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, India** — very strong on nuclear and strategic issues.
- **Centre for Policy Research** — strong on democracy and rights, left-leaning.
- **CSEP** — serious economic research, left-leaning.

**International, and these are genuinely excellent:**
- **Chatham House** — free content, named experts, consistently careful.
- **Crisis Group** — exceptional on field-level conflict reporting.
- **Council on Foreign Relations (CFR)** — strong on US foreign policy.
- **Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI)** — publishes the actual global military expenditure database. **Primary data, not analysis.**
- **Stimson Center** — strong on nuclear and security.
- **SWP (German Institute for International and Security Affairs)** — free, long-form, and consistently the least political of any major Western think tank.

**The criterion that matters more than reputation:** does it publish **named authors** with **disclosed funding**, and does it release **primary data** you can check?

**A think tank that releases data you can independently verify is worth more than a famous one that publishes only conclusions.**

## Videos: use them for orientation, not for facts

**YouTube is genuinely good for geopolitics and genuinely bad for accuracy.**

**Its great strengths:** a map is far easier to understand in motion than on paper. A good presenter can explain a border dispute in twenty minutes better than a textbook can in three hours.

**Its specific weakness:** these videos are made to be watched to the end. That creates incentives that are not about accuracy.

**What works:**

- **Caspian Report** — map-based geopolitics, clear, and unusually balanced for the format.
- **RealLifeLore** — genuinely excellent on why borders are where they are. The best channel for Phase 1's content.
- **GeoWizard** — cartography, borders, and the physical geography underneath politics.
- **CGP Grey** — borders and geopolitics, thoughtful, and willing to complicate.
- **Wendover Productions** — logistics, infrastructure, and how the world actually physically works. **The best channel nobody in geopolitics watches, and it is arguably more useful than most that do.**
- **Practical History** and **Kings and Generals** — military history, properly researched.
- **Thought Emporium** — thoughtful long-form geopolitical analysis.
- **Bloomberg Originals** and **Financial Times video** — business and economics explainers.

**The rule:** **use a video to understand a topic, never to verify a specific fact.** If you need the exact figure, the exact date, or the exact wording of a claim, you need a written source you can quote.

**And the tell for any video:** does it show sources on screen, or does it just assert? And is it about a topic, or about a position?

## The trap nobody warns you about

**Here is the part of this file I think is most important.**

**Your first encounter with any viewpoint is shaped almost entirely by what you happen to see first.**

**You cannot control this. But you can account for it.**

**Research on "prebunking" — giving people a warning about manipulation techniques before they encounter them — shows that it works.** People who have been told in advance to watch for false balance and unnamed sources are measurably harder to manipulate later.

**Which is exactly what this phase has been doing to you.** By teaching you the techniques before you meet them, this course has tried to make you harder to influence than you would otherwise be.

**It does not work completely.** Nobody becomes immune by reading a course. **But the effect is real, and the same principle applies to everything else you learn.**

**Which is an argument for a specific habit:** when you start a genuinely new topic, spend ten minutes finding out how people manipulate narratives about it, before reading any of them.

**Because once you know the terrain, you are much harder to lead.**

## What this file is not

**Being a sceptic is not the same as being uninformable.** Some sources are better than others and you should use the better ones more. **Evaluating everything from first principles all the time is exhausting and it makes you slower, not wiser.**

**Nor is disclosure of funding the same as bias.** A government-funded think tank may be accurate. **An undisclosed funder is a reason for caution, not a reason to dismiss.**

**And being aware of techniques does not make you immune — it makes you slower.** The people most confident of their immunity are the most exposed, because confidence reduces vigilance. **Scepticism you have to work at is worth something. Scepticism you feel automatically protects you from nothing.**
