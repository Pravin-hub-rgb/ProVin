# 1.5 The World's Doorways - Hormuz, Malacca, Bab-el-Mandeb and Suez

## First — what is a chokepoint?

Picture your house. Whatever is inside it, **if the door is locked, nothing gets in and nothing gets out.** The door is the only thing the whole house turns on.

A **chokepoint** is exactly that for a country — except at the scale of the planet.

**A chokepoint is a narrow stretch of water through which a large share of the world's oil or trade must pass.** Close it, and a huge part of the world stops.

**So why are chokepoints needed at all? There is plenty of water — why narrow passages?**

Because the oceans are wide almost everywhere, but in a few places the land pinches in. Those pinch points are chokepoints.

**Here is the clearest example.** Imagine your house has only one door, and you stand in front of it. Everything entering or leaving your house has to pass you. That is a chokepoint.

**Countries near a chokepoint have enormous bargaining power** — far more than their size or their military strength would suggest.

**And one connection to India specifically.** Phase 0 taught that India imports about **85 percent of its oil.** So these chokepoints are not just "the world's problem." They are **India's oxygen supply.**

## Five of the world's most important chokepoints

Here are the five that carry most of the world's oil and trade, and what each one actually does.

### Chokepoint 1 — The Strait of Hormuz (the king of oil)

**This is the most important chokepoint on Earth for oil.**

**Where is it?** It lies between **Iran and Oman**, connecting the Persian Gulf — where the oil comes from — to the Arabian Sea and the Indian Ocean.

**How much oil goes through it?**

Roughly **20 percent of the world's oil** passes through this one narrow stretch, along with about **20 percent of global LNG** (liquefied natural gas).

**And here is the fact that makes it genuinely frightening.** The Strait of Hormuz is the **narrowest major chokepoint in the world.** The navigable shipping channel is only about **2 miles (3 km) wide**, and the strait itself is about **21 nautical miles (39 km)** across.

**So roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes through a channel about 3 kilometres wide**, sitting between Iran and Oman.

**What this means for India.** A large share of India's oil comes from the Gulf, and it passes through Hormuz. So India depends on it enormously — while sitting **very far away from it.** Hormuz is between Middle Eastern countries, not near India.

That is why the Gulf states have mattered so consistently in global politics, despite their modest military power. **They sit on top of the most important chokepoint in the world.**

### Chokepoint 2 — The Strait of Malacca (the king of trade)

**This is the most important chokepoint on Earth for trade.**

**Where is it?** Between the **Malay Peninsula (Malaysia) and Sumatra (Indonesia)**, in Southeast Asia.

**How much trade goes through it?**

A very large share. It is commonly estimated that **a quarter to a third of world trade** passes through this one strait. It is the primary route for trade between Asia and Europe, and between Asia and the Middle East.

**Connect this back to the last file.** File 1.4 ended by saying the Palk Strait leads into the Bay of Bengal, and the Bay of Bengal leads into Malacca. So **Malacca is the endpoint of a route that starts at India's coast.**

**What this means for India.** Most of India's trade with East Asia — China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia — goes through Malacca. So India depends on it heavily, and India is **nowhere near it.** Phase 4 covers how India responds to this problem.

### Chokepoint 3 — The Bab-el-Mandeb (the door to the Red Sea)

This connects the **Red Sea** to the **Gulf of Aden**, which leads into the Arabian Sea.

**Where is it?** Between **Yemen and Djibouti.**

**Why does it matter?** Because the **Suez Canal** lies beyond it. Everything travelling between Asia and Europe through the Suez route must pass through Bab-el-Mandeb first. It is a **double chokepoint** — a gate, and the gateway to the main gate.

**What this means for India.** When ships from India travel to Europe or the Gulf through the Suez route, they use this strait. **That is why "Red Sea attacks" in the news affect India directly** — Indian ships use that route.

### Chokepoint 4 — The Suez Canal (the shortcut that saves the world 10,000 km)

This one is a **canal** rather than a strait, but it functions exactly like a chokepoint.

**Where is it?** In Egypt. It connects the **Mediterranean Sea to the Red Sea.** It is about **193 km long.**

**Why is it so important?** Because it is the **shortest route between Europe (via the Mediterranean) and Asia (via the Red Sea and Indian Ocean).**

**Without the Suez Canal**, ships would have to sail around the entire southern tip of Africa — the Cape of Good Hope — which adds roughly **9,000 kilometres** to the journey.

**Suez saves about 10,000 kilometres of sailing.**

**So when the Suez Canal gets disrupted** — as shipping traffic fell during the Red Sea attacks of 2024 — the world does not stop. Instead, ships take a **much longer route**, shipping companies absorb the extra fuel and time, and the extra cost eventually shows up in the price of goods.

### Chokepoint 5 — The Strait of Gibraltar (the Atlantic's door)

This sits between **Spain and Morocco**, connecting the Atlantic Ocean to the Mediterranean.

**Why does it matter?** Because it is the **only entrance to the Mediterranean Sea.**

**And here is how it connects to Suez.** The Suez Canal, which carries the Asia-Europe trade, empties into the Mediterranean. And to get from the Mediterranean out into the Atlantic — and therefore to America — ships must pass through Gibraltar.

**So the full route from Asia to Europe is:**

```
Asia → Indian Ocean → Bab-el-Mandeb → Red Sea → Suez Canal
     → Mediterranean → Gibraltar → Atlantic → Europe
```

**This is a chain, and every single link in it is a chokepoint.** If one link fails, the whole chain slows down.

## The other chokepoints worth knowing

Those five are the most important, but there are others. Here is the wider list, because building a full mental map of the world's doorways is genuinely useful:

- **Gibraltar** (Spain–Morocco) — Atlantic to Mediterranean
- **Suez Canal** (Egypt) — Mediterranean to Red Sea
- **Bab-el-Mandeb** (Yemen–Djibouti) — Red Sea to Gulf of Aden
- **Hormuz** (Iran–Oman) — Persian Gulf to Arabian Sea
- **Malacca** (Malaysia–Indonesia) — Andaman Sea to South China Sea
- **Sunda Strait** (Indonesia) — Java Sea to Indian Ocean
- **Lombok Strait** (Indonesia) — Bali Sea to Indian Ocean
- **Makassar Strait** (Indonesia) — Celebes Sea to Java Sea
- **Taiwan Strait** (China–Taiwan) — South China Sea to East China Sea
- **Bering Strait** (Russia–USA) — Pacific to Arctic
- **Danish Straits** (Denmark) — Baltic Sea to North Sea
- **Cape of Good Hope** (South Africa) — the alternative to Suez, not a strait
- **Panama Canal** (Panama) — Atlantic to Pacific

**Now notice something.** Look at that list. **India does not appear on it.**

**India has no chokepoint of its own.** It is a peninsula, so it has no narrow gateway through which world trade passes.

**That is a very significant fact.** It means **India depends on other countries' chokepoints** — Hormuz, Malacca, Bab-el-Mandeb and Suez are all located in other countries' waters.

Which is exactly why India's strategy in Phase 4 is about building **partner ports** in Oman, Mauritius, Myanmar and Vietnam, so that there is a backup if one chokepoint closes.

## India's relationship with chokepoints

| Chokepoint | What passes through it | What it means for India |
|---|---|---|
| **Hormuz** | ~20% of world oil | A large share of India's oil comes through here, but it is far from India |
| **Malacca** | ~25–33% of world trade | Most of India's East Asia trade goes through here, but India is far away |
| **Bab-el-Mandeb** | The door to the Red Sea | India-Europe shipping uses it, so Red Sea attacks hit India directly |
| **Suez** | The Asia–Europe shortcut | Saves about 10,000 km on India's route to Europe |
| **Gibraltar** | Atlantic to Mediterranean | The final link in the Suez chain |

**Here is the key realisation, and it is the point of this file.**

**India controls none of these chokepoints.** Every single one of them sits in another country's waters — Iran, Oman, Malaysia, Singapore, Yemen, Djibouti, Egypt, and Spain and Morocco.

**So India's entire maritime trade depends on decisions made by other countries.** And that is precisely why the Indian Ocean has become the central arena of Indian foreign policy.

## What this file is not

**A chokepoint does not mean the country is powerful or weak.** It only means the narrow water is important. Oman and Yemen are not militarily powerful countries, but they sit next to Hormuz and Bab-el-Mandeb, so they are geopolitically essential.

**And closing a chokepoint does not mean the world stops.** Trade gets **rerouted** — it just becomes far longer and more expensive. Countries that close a chokepoint hurt themselves too, which is why it almost never happens permanently.

Also, **not all chokepoints are equally important.** Hormuz for oil and Malacca for trade are the two that matter most. The others are important but secondary. For Phase 1, remember four: Hormuz, Malacca, Bab-el-Mandeb, Suez.
