The first file of this phase was about India's **shape**. Now we get to the question every schoolchild can answer — but which almost nobody thinks about properly.

**Who are India's neighbours?**

The answer is easy. The interesting part is what the answer implies.

# 1.2 Seven Neighbours - Land Borders and Their Lengths

## An easy question with a hard answer

Let's start with the simple one. **How many land neighbours does India have?**

Most people say **seven**. And that is **correct.**

Now try a harder version: **do any other countries have that many?**

This surprises people. **Very few do.** More than half the world's countries have fewer than three land neighbours. And the handful that have seven or more are rare exceptions.

**Why?** Because the number of neighbours is a **geographical accident.** The world's land ended up arranged so that some countries sit stacked on top of each other (Europe), and some are far apart from everyone (America).

And now the thing that is specifically true of India:

**India's neighbours have neighbours too — the seven countries right next to India.**

**And two of those neighbours have nuclear weapons: China and Pakistan.**

## India's seven neighbours

Let us take them one at a time, because each has its own story.

### 1. Bangladesh (to the east)

India's longest **land** border — **4,096 km.** The special thing about Bangladesh is what Phase 0 already told us: **it is surrounded by India on three sides**, which is a direct consequence of the Radcliffe Line.

Bangladesh is one of the most densely populated countries on Earth, and its modern history is unusual because it broke away from Pakistan in 1971 (Phase 2 covers that in detail). So the relationship with India is **complicated — close on one side, and deeply mistrustful on the other.**

### 2. China (to the north and north-east)

**3,488 km** — India's second longest land border, and **the longest and most contested land border in the world.** India and China do not have an agreed border line at all, which is why the LAC (the Line of Actual Control, taught in file 1.3) exists.

China is simultaneously India's largest trading partner and its largest strategic concern. That apparent contradiction is a major theme of Phase 4.

### 3. Pakistan (to the west and north-west)

**3,323 km.** Phase 0 already introduced Kashmir. India and Pakistan have fought **two full wars** (1947–48 and 1965) plus a third in 1999 (Kargil). And **both have nuclear weapons.**

Pakistan is the only neighbour India has actually gone to war with, and that makes it the most emotionally loaded relationship on this list.

### 4. Nepal (to the north, in the Himalayas)

**1,770 km.** Nepal lies almost entirely in the Himalayas. The relationship is genuinely unusual — Nepal's own constitution explicitly states that it does not regard India as a sovereign superior. Phase 4 treats this as an interesting case study.

### 5. Myanmar (to the east, in the north-east)

**1,643 km.** Myanmar used to be called Burma. In Phase 4 we will look at China's "encirclement" strategy, in which Myanmar plays a role.

### 6. Bhutan (to the north-east, in the Himalayas)

**699 km.** Bhutan has the shortest border of India's neighbours. Its relationship with India is **stable and close** — noticeably so.

### 7. Afghanistan (to the north-west — but only a sliver)

**Just 106 km.** And this one is a special case. **Afghanistan does not touch India directly.** This tiny line runs between **India and Pakistan.**

So India has no direct relationship with Afghanistan — it goes through Pakistan. That connects back to the enclave/exclave problem and the Durand Line from Phase 0.

## The full table

Here is all of it at once:

| Neighbour | Direction | Border (km) | Nuclear? | Key point |
|---|---|---:|:---:|---|
| Bangladesh | East | 4,096 | No | Longest; surrounded by India on three sides |
| China | North/NE | 3,488 | **Yes** | The most contested border in the world |
| Pakistan | West/NW | 3,323 | **Yes** | Three wars fought |
| Nepal | North | 1,770 | No | Almost entirely in the Himalayas |
| Myanmar | East | 1,643 | No | Sits between China and India |
| Bhutan | NE | 699 | No | Stable, close relationship |
| Afghanistan | NW | 106 | No | Reached through Pakistan, not direct |
| **TOTAL** | | **~15,106** | | |

Three things to notice while reading that table.

**First: two neighbours have nuclear weapons — China and Pakistan — and both have disputed borders with India.** In a world where two nuclear-armed states sit on either side of you with active territorial disputes, **every decision gets made very carefully**, because the cost of a mistake is enormous. This is one reason India's foreign policy is so cautious and so consistently **non-aligned** (not siding with any major power bloc, like during the Cold War).

**Second: border length and relationship quality have no connection.** The longest border is Bangladesh, and that relationship is distrustful. The shortest is Bhutan, and that relationship is friendly. **Border length is a fact about geography. Relationship quality is a fact about politics.** Never assume one from the other.

**Third: some borders have a formal treaty and some have none.** China has never signed a border agreement with India. That gap matters, and Phase 3 returns to it.

## Position matters more than size

One more idea worth building here, because it will be used constantly from Phase 3 onward: **for each neighbour, India's *position* relative to that neighbour is different — and position, not size, is what actually determines behaviour.**

- **China** sits behind the Himalayas to the north, with the Bay of Bengal in between. It is India's biggest trading partner *and* its biggest military concern.
- **Pakistan** sits to the west with Kashmir in between — the most sensitive relationship, and the only one that has produced wars.
- **Bangladesh** is close and depends on India heavily, but the "enclave" problem and water-sharing disputes sour it.
- **Nepal and Bhutan** sit in the Himalayas, where China's influence is growing. Phase 4 covers this.
- **Myanmar** sits between India and China and is pulled both ways.
- **Afghanistan** is reachable only through Pakistan, which shaped India's decision to support the Northern Alliance in the 1990s (Phase 2 covers this).

**The point: these seven names are not just a list.** The relationships, positions and pressures between them *are* geopolitics. Phase 4 is entirely about this.

## What this file is not

**Having a neighbour does not mean having an enemy.** Nepal and Bhutan are friends. China is India's largest trading partner. Border tells you where a country is, not how it feels about you. Phase 0 said this and Phase 4 will prove it.

And **do not assume "seven neighbours means seven problems."** Most borders in the world are ordinary, unremarkable lines. Only a few — the LAC and Kashmir — are genuinely disputed. The number of neighbours tells you about geography, not about tension.

One more: **Afghanistan is a "neighbour" but has no direct relationship with India.** That is not because India defeated anyone. It is simply that the world's geography put Pakistan between the two of them. **Geography decided this one, not policy.**

## Bridge to the next file

You now know India's neighbours and how long each border is.

But one question remains, and it is the most important question in this phase. The table says the China border is 3,488 km. **But which line is that 3,488 km measured along?**

The same question arises in three more places: Pakistan (Kashmir), Afghanistan (Durand), and China's north-east (Arunachal).

These are the **"red line" borders** — lines drawn by a particular person, in a particular year, which are still disputed today. Radcliffe (1947), McMahon (1914), Durand (1893), and the LAC, which has no date at all.

**This is the heart of Phase 1**, and the next file: `1.3 Red Line Borders - Radcliffe, McMahon, Durand, LoC and LAC.md`.
