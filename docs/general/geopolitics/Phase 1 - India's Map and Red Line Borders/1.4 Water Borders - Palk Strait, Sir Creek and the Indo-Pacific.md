# 1.4 Water Borders - Palk Strait, Sir Creek and the Indo-Pacific

## A foundation worth getting right first

Phase 0 taught us that **there is no line on the ground — only on a map.** And that **ownership rules are different for land and sea.**

This file is about the second half of that, because it is genuinely different, and beginners get it wrong all the time.

**On land**, a country "owns" an area if another country agrees it does (or if it wins a war over it).

**At sea**, nobody owns the sea. But countries do have rights over specific bands of it, and the whole system is built around **distances measured from the coast.**

That is the key idea. Everything below is just an application of "how far out from my coast do my rights go?"

## The sea's three zones

Every coastline in the world has three bands of water coming off it, and each has a different rule.

### Zone 1 — The Territorial Sea (12 nautical miles)

**Every country has water extending 12 nautical miles from its coastline, and this water is its own.**

**How far is 12 nautical miles?** About **22 kilometres.** (A nautical mile is 1.852 km, and it is defined as one minute of latitude — the reason for that is in the next paragraph.)

**This means India's territorial sea** — off Gujarat, off the Bay of Bengal, off the Arabian Sea — **is Indian water.** Another country cannot sail a warship there without India's permission, because India would find it hostile.

Inside this zone, the country has **full sovereignty** — exactly like on land.

### Zone 2 — The Exclusive Economic Zone, or EEZ (200 nautical miles)

From the end of the territorial sea out to **200 nautical miles** (about **370 kilometres**), a country has a much larger band.

The EEZ does **not** mean sovereignty. It means **specific rights:**

- **The right to extract resources** — oil, gas, fish, minerals.
- **The right to manage the environment** there.
- Other countries **cannot** extract resources in that band.

But another country **can** sail through it. If you are in the EEZ of a friendly country, your ships have a right of innocent passage. You cannot stop them, and you cannot build islands there.

**And here is a number that is genuinely striking for an Indian reader.**

India's EEZ is about **2.3 million square kilometres.**

India's land area is about **3.29 million square kilometres.**

So India's sea area is roughly **70 percent the size of its land area.** Not smaller — only 30 percent smaller.

**India's sea is not a thin strip. It is almost as large as its land.**

And there is a specific reason, which connects straight back to file 1.1. Because India is a **peninsula**, it has a coastline on the Arabian Sea *and* on the Bay of Bengal. Those two coasts each generate 200 nautical miles of EEZ, pointing in opposite directions. A country shaped like a landlocked square would have one modest band; India has two.

### Zone 3 — The High Seas

Everything beyond the EEZ. Nobody owns it. It belongs to everyone, which in practice means whoever can project force into it decides what happens there.

Phase 0 made this point: **"no owner" does not mean "no control."**

### The whole thing in one picture

```
      200 nautical miles (EEZ)  |  12 nautical miles (Territorial Sea)
  ─────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────  Coastline
      [High Seas, or someone else's EEZ]
```

**And a critical difference from land borders.** On land, a border is a **line**. At sea, a border is a **distance from your own coast.**

That means sea disputes are almost never about "which line are we standing on." They are about **"how many kilometres from whose coast."** Sir Creek is exactly that kind of dispute, and it is a completely different animal from the LAC.

## Two real water disputes involving India

### Sir Creek (India and Pakistan)

**Sir Creek** is in Gujarat, at the mouth of the Indus river, where the Indus empties into the sea.

It is a small, marshy tidal area, and **both India and Pakistan claim it**, while the actual control does not match the claim.

**Why does this small place matter?**

Because it is not really about the creek itself. It is about **energy.** There is a very large natural gas field right next to it — the Surat gas field. So a tiny sliver of disputed water sits next to something worth a great deal.

**And here is the lesson, which is genuinely confusing if nobody explains it.**

The **Indus Waters Treaty (1960)** settled how the *Indus river's water* would be shared between India and Pakistan. It gave Pakistan the larger share of the river.

**But that treaty was about the river. It was not about where the coastline ends.**

Two completely different questions: *who gets the water* and *where is the boundary in the sea*. The treaty answered the first and left the second open. Sir Creek is still disputed.

### The Palk Strait (India and Sri Lanka)

The Palk Strait lies **between India and Sri Lanka** — between Rameswaram in Tamil Nadu and Talaimannar in Sri Lanka. Sri Lanka's Jaffna peninsula sits on the far side.

<geo-map zoom="indian-ocean" highlight="India, Sri Lanka" labels="India" caption="The Indian Ocean rim. Nearly all of the world's shipping, and much of its oil, moves through water touching these shores." />

**How narrow is it?** At its narrowest, about **72 kilometres.**

**Why does a 72 km gap matter?** Three reasons.

**First: it is a natural border.** The Palk Strait is the natural boundary between India and Sri Lanka — which matters because Phase 4 will look at India's relationship with Sri Lanka, and Sri Lanka is also where India and China have competing interests.

**Second: it is a possible shipping shortcut.** If tankers could use the Palk Strait, the route from southern India to Europe would shorten dramatically — saving more than a thousand kilometres. It is a real possibility rather than current reality, since it is too narrow and too shallow for bulk shipping today.

**Third, and most important: it is a worked example.** The Palk Strait is an Indian example of the principle from Phase 0 — **the most valuable places on Earth are narrow waterways.** File 1.5 looks at the big ones.

## India's islands — and why they are not tourist attractions

While we are talking about sea, one thing must be mentioned, because it is commonly misunderstood.

**India has two important island groups, and they are not holiday destinations.**

**The Andaman and Nicobar Islands** — in the Bay of Bengal, between India and Southeast Asia.

**Lakshadweep** — in the Arabian Sea, southwest of Kerala.

**Why are they strategically important?**

The Andaman and Nicobar islands sit **in the middle of the Bay of Bengal.** That means from them you can observe and influence the route between the Indian Ocean and the Strait of Malacca — the busiest shipping passage on Earth, which is the subject of the next file.

And Lakshadweep sits in the Arabian Sea, close to the routes that connect India to the Gulf and Africa.

**These islands are a perfect example of Phase 0's fourth dial.** They have almost no land, almost no resources, and almost no population. What they have is **position** — and position, we established, is the most powerful dial there is.

Phase 4 returns to this as part of the "String of Pearls" strategy.

## What "Indo-Pacific" means

Now the term you will see everywhere in the news.

**"Indo-Pacific" simply means the Indian Ocean plus the Pacific Ocean.**

The Indian Ocean is where Asia meets Africa and Australia. The Pacific is the largest ocean, between Asia, Australia and the Americas. Together they cover the waters surrounding almost every country that matters to India.

**But is this a geographical term or a political one?**

**Both.** It is an area name *and* a political idea.

The political part is this: **several countries, particularly the United States, use the term "Indo-Pacific" to describe a region where they want to increase their own influence.** When America talks about the "Indo-Pacific strategy," it means its own strategy for that region.

**And the connection back to Phase 0.** Phase 0 said position is the most powerful dial. That is exactly why this term matters to India: **India is at the centre of this area.** The peninsula is in the middle — connected to Asia, and sitting between two oceans.

That is why India is described as an "Indo-Pacific power." Not because of its size, but because of where it is.

## What this file is not

**The fact that nobody owns the sea does not mean nobody controls it.** Phase 0 made this point and it is repeated here. Countries control their territorial seas and their resource rights in the EEZ, and in the high seas, control goes to whoever can project force.

**And "sea borders" are not the same thing as "land borders."** This is the most common mistake people make. A land border is a **line** that both sides agreed to. A sea border is a **distance measured from your own coast.** Because they work differently, they produce different kinds of disputes — Sir Creek is a sea dispute about distances, and the LAC is a land dispute about actual control. Mixing them up will confuse you completely.
