# 1.6 Small India - Sikkim, Arunachal and the Chicken's Neck

## A joke that contains a serious point

Imagine you are showing a friend your home. But the house has an extension — an extra set of rooms — and the only way to reach it is down a very narrow lane.

Now think about what happens if that lane is blocked. The entire extension is **cut off**. Nobody gets in, nobody gets out.

**"Chicken's Neck" is exactly this — except for India.** And it is not a joke at all.

## What the North-East is, and why it is different

Let us start with the basics.

**Mainland India** is the big connected part — Uttar Pradesh down to Kerala.

**North-East India** is a separate group of **eight states:**

- Assam
- Arunachal Pradesh
- Manipur
- Meghalaya
- Mizoram
- Nagaland
- Sikkim
- Tripura

And **these eight states connect to mainland India through one narrow strip of land.**

That strip is called the **Siliguri Corridor**, or the **"Chicken's Neck."**

**And here is what makes it narrow.** The corridor is bounded on three sides by other countries:

- **Nepal and Bhutan** to the north-west
- **Bangladesh and Myanmar** to the south-east

So it is a strip of Indian land with foreign territory pressing in on it from three sides.

## How narrow is it?

**Very narrow.** It is commonly described as roughly **60 to 70 kilometres wide.** (Sources vary somewhat — you will see figures between 40 and 70 km — but the idea is that this is a very thin lane.)

**To picture it:** 60–70 km is wide enough that a large city would fill it. So this is a lane just wide enough for a road and a railway line — not wide enough for a large military force or a big airport to sit comfortably.

**This is India's own chokepoint.** File 1.5 covered the world's external ones (Hormuz, Malacca, Bab-el-Mandeb, Suez). This one is **internal** — and it is arguably just as important to India.

## Why the Chicken's Neck is so important

Three reasons, and all three matter.

### Reason 1 — It is the only connection

**If this strip is blocked, the entire North-East is cut off from mainland India.**

That means electricity, food, medicine and everything else that eight states and their tens of millions of people need would have to come through this one strip.

**And it is a bottleneck.** A narrow lane is fragile. If something happens on it — a flood, a blockade, a protest, a conflict — the effect spreads across everything.

**That is why this corridor is so sensitive to India.** It is not just a road. It is the **lifeline** of the North-East.

### Reason 2 — China sees it as a window

Now an important geopolitical point. **China has looked at this corridor as an opportunity.**

China has the Belt and Road Initiative — a huge trade route running from China towards Europe. And one of its routes runs through this region.

**And for China, this corridor is strategically useful.** If it had influence over the route here, it would gain a road that runs inside India.

**This is why the Chicken's Neck has become a contested zone** — with Chinese influence, Indian influence and local politics all operating at once.

### Reason 3 — It is a result of history, not geology

And here is the deepest point. **This corridor was not made by nature.**

It is not a geological feature. It is a **historical accident** that dates to the **1950s** — the period when India and China were still negotiating the status of Tibet and the North-East Frontier Agency. India administered this area and connected it with a narrow strip. (The full story is in Phase 2, because it is tied to the origins of the India-China relationship.)

**This point matters**, because Phase 0 taught us: *geography decides, policy decides.* **This is a case where policy created geography.** A political decision produced a strip of land that is now a permanent strategic fact.

## Two North-East states worth knowing: Sikkim and Arunachal

### Sikkim — the smallest state, in the strangest position

**Sikkim** is India's **smallest state** by area, and it became part of India in **1975** (Phase 2 has the detail).

**Why is it special?**

- **It sits on a "tri-junction"** — the point where **Nepal, Bhutan and China (Tibet)** meet. Three countries come together in one small area.
- It has a very small population, sits in a beautiful mountainous region, and includes **Kanchenjunga (8,586 m)**, India's highest peak.

**What does the tri-junction mean geopolitically?** It means a tiny state is the meeting point of three countries' borders. Small area, but it is where three strategic interests collide. This is also where Chinese pressure is strongest, because Tibet rises up from that direction.

### Arunachal Pradesh — the biggest state, and the most disputed

**Arunachal Pradesh** is India's **largest state** by area (about 83,743 km²).

**But here is the important thing: a significant part of Arunachal Pradesh is claimed by China.**

In file 1.3 you met the **McMahon Line** — drawn in 1914, accepted by British India, never accepted by China. That line runs through Arunachal Pradesh.

So Arunachal has **two claims on it:**

- **India says:** "This is our state, and we control it."
- **China says:** "This line runs here, and we do not accept it, so most of this is ours."

**And here is the ground reality that matters: India has controlled the whole area since the 1962 war** (Phase 2 covers that in detail).

**So Arunachal is the single most important example in this course of the difference between a claim and control.** Remember that from Phase 0 — this is it, in the real world.

**You will also hear the name "Tawang" in the news.** Tawang is a district in Arunachal Pradesh, and it was one of the locations of the 2020 India-China clashes, just as Galwan Valley in Ladakh was.

**In one line: Arunachal Pradesh is the live case where a claim, a line that is not accepted, and actual control all disagree.**

## One more thing about the North-East — and it connects to everything

There is a pattern in the North-East that appears constantly in the news and that most people do not understand.

**The North-East feels isolated.** And this is not a mood — it is geography and economics together:

- The terrain is extremely difficult, so building roads and railways is slow and expensive.
- The economy is weaker than the rest of India.
- Because of the Chicken's Neck, the area depends on one thin route out.

**And when an area feels cut off, something predictable tends to happen.** You will hear about **separatist demands** — groups who argue that the region should be a separate state.

**This is the direct link to Phase 0.** You learned there that geography shapes politics. **Here is geography producing politics in real time:** isolation created by terrain and a narrow corridor feeds directly into demands for separation.

Phase 4 examines this properly, because India's internal geopolitics is a major topic.

## What this file is not

**"North-East India" does not mean it is less important than mainland India.** Tens of millions of people live there, it is strategically critical, and it is arguably India's most vulnerable internal region.

**And do not assume the Chicken's Neck is India's weakest point in every sense.** It is genuinely fragile — but it is also India's most important internal connection, and keeping it secure keeps the whole region secure.

**Nor is it fair to say "this is all China's doing."** The Chicken's Neck's vulnerability is historical (a 1950s decision), geographical (it is surrounded by Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Myanmar), and political (local demands plus outside influence). China is one factor in a much more complicated picture.
