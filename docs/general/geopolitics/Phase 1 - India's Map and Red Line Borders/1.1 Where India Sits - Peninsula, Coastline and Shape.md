# 1.1 Where India Sits - Peninsula, Coastline and Shape

## An odd question to start with

Imagine someone from another family asks where you live. You would answer "Delhi," or "Mumbai," or "Kolkata."

But if they asked **"where on Earth?"** your answer would change. You would say "South Asia," or "the continent of Asia," or "in both the northern and the eastern hemisphere."

Now suppose they ask: "What does India look like?" What comes to mind?

For most people, a long thin shape pointing downward. Or something that looks like a triangle standing on one corner.

That guess is correct. And this entire file is about one thing: **why India's shape matters.**

## Where India is, in exact numbers

Let us start with coordinates, because Phase 0 gave you the numbers and now we will see what they mean.

- India runs from about **8°4′ north to 37°6′ north**.
- And from about **68°7′ east to 97°25′ east**.

The meaning of that is simple but worth stating clearly. India sits in **one of the very few parts of the world where land actually exists.** Remember from Phase 0 that about 71 percent of the planet is water. India is one of the exceptions, and everything interesting in Indian geopolitics happens because of it.

And here is the number that surprises people most: **India's coastline is about 7,516 kilometres long** (including islands). That puts India in the top five coastlines in the world.

But the number that matters more than the length is this: **India is surrounded by sea on more than one side.**

There are two different oceans next to India. On the west is the **Arabian Sea**, touching Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka and Kerala. On the east is the **Bay of Bengal**, touching West Bengal, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu.

**Two oceans. Two sides. And that makes India a peninsula.**

## What "peninsula" means

**A peninsula is a piece of land that has water on three sides and connects to more land on the fourth.**

Picture a bowl of water with a piece of land dipping into it. The piece of land is surrounded by water and attached to the shore. That is a peninsula.

India is a peninsula, and it is one of the large ones in the world.

## Why being a peninsula matters so much

This may not sound dramatic yet, but it is the most important concept in this phase. Read carefully.

Being a peninsula means India has **a mountain wall on one side** (the Himalayas in the north) and **open water on the other three**. Those three sides of water give India four separate advantages.

### Advantage 1 — a free natural wall

Suppose you need a wall around your country so nobody can walk in. And you have no money.

What do you do? You point to the mountains in the north and say "the Himalayas will do."

**That is a natural wall.** Peaks over 8,000 metres. No army walks over those. Only a handful of mountaineers a year go there.

The consequence is that **India gets its northern security almost for free**, which is why India's strategic focus has always been east and west — because in the north, the mountains defend themselves.

### Advantage 2 — two trade routes

Being a peninsula means India has **two separate oceans**, and therefore two ways to reach the rest of the world:

- **West, through the Arabian Sea** — to the Gulf, to Europe, to America.
- **East, through the Bay of Bengal** — to Southeast Asia, to China, to Japan, to Australia.

Now imagine India was bordered by sea on only one side. All of its trade would funnel through a single gateway, and the day that gateway closed, India's trade stops.

**Being a peninsula gives India two routes.** If one closes, the other can take over. That is a backup, and backups are valuable.

This is also why India matters so much to global shipping even though it owns no chokepoint — the world's busiest east-west shipping route passes through waters that touch India's coast.

### Advantage 3 — fishing, water and shipyards

Water on three sides means **fish**, clean seawater for cooling, and **shipyards** close at hand. Fishing communities and ship-building industries naturally grow up on a coast.

### Advantage 4 — but it is also a weakness

Here is the honest part. A peninsula is **not only strength.**

If there is water on three sides, there is also an approach route on three sides. Today, with ships, missiles and submarines, nothing stops an approach the way a mountain range does.

**So a peninsula is harder to defend than a landlocked country.** That is exactly why India's navy matters so much, and why the Indian Ocean is the centre of Indian foreign policy in Phase 4.

## India's extreme points

Every country has its most northern, southern, eastern and western points. India's are worth knowing, partly because people enjoy them and partly because they are genuinely interesting.

**Most southern — Indira Point.** This is on Great Nicobar island. Notice that it lies **slightly south of Kanyakumari**, which is the "southernmost tip" everybody knows about. Kanyakumari is the southernmost *mainland* point; Indira Point is actually further south because it is on an island.

**Most northern — Indira Col.** In Ladakh, high inside the Himalayas. Almost nobody alive has been there.

**Most eastern — Kibithu**, in Arunachal Pradesh. **Most western — Ghuar Mota**, in Kutch, Gujarat.

Now notice something. These four extreme points are all in **the most crowded and contested parts of India.** North is the Himalayas and China. East is Arunachal and Myanmar. West is Pakistan and the sea.

**India has no quiet side.** Every direction faces a neighbour, a line, or the ocean.

## The Himalayas — the wall in the north

Phase 1's first file would be incomplete without properly understanding the Himalayas, because they are the centre of everything in this phase.

**The Himalayas are the highest mountain range on Earth** — effectively three parallel ranges running west to east.

The facts that matter for this course:

- **Height:** the highest peak is **Everest (8,849 m)**, on the Nepal-China border. India's own highest is **Kanchenjunga (8,586 m)**, in Sikkim.
- **They separate two worlds.** Standing on the summit of Everest, you are between sky and earth in a way that changes everything — temperature, air, language, culture. North of the range is the Tibetan plateau, one of the highest and most isolated places on Earth.

**And here is the geopolitically crucial point.** In Phase 0 we learned that natural borders are politically calmer, because both sides can point at the same mountain and agree where the line is.

The Himalayas is more complicated than that, in one specific way: **it is not a line, it is a range, and it has passes through it.**

A **pass** is a gap or low point in a mountain range — a place where you can actually walk from one side to the other. The Himalayas has several. **And that is precisely why a separate line was needed: the McMahon Line and later the Line of Actual Control.** Which is the subject of file 1.3.

## How big is India?

A few numbers, for context:

- **Area:** about **3.29 million km²** — the **seventh largest** country in the world.
- That is a little over **two percent** of the world's land surface.
- **Population (2023):** over **1.4 billion** — the most of any country. India passed China in 2023.

Now let me connect this back to Phase 0. **India is the seventh biggest country and the most populous. That does not mean it is the most powerful country.** Phase 0 taught that size is the weakest of the four dials.

India's actual strength comes from its **shape** — the peninsula, two oceans, a mountain wall — and from its **position** in the Indian Ocean. Those are what this phase is about.

## Twenty-eight states and eight union territories

A quick aside, because it comes up in the news and confuses people.

A **state** means a division of India with its own government — Maharashtra, Kerala, Punjab. A **union territory** means it is administered directly from the centre, like Delhi.

**India currently has 28 states and 8 union territories.**

These numbers change, and one change matters for this course. In **2019, Jammu & Kashmir was reorganised** into two union territories: **Jammu & Kashmir** and **Ladakh**. So J&K is no longer a state.

This matters because **Kashmir is one of the central topics of Phase 1**, and you need to know what J&K actually is today before reading about it.

## What this file is not

**Being a peninsula on its own achieves nothing.** Plenty of peninsulas are not powerful, and plenty of powerful countries are not peninsulas. A peninsula offers a *possibility*, not a guarantee — the possibility has to be converted into real strength through a navy, ports, trade and industry. Phase 4 looks at how India did that.

And it is wrong to assume "long coastline means powerful country." A long coastline means there are many places from which you *can* be attacked, which is both an advantage and a liability.

The most common mistake of all is "big country equals powerful country." Phase 0 already covered this. India is big, but that is not why it matters. Its shape and position are.
