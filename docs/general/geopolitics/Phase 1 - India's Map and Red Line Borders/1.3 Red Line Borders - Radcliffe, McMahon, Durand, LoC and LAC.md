# 1.3 Red Line Borders - Radcliffe, McMahon, Durand, LoC and LAC

## First — what even is a "line"?

Here is a foundational point that gets missed, so let us clear it up.

**There is no line on the ground.** There are rocks, trees, rivers and mountains. A line exists only **on a map** — on paper, drawn in ink, with a ruler.

So does that line exist on the ground too? Sometimes. There is a border wall on the US-Mexico border, and it is being built right now — that one is physically real. But in many places there is nothing. There is no wall between India and China in the Himalayas. There is no fence. There is just an **agreement** — two governments who have agreed where the line goes, which may or may not match what each of them would prefer.

**And that leads to a distinction that is the core idea of this entire file.**

A **border** means a line that **both sides have agreed to.**

A **Line of Control** or **Line of Actual Control** means a line that exists on the ground between two countries' forces but that **the two sides have not fully agreed on.** It is really a ceasefire line — "this is where the soldiers stopped."

**This difference matters enormously.** While a conflict is running, there is a line on the ground between the opposing soldiers, and both sides know that it is not the real border — it is only where things are *right now*.

## Five lines, five stories

### Line 1 — The Radcliffe Line (1947) — the one that created India

This is the most consequential line in Indian history. Without it, there is no India and no Pakistan.

**What happened?**

When British rule was ending in 1947, two things had to be decided:

1. How to split British India into separate countries.
2. Where the border between each new country would run.

**Neither was easy.** Because for six hundred years the British had governed India as one unit, so "India's internal borders" had never actually existed. There was no historical precedent for the question.

The British government sent **one lawyer** — **Sir Cyril Radcliffe** — and set up boundary commissions to draw the lines.

**Now the most important fact in this file.**

Radcliffe arrived on **8 July 1947**. His award was published on **17 August 1947**.

**He did the entire job in about five weeks.** And the job decided which country millions of people would live in.

**Radcliffe himself had never visited India.** He had never seen the land. He worked entirely from maps and reports.

The result: the Radcliffe Line divided British India into **India and West Pakistan**. And when Bangladesh was created in 1971, the same process was applied again to draw a new line between East Pakistan and Bangladesh.

**Why is this line "red"?** Because:

- It was drawn in **five weeks**.
- The people drawing it **had never been there**.
- It separated **millions of people from their own families and villages**, without asking them.
- And it remains a sensitive subject between India and Pakistan today.

### Line 2 — The Durand Line (1893) — Afghanistan and Pakistan

This is the oldest line, and the strangest case.

**What happened?** In 1893, as British India began extending its control northward, the British Foreign Secretary — **Sir Mortimer Durand** — reached an agreement with the Emir of Afghanistan, **Abdur Rahman Khan**.

That agreement drew a line between Afghanistan and British India. It is called the **Durand Line**.

**Here is the problem.** The line was drawn between **Afghanistan and British India.** Afghanistan believed it was agreeing to a boundary with British India.

Then India became independent. What happened to the line?

India **inherited** it — India accepted that this line was now the boundary between **Pakistan and Afghanistan**.

**And then a very serious problem emerged: Afghanistan has never officially recognised this line.** To this day, Afghan politicians and, more recently, the Taliban leadership have said the line was "unfair" and that Afghanistan "lost" by accepting it.

**What this means:** the line is a real fact on the ground, but one of the parties has never said "yes." It is a case where a boundary was drawn and a promise was never completed, because the rulers and the times changed.

The **Durand Line is roughly 2,640 km** long, running from the Pamir mountains to the Iranian border. For Afghanistan it is enormously important, because there is no other route out (Phase 4 covers this).

### Line 3 — The McMahon Line (1914) — Arunachal and Tibet

This one is confusing because it was drawn with **Tibet**.

**What happened?** In 1914, British India signed an agreement with Tibet at the **Simla Convention** (held in Simla, in present-day Himachal Pradesh). The parties to that agreement were:

- **British India**, and
- **China**, which at the time was treated as representing Tibet, because Tibet had by then declared itself part of China.

That agreement drew a line called the **McMahon Line**, between British India and Tibet.

**Two things are crucial here.**

**First: British India accepted the line.**

**Second: China did not.** And China has still not accepted it today.

So the line was accepted by one side and never accepted by the other. When China declared in 1949 that Tibet was part of China, the entire line became disputed.

This is the origin of the dispute over **Arunachal Pradesh**, and the point should be stated as plainly as possible:

**A significant part of Arunachal Pradesh is claimed by China, and India exercises control over it.**

This is the live example of the "claim versus control" distinction from Phase 0, and it is not theoretical.

### Lines 4 and 5 — The LoC and the LAC — the two lines in Kashmir

Now we come to the two lines that cause the most confusion, because they are in the same region but govern different areas.

**First, the picture.**

Kashmir came to India in 1947. But during the **first war of 1947–48**, part of that territory came under Pakistani control, and the line has never been settled since.

There are two different lines:

**The Line of Control (LoC)** divides the part of Kashmir administered by Pakistan — which Pakistan calls Azad Jammu and Kashmir and Gilgit-Baltistan — from the part administered by India.

- It is **only a ceasefire line.** It came into being after the 1947–48 war.
- It is **not a recognised international boundary.**
- It is **not settled.** Both sides still argue about what should ultimately happen.

**The Line of Actual Control (LAC)** lies between **India and China** in the Himalayas, and it is **the most unusual border on Earth.** It gets its own explanation below.

## The LAC — the most unique border in the world

If one thing in this file deserves your full attention, it is the LAC.

**The basic fact:** there is **no agreed border** between India and China. Not one like the US-Canada border, where both sides signed a treaty and surveyors marked it on the ground.

**What "Line of Actual Control" actually means:**

- For **India**, it means "the line beyond which India exercises control."
- For **China**, it means "the line beyond which China exercises control."

**And these two lines are not the same.**

India says: "The LAC runs here, and everything beyond it is ours."
China says: "The LAC runs here — a bit further over — and everything beyond that is ours."

**Two different lines. Both called "our LAC."**

And here is the crucial part: **the LAC was never "drawn" in the normal sense.** It is not a signed treaty. It is not marked on the ground with pillars. It simply reflects **where each side's soldiers are currently positioned.**

**The LAC has two sectors.**

**The western sector (Ladakh and Aksai Chin).** Here the line runs along the highest Himalayan crests. This is the sector where the two countries' soldiers are physically close to each other, and where the **2020 Galwan Valley clash** happened (Phase 2 covers that in detail).

**The eastern sector (Arunachal).** Here the **McMahon Line** runs, the one agreed in 1914 that China does not accept.

**To visualise the LAC, remember that both sides describe different lines.**

- **India's LAC** — in Aksai Chin, the line runs along the Himalayan crest, where India holds the higher ground.
- **China's LAC** — in Aksai Chin, the line runs somewhat further west, where China holds the ground.

The two descriptions differ because **the two sides control different portions of the same plateau.**

## Two more things about the LAC

**First: the LAC is not demarcated on the ground.** There is no stone, no pillar, no fence saying "the line starts here." It is only an understanding between two sides whose soldiers happen to be in particular positions. **Which means it can move.** If positions change, the line changes.

**Second: Aksai Chin is a special case worth understanding.**

Aksai Chin is a **high-altitude plateau** — very cold, very thin air. China has held it since the 1962 war, against India's claim (Phase 2 covers that). And China has built a major highway through it connecting Xinjiang to Tibet.

**This road matters to India**, because beyond Aksai Chin lies China's own Tibet. A road that gives China secure overland access to Tibet is a strategic concern for every country on India's side of the Himalaya.

This point is advanced, but hold onto it: **Aksai Chin is a place where China holds territory that India claims, and is using it to connect Xinjiang with Tibet.** Phase 2 and Phase 4 both return to it.

## All five lines at a glance

| Line | Year | Between | How it was made | Status today |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Durand Line** | 1893 | British India ↔ Afghanistan | Mortimer Durand agreed it with the Afghan Emir | Afghanistan has never officially recognised it |
| **McMahon Line** | 1914 | British India ↔ Tibet | Signed at the Simla Convention | China does not accept it; underpins the Arunachal claim |
| **Radcliffe Line** | 1947 | India ↔ West Pakistan (then Bangladesh) | Cyril Radcliffe, five weeks, without visiting India | Still sensitive between India and Pakistan |
| **LoC** | 1949 | India ↔ Pakistan (Kashmir) | Ceasefire line after the 1947–48 war | Not settled; a ceasefire line only |
| **LAC** | (never signed) | India ↔ China (Himalaya) | Defined by actual military control, never marked | The world's most unique disputed border |

## One deeper point — these five lines are a family

Now look at all five together, and notice a pattern.

**Some were drawn by the British** — Durand, McMahon, Radcliffe. These are "colonial" lines.

**Some were created by wars** — the LoC and the LAC. These are "ceasefire" lines.

**And not one of them is complete.** None is fully agreed, none is fully rejected. Every single one has a grey zone.

There is also one thing all five have in common: **the argument is always about who owns which piece of ground** — and the argument is almost always about **peaks and ridges.** That is because, as Phase 0 taught us, a mountain range is not a line, it is a belt. So the real question is always "which ridge?"

**And the final point: these five lines are only India's.** This is the most complicated border geography in the world. Almost nowhere else do you find this many critical, unfinished, disputed lines in one place.

So whenever you hear "the India-China border dispute" or "the Kashmir issue," remember: **it is not one line.** It is two or three separate lines — Radcliffe, the LoC, and the LAC/McMahon — each with its own completely separate history.

## What this file is not

**"Line" and "border" do not mean "correct."** Every line was drawn by somebody. The question is never "is this line wrong?" The question is **"who drew this line, and who has accepted it?"** That distinction matters, because a lot of commentary collapses the two, and the moment somebody says "this border is wrong," the argument is usually over before it starts.

**"The LAC is not a line" also does not mean "there will never be an LAC."** It means it currently reflects **today's control**, not an agreement. That is not the same as saying it will change.

And one very common mistake: **"the LAC and the McMahon Line are the same thing."** They are not. The McMahon Line is the **1914 line**, which China does not accept. The LAC is **today's actual control line**. They are related but they are not the same, and the LAC's western sector has nothing to do with the McMahon Line at all.
