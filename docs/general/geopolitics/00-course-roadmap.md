# Geopolitics — From Zero to Daily

## First — what does "geopolitics" even mean?

Let us break the word apart, because that is usually the fastest way to understand anything.

**Geo** means the earth, the land, the physical side of things. You see it in *geography* (the study of land), *geology* (the study of the earth's layers). **Politics** means power — who decides, who rules, who gets their way. And politics itself has another word inside it that you already use every day — **policy**, meaning the decision about how things should go.

So **geopolitics** simply means: **the game of power played out on the land.**

Or think of it as a cricket match. If you looked only at the players, you would miss the most important part of the game. The real story is which team has more space on the field, how many players it has, who is bowling right now, and where the pitch is worn out. **That is the geography. And the geography decides who wins.** Geopolitics is exactly that — except the field is the world, the players are countries, and the match is called history.

Now one honest thing before we go further. Most people learn geopolitics by starting with today's news. That is a mistake, and it is the single most common one.

**News is the end of geopolitics, not the beginning.** By the time something reaches a headline, the interesting part is already over. The decision has been made. Who wanted what, who could block it, which mountain made it possible — all of that is settled by the time you read the headline. So if you start there, every story is an isolated island with no bridge to the last one.

The fix is to build the bridge first. **Learn the map, then read the news.** After that, every headline will feel like a puzzle piece dropping into a slot you already have.

## One idea that will feel strange at first

People often say things like "these countries are the good ones" or "that country is the bad one." **That language does not work in geopolitics, and understanding why is the single most valuable thing in this whole course.**

Here is a simple way to see it. Imagine two friends, and both of them want ice cream. One shop sells more ice cream than the other. Is the second friend a bad person? No. He is doing exactly what makes sense from where he is standing.

This is called **interest** — meaning your own advantage. When you read a headline saying "country X is against country Y," your first reaction should be: **"what is X's interest here?"** Not "which one is right." That single shift changes everything.

We will build this idea slowly across several phases. Right now just hold onto the idea that countries act from their own position, and that their position usually makes their actions understandable, even when you disagree with them.

## The course route — 7 phases

The course is broken into seven phases. (You can read "phase" as just "part".) Each phase builds one real thing.

| Phase | What you will learn | Why it has to come here |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase 0** | The world map. Continents and oceans. What a country actually is. How borders are made. How to read a map | Nothing else makes sense until you know what the things on a map are |
| **Phase 1** | Where India sits. Its seven neighbours. The "red line" borders. The world's most important waterways | You cannot understand India's foreign policy before you understand the land it operates on |
| **Phase 2** | The timeline, from colonial rule to today — wars, independence, the Cold War, 1991, the rise of China | Every current dispute is leftover from something that happened earlier |
| **Phase 3** | Interest instead of morality. How power works. Alliances, blocs, sanctions | This gives you the vocabulary to describe what Phase 2 produced |
| **Phase 4** | India versus China, Pakistan, its neighbours, Russia and America, the Indian Ocean, and India's internal politics | Your own country as the unit of analysis |
| **Phase 5** | How to take a headline apart. How to hold two opposing views. How to spot propaganda. Keeping a weekly log | The skill that makes everything else self-sustaining |
| **Phase 6** | Channels, think tanks, reading lists, a daily routine, and AI prompts for catching up | Turns knowledge into a habit you can actually maintain |

**Phases 0 and 1 are the heaviest** in terms of raw information, and that is on purpose — they build your vocabulary. Phase 2 is the memory you reach for when someone asks *why* things are the way they are. Phases 3 and 4 are where the real analytical work begins. Phase 5 is the part people skip, and it is the only part that genuinely separates informed people from loud ones. Phase 6 is logistics.

## Your daily twenty-minute routine

Most people quit this subject not because it is boring, but because they treat it like social media — forty-minute bursts followed by three days of nothing. That does not work, because geopolitics has no quick rewards. It builds slowly and there is no moment where it feels like something happened today.

What works is a small, fixed routine. Twenty minutes, most days. The exact shape matters less than the fact that it never changes.

1. **Five minutes — scan.** Open one reputable news source and read headlines only. Do not click anything. Notice how many you could not place on a map.
2. **Five minutes — place.** Pick the one story you could not place and spend five minutes finding where it happened and who is involved. **This single habit is what builds the map in your head.**
3. **Seven minutes — one long thing.** A video essay, a think-tank article, or a chapter of a book. **One thing finished beats five things skimmed.**
4. **Three minutes — write two lines.** What did you learn, and what still do not understand?

That last step is the one people skip, and it is the one that makes the rest permanent. Writing "I still don't understand why the Line of Control is where it is" turns a gap into a question. And a question is something you can actually go and answer. A vague feeling of confusion is not.

If you miss a day, that is completely fine. **What is not fine is trying to repay a missed day by reading eighty minutes the next day.** Missing a day is normal. Trying to catch up is exactly how people quit in week three.

## How you will know it is working

Geopolitics is unusually hard to measure, because unlike chess there is no rating and no final result to reveal whether you improved. Three things are worth watching, and none of them is a number on a screen.

The first is how often you can place a headline on a map without searching. At the start, almost none. After a few months, most major ones. That shift is the clearest evidence that a framework is forming.

The second is whether you can explain an opposing view accurately. Not sympathetically — accurately. If you can say why an Indian, a Pakistani, a Chinese and an American reader each read the same event differently, without making any of the four sound stupid, then your understanding is real. If you can only argue one side, you have absorbed a narrative rather than learned a subject.

The third is whether you have changed your mind about anything recently. This is the hardest one to notice and the most reliable one. A framework that never makes you update anything is not a framework. It is a belief.

## An honest note about the maps in this course

Every map in this course is drawn from public geographic data, and every map dataset in the world encodes *some* country's version of the world. Where borders are disputed — and several of India's neighbours are disputed — a map is showing one claim, not the truth.

This is not a flaw I can engineer away, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. So it is stated plainly instead: **where a map and a disputed claim disagree, trust the text and treat the map as a rough guide.**

You will meet this exact issue again in Phase 1 with the Line of Actual Control, which is the purest example in the world of a border that physically exists on the ground and is agreed by nobody at all. We will look at that in detail when we get there.

## What this course is not

This is **not a prediction course.** Nobody can reliably tell you what will happen in Kashmir or at the Strait of Hormuz next year, and anyone who sounds confident is selling something — attention, access, or a worldview. If you finish this course able to make confident predictions, you have learned the wrong thing.

It is also **not a course about which country is good or bad.** Geopolitics runs on interest, not morality, and that is the most useful idea in the subject. A country doing something you think is terrible may be acting perfectly rationally from its own position, and understanding *that* is not the same as approving of it. You are allowed to both understand something and object to it.

It is **not a history course either.** Phase 2 contains only enough timeline to explain the present, not enough to make you a historian. The test for whether a historical fact belongs here is simple: if knowing it would change how you read a current headline, it stays. If it would not, it goes.

And it is **not a replacement for primary sources.** The think tanks, channels and publications listed in Phase 6 are your next step, not something this course replaces.
