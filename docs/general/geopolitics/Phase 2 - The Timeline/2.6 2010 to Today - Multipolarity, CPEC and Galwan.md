# 2.6 2010 to Today - Multipolarity, CPEC and Galwan

## The world after 2010 — "multipolar"

Let us start with a word you will keep hearing: **multipolar.**

**"Polar" means two poles.** The Cold War world was **bipolar** — two superpowers, the United States and the Soviet Union, and everyone else had to pick a side.

**"Multipolar" means many poles** — several centres of power, and no single one that dominates.

**So what changed?** The Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, but the United States remained the only superpower. **That unipolar moment lasted roughly until 2008.**

Then several things happened at once:

- **China's rise** gave the world a second pole of genuine power.
- **India** became a large economy and began asserting itself more.
- **Europe** was economically huge but politically weak.
- **Russia** recovered from its 1990s collapse and became assertive again.
- **Regional powers** — the Gulf states, Turkey, Brazil — became more influential.

**And this is where you should be careful.** "Multipolar" is a description, not a settled fact. It means "the world is becoming more complicated and less dominated by one power." It does not mean "the United States is no longer powerful" — America remains by far the largest military power and the largest economy by some measures.

**But the difference matters, because in a unipolar world there was essentially one decision-maker.** In a multipolar world, a single country like India cannot be moved against the wishes of others.

**And for India specifically, that is exactly what happened — starting around 2014.**

## The message from abroad — "Act East"

**Around 2014, India's leaders began talking openly about an "Act East" policy.** The message was simple: **the centre of the world's attention was moving to Asia, and India had to move with it.**

**Why did this matter so much?** Because for the previous twenty years, the world's attention had been elsewhere — on Europe, then on the United States, then on China. India was, diplomatically speaking, a spectator.

**In 2014, several things happened at once that put India at the centre of everyone's attention:**

**First, the United States began to pay attention to the Indo-Pacific region**, and announced a "rebalance to Asia" policy.

**Second, the Indian Prime Minister visited Japan as the first foreign dignitary.** And in 2016 India and Japan signed the first nuclear agreement between India and any other country.

**Third, in 2015, India signed the** **China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC)** agreement.

**Now — CPEC is where we get to something important, because it looks contradictory.**

## CPEC — the corridor through Kashmir

**"CPEC" means the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor.** It is a large infrastructure project — roads, railways, power plants — running from China's Xinjiang province, through Pakistani-controlled Kashmir, to the Arabian Sea at Gwadar in Pakistan.

**Now look carefully at that route.** It runs from **China, through **disputed territory that India claims**, to the Arabian Sea.

**And India's reaction was immediate and hostile.** India called it a "violation of its sovereignty" and formally protested.

**Why was this so sensitive?** Let me connect it to Phase 1.

Recall the **Chicken's Neck** — the narrow 60–70 km strip through which everything must reach the North-East. Recall that **Kashmir is the disputed territory** where India and Pakistan have fought multiple wars.

**So CPEC is a corridor running through disputed territory, built by China, with India on the wrong side of it.**

**And here is the crucial point that is easy to miss.**

**Phase 1 taught us that the world's most valuable real estate is not fertile land — it is the narrow waterways through which oil and trade must pass.** CPEC is that idea applied to land.

**China's western provinces — Xinjiang — are landlocked.** Everything China imports or exports in the west must travel either overland through the Himalaya, or **across the Arabian Sea through Gwadar and the Indian Ocean.**

**And the Indian Ocean is exactly where India sits.** So CPEC is, whether or not it is described this way, **a Chinese project designed to bypass India in India's own ocean.**

**That is why India protests, and why this is genuinely strategic rather than merely a sovereignty objection.**

## India and the United States — a relationship that changed

**Now the other side of this. Because India was now being courted by the United States.**

**And there is history here that needs stating, because it explains the shift.**

**India had been close to the Soviet Union for decades.** The Soviet Union was India's main supplier of defence equipment from the 1950s to the 1990s. India did not have a defence relationship with the United States at all until **2005**, when a ten-year defence cooperation agreement was signed.

**Then things moved fast.**

- **In 2005**, the US–India defence agreement. This was the first significant military cooperation between the two countries ever.
- **In 2008**, India signed a nuclear civil agreement with the United States, opening India's civilian nuclear sector to foreign technology.
- **By 2016**, the United States had publicly described India as a "major strategic partner."
- **In 2019**, India granted the United States access to Indian military bases for logistics and refuelling.

**Now — what does this mean strategically?**

**First: the relationship is real but limited.** India is a **strategic partner**, not an **ally.** India is not in any military alliance with the United States. There is no common defence treaty. The word "ally" would be misleading.

**Second: this makes India more secure — up to a point.** If the United States takes a closer interest in Indian security, that is a form of deterrence. But it also means India has become a potential **counterweight to China**, which is partly what the United States wants.

**Third — and this is the honest part — a country that is courted by everyone is sometimes courted by nobody.**

**Because India's biggest asset for decades has been its non-aligned position.** Everyone, both the United States and China, wants India on their side. And because India has refused to join any alliance, it has been able to hold both.

**But that independence has a cost.** When India needs urgent help — investment, technology, defence equipment — it has to negotiate with both sides, and it gets less than either would give an ally.

## 2020 — Galwan Valley

**This is the most important recent event in this timeline, and it needs to be explained properly.**

**In June 2020, Indian and Chinese soldiers physically fought at Galwan Valley, in Ladakh.** **Soldiers died on both sides.** Chinese soldiers died and their bodies were not immediately returned; India said it was only after verification that they were handed back.

**And this was the first time Indian and Chinese forces had physically killed each other since 1975.** Fifty years.

**What actually happened?**

This is important, because the popular version of this story is wrong in an instructive way.

**Both India and China had been building military infrastructure in Ladakh on their side of the LAC.** Each was building **roads, fuel depots and observation posts** in the area — and both felt they were doing so on their own side of the line.

**The area was contested.** The LAC in the western sector is not marked on the ground — Phase 1 explained why. **So both sides had built structures in places where each believed it was inside its own line, and which the other considered outside.**

**So both sides were building a village-type post, at an area called Pangong Lake, built about 100 metres apart. And then there was a physical clash.**

**What happened on the night of 15 June 2020 is that an Indian patrol went to the higher ground above the Chinese post, where a Chinese patrol was already present. And a violent physical fight broke out.**

**India's version was that Chinese soldiers used weapons against Indian soldiers who were unarmed.** **India's version was that Chinese soldiers came to an area where Indian soldiers were, and that Chinese weapons were used. China's version was that the Indian side was the aggressor.**

**Both sides' versions of who went there first are still disputed. But both sides accept that the two patrols came into physical contact, and that a fight happened.**

**So it was an unplanned confrontation in an undemarcated area, between soldiers who did not want a war, at a line neither had agreed.**

## Why this mattered so much

**It changed the relationship permanently, in four ways.**

**First, the trust went.** After 2020, both India and China distrust each other. That is different from before, when there was competition but also engagement.

**Second, the economic relationship was damaged.** Indian apps were banned in China, Chinese investment in India was effectively frozen. This came after a long period of growth in trade.

**Third, both sides built up their forces along the LAC.** And **for the first time, the LAC had actual markers.** After 2020, both sides agreed to mark the line on the ground, in some sectors.

**Fourth — and this is the part worth sitting with —** **this made India and the United States closer.** Because a country that faces a military threat from one side, from another side becomes less attractive as a partner. **That is not a good thing, and it is why Phase 4 spends time on India's "multi-alignment" strategy.**

**And the phrase used in India at the time captures it exactly: "At ease with no one."** Which means, being comfortable with no one.

## What "multipolar" means for India

Now the payoff. **Why did the world become multipolar, and what does India have to do with it?**

**Here is the direct connection.**

**Since the early 2000s, India's economy has grown at a rate no country has matched.** India is now the world's fifth largest economy, and the third largest in Asia.

**And the world's attention followed the money.** When India's economy grew, the world's great powers began to pay attention to India. Not out of kindness — because in a multipolar world, you cannot ignore a country that is growing.

**But here is the catch, and it is the strategic problem India faces today.**

**In a unipolar world, a small state could survive by being on the side of the one superpower.** It did not have to worry about being caught between two.

**In a multipolar world, that option disappears.** Because there is no single superpower any more. **India is now being courted by both the United States and China at the same time.** And in that situation, there is one thing that matters above all: **India's own strategic choices.**

**India's position in the world today is almost unique.** It has:
- A large economy (fifth in the world)
- A huge population (over 1.4 billion)
- A young population (a demographic advantage that will last decades)
- A location (India's own Ocean, surrounded by chokepoints)
- Nuclear weapons
- Democratic government
- Ties with both the United States and China

**No other country has this exact combination.** And that is why, in a multipolar world, India is more strategically important than at any point since 1947.

**But that cuts both ways.** Because a multipolar world is also a world where **India is genuinely important to the United States and China — which means it is genuinely contested by both.**

## What this file is not

**Multipolarity does not mean the United States is no longer a superpower.** America remains by far the largest military power. It means there is no longer one power that can decide things alone.

**Nor does it mean the bipolar world is over and the new one is settled.** Multipolarity is a description of a trend, not a fixed state. The exact number of poles is itself contested.

**Nor was CPEC built to "humiliate India."** It was built for commercial reasons — to move goods from landlocked Xinjiang to the sea. But the fact that it bypasses India in India's own ocean makes India's objections strategic rather than merely formal.

**And the 2020 Galwan clash was not planned by either side.** It was an unplanned physical confrontation in an undemarcated area, between soldiers who did not want a war. Both sides' accounts differ on who started it, and both accept that soldiers died.

**And India's strategic position is not simply an advantage.** Being courted by both superpowers means being on the wrong side of both. A country that refuses to commit, when decisions are being made, gets less than an ally would.
