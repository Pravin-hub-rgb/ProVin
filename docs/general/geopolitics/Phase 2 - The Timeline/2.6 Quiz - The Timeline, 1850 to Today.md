# 2.6 Quiz - The Timeline, 1850 to Today
## MCQ

Q: What made it possible for the East India Company to conquer India?
A. It had the largest army in the world
B. It used profits from trade to fund an army, which conquered territory that generated more profit
C. Indian armies refused to fight at all
D. Britain had discovered a superior weapon
> B. The self-reinforcing cycle is the key: trade financed an army, the army conquered territory, territory produced more revenue, and the revenue bought a bigger army. Britain did not so much conquer India as finance its own conquest using profits from India.

---

Q: How did the East India Company defeat the much larger Indian armies?
A. Through superior military training
B. By bribing divisions within enemy coalitions and paying for enemy officers to stand still
C. By using cavalry charges
D. By persuading rulers to fight other rulers without bribery
> B. The battle of Plassey in 1757 is the clearest example — the Company won in hours because it had bribed the rival commander's officers. Money, not firepower, was the decisive weapon.

---

Q: What changed for India in 1858 after the Revolt of 1857?
A. British rule ended completely
B. The East India Company was abolished and the British Crown took direct control
C. India became an independent republic
D. Partition of India was announced
> B. The 1857 revolt ended Company rule permanently. The Crown took direct control in 1858, and India became the British Raj administered from London. The revolt was crushed; only the governing institution changed.

---

Q: What was the most important fact about the Durand Line and McMahon Line?
A. Both were drawn before 1857
B. Both were drawn during the period of direct British Crown rule, in 1893 and 1914
C. Both were drawn after Indian independence
D. Both were drawn by the United Nations
> B. Both dates fall inside the Crown-rule period. Britain drew lines across the Himalaya in 1893 and 1914 for reasons of imperial control rather than where the land actually belonged — which is why Afghanistan has never recognised one and China has never accepted the other.

---

Q: What was the alliance system, and why did it make a local conflict into a global one?
A. An economic agreement that raised prices
B. Defensive promises among European powers that meant a local conflict automatically drew in others
C. A military training programme
D. A colonial trading company
> B. The Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente had promised to defend each other, so a French-German argument over Alsace-Lorraine drew in Britain, Russia and others. Alliances convert small conflicts into global ones, which applies today as much as it did in 1914.

---

Q: How many Indian soldiers served in the First World War, and why?
A. About 50,000, as volunteers from an independent India
B. About 1.5 million, because India was under British administration and had no government that could refuse
C. None, because India stayed neutral
D. About 10 million, as a sign of India's power
> B. India was a territory under British administration, and its soldiers served in the British army. No Indian government existed that could have said no — and that inability to refuse is exactly what the independence movement spent thirty years attacking.

---

Q: What was satyagraha?
A. A military strategy
B. Non-violent resistance through marches, fasts and boycotts, deliberately accepting punishment
C. A financial boycott of British goods only
D. A legal case fought in British courts
> B. Satyagraha meant "holding firmly to truth," practised as non-violent resistance. It worked because it could not be suppressed by force — when thousands march calmly and refuse to move, a policeman cannot solve the problem by hitting them.

---

Q: Why was the partition of Punjab so much more violent than Bengal's?
A. Punjab was geographically larger
B. Punjab's population was genuinely mixed everywhere, so no line could divide it cleanly
C. Punjab had more British troops
D. Punjab was not partitioned at all
> B. In Punjab, Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs lived in the same villages, so the dividing line had to cut through mixed populations. That is why so many people ended up on the wrong side, and why the violence was far more intense.

---

Q: Roughly how many people died in the partition of India in 1947?
A. About 20,000
B. Somewhere around a million, with estimates from 200,000 to over 2 million
C. About 5 million
D. About 20 million
> B. Estimates range from about 200,000 to over 2 million, with most historians near 1 million. The uncertainty exists because the killing happened in a chaotic period and records were destroyed. It is reasonable to say "somewhere around a million."

---

Q: Why was the Chicken's Neck created by a decision made in 1905?
A. A religious council drew a line
B. The British partitioned Bengal for administrative convenience, and that line determined the corridor
C. China drew it
D. It is a natural formation
> B. The 1905 partition of Bengal was made on administrative rather than religious grounds, and was reversed in 1911. But that line determined where the Siliguri corridor runs — so a bureaucratic decision from 1905 still shapes Indian strategic geography today.

---

Q: What was Non-Alignment?
A. India refusing to trade with anyone
B. India refusing to join either Cold War bloc while keeping relations with both
C. India declaring military neutrality in all wars
D. The Non-Aligned Movement's founding members
> B. India refused to join either the US-led or Soviet-led bloc and helped found the Non-Aligned Movement in 1961. But it kept ties with both — this is the key to understanding what followed.

---

Q: Was India strictly neutral during the Cold War?
A. Yes, India took no side at all
B. No — India signed the Indo-American Treaty of Alliance in 1954 and kept relations with the Soviet Union
C. India was formally a member of NATO
D. India supported the Soviet bloc exclusively
> B. India signed a mutual defence treaty with the United States in 1954 and visited Moscow in 1955. It was non-aligned in the sense of refusing to let any single power define it, not in the sense of refusing everyone.

---

Q: What did India actually lose in the 1962 war with China?
A. All of the territory north of the Himalayas
B. Aksai Chin and thousands of square kilometres in Ladakh
C. The whole state of Jammu and Kashmir
D. Nothing — India won the war
> B. India lost Aksai Chin, a high plateau in Ladakh. China had already built a road through the area linking Xinjiang to Tibet, and taking control of it gave China control of where the LAC runs. But India did not lose territory in the Himalayas itself.

---

Q: Why did the 1962 war happen so quickly against India?
A. India was fighting a two-front war
B. India's forward positions were unprepared and its Defence Minister dismissed warnings that China would attack
C. India had no army in Ladakh
D. China used nuclear weapons
> C. India had assumed the line could not be used for anything except a claim on paper. Its forward positions were poorly fortified and its leadership did not believe an attack was coming, so the Indian army was caught off guard. This is Phase 1's "claim versus control" with real consequences.

---

Q: What happened at the Simla Agreement in 1972?
A. India annexed more territory in Kashmir
B. Both countries agreed to accept the Line of Control, and Pakistan accepted that Kashmir was part of India
C. The two countries agreed to end all dialogue
D. Britain mediated a new partition
> B. The Simla Agreement accepted the LoC as permanent in exchange for both sides respecting each other's territorial integrity — meaning Pakistan accepted that Kashmir was part of India. It is the reason there has been no full-scale India-Pakistan war since 1971.

---

Q: What made the 1971 war's outcome contradictory for India?
A. India lost territory to China the same year
B. India won decisively in the east, creating Bangladesh, and was defeated in the west by Pakistan's nuclear bombs in the same week
C. India refused to accept the ceasefire
D. China intervened on Pakistan's side
> B. India created Bangladesh with a decisive military victory, and in the same week Pakistan used nuclear weapons as bombs against Indian airfields, killing around 130 Indian pilots in a single afternoon. Two opposite results in the same week is what made 1971 decisive.

---

Q: What were the three motives for India's war in 1971?
A. Land, oil and religion
B. Humanitarian (refugees), strategic (preventing a corridor through Indian territory), and military
C. Trade, empire and prestige
D. Only humanitarian
> B. All three applied simultaneously. Ten million refugees had arrived, West Pakistan had offered a corridor across East Pakistan to reach allied troops, and the Indian army expected a quick victory — which it got.

---

Q: What was a "nuclear stalemate," and what did it produce?
A. Both countries refusing to build nukes
B. Neither side able to fight a full-scale war, so both fought below that threshold
C. A formal peace treaty signed in 1972
D. The end of the Kashmir dispute
> B. After 1971 both sides had nuclear weapons, so neither could win a full war. That pushed both toward proxy conflict, infiltration and military coups — which is why Kashmir news often involves non-state actors rather than armies.

---

Q: Why did Pakistan test nuclear weapons in 1974?
A. To attack India
B. Because India had demonstrated in the 1971 war that it could win a conventional war decisively
C. At British request
D. Because of the Simla Agreement
> B. Pakistan concluded from India's 1971 victory that only nuclear weapons could guarantee its survival. India tested three months later, beginning a bilateral arms race between the two neighbours.

---

Q: Why did Vajpayee declare India a nuclear weapons state in 1998?
A. To threaten China
B. Because Indian tests had triggered a financial crisis and sanctions threats, and declaring the status put the pressure back on Pakistan
C. At the United Nations' request
D. Because Pakistan had tested first
> B. The rupee crashed and foreign investment fled after the January 1998 tests. By declaring India a nuclear weapons state publicly, Vajpayee shifted the pressure onto Pakistan rather than accepting it — and the sanctions threat evaporated.

---

Q: When did India sign the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty?
A. 1963
B. 1974
C. 1998
D. 2008
> D. India signed in 2008 as a recognised nuclear weapons state. Until then it was outside the treaty entirely, having tested in 1974 and 1998 and refused to accept the bargain of giving up nukes in exchange for civil nuclear power.

---

Q: Why did India face a balance-of-payments crisis in 1991?
A. Because it imported 85 percent of its oil and oil prices had risen sharply
B. Because India spent too much on defence
C. Because the Soviet Union stopped trading with India
D. Because of a drought in the Punjab
> A. India imports roughly 85 percent of its oil, so it cannot pay when prices rise. This is a direct consequence of the resource position Phase 0 described — India's oil price is decided in other countries' waters.

---

Q: What was the most significant thing about the 1991 reforms?
A. India became a fully free-market economy
B. The licence raj was abolished and foreign investment opened up, but democracy, land ownership and the planning system were left intact
C. India adopted socialism
D. India returned to colonial-era trade rules
> B. India's reforms were partial and gradual. Trade and investment opened up substantially, but the public sector, land ownership and the five-year plans survived. "India became free market" is not accurate, and anyone who says otherwise has not read the details.

---

Q: Why did the Kargil War happen in 1999?
A. Pakistan wanted to annex all of Kashmir
B. Because the Line of Control was undemarcated in that sector, so there was no agreed line to respect
C. Because India attacked first
D. Because of the nuclear tests
> B. The Kargil area had no posts on the LoC and no agreed demarcation. The crisis came out of an unmapped boundary. Afterwards, the Lahore Declaration committed both countries to negotiation, and that dialogue channel has been maintained since.

---

Q: Why did China become the world's factory?
A. Because of foreign aid
B. Because of Deng Xiaoping's reforms from 1978 and joining the WTO in 2001, which ended Western import restrictions
C. Because it discovered oil
D. Because it conquered Japan
> B. Two decisions explain almost all of it: economic reform beginning in 1978, and WTO membership in 2001 which removed Western trade barriers and made Chinese manufacturing globally competitive. Once you know those two facts, the rest follows.

---

Q: Why did China's rise create a security problem for India?
A. Because China invaded Kashmir
B. Because China's rise made it shipping-dependent, and it built ports across the Indian Ocean to secure that dependence
C. Because China stopped buying Indian goods
D. Because China's economy is larger than India's
> B. As the world's factory, China depends on oil and raw materials moving by sea through Hormuz and Malacca. That dependence pushed it to build ports in Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and Djibouti — ports that sit in India's ocean.

---

Q: Why is India's oil dependence its biggest strategic vulnerability?
A. Because India has no navy
B. Because it imports 85 percent of its oil from the Gulf, all of it through a strait in another country's region
C. Because its oil fields are empty
D. Because India does not have enough storage
> B. India imports far more Gulf oil than China does, has no control over Hormuz, and has no alternative suppliers who could replace it. That combination makes India's oil supply its single most exposed strategic dependency.

---

Q: What does "multipolar" mean?
A. That the world has many time zones
B. That the world has several centres of power rather than one or two
C. That the world is divided into many countries
D. That no country is powerful
> B. Multipolar describes a world with several centres of power — the United States, China, India, Russia, and regional powers — rather than the two poles of the Cold War. It does not mean the United States is no longer a superpower.

---

Q: What is CPEC?
A. A free trade agreement between China, Pakistan and Ecuador
B. A China-built economic corridor from Xinjiang through Pakistan-administered Kashmir to the Arabian Sea
C. A Chinese naval base in the Indian Ocean
D. A climate change agreement
> B. CPEC runs from landlocked Xinjiang through disputed territory to Gwadar on the Arabian Sea. Its commercial purpose is real, but it also gives China a way to move goods without passing through Indian waters, which is why India treats it as strategic rather than merely a sovereignty objection.

---

Q: What happened at Galwan Valley in June 2020?
A. China annexed Ladakh
B. Indian and Chinese soldiers fought physically and soldiers died on both sides, in an undemarcated area
C. Both sides signed a peace treaty
D. The Indian army captured the Chinese post
> B. It was the first time Indian and Chinese forces had killed each other since 1975. It happened in an area where the LAC is not marked on the ground, and both sides' accounts of the sequence differ while both accept that soldiers died.

---

Q: Why was the Galwan clash an accident rather than a planned war?
A. Both sides wanted to negotiate
B. Both sides were building posts they believed were on their own side of an unmarked line, and soldiers came into contact
C. China wanted to test India
D. The weather caused it
> B. Both India and China had been building roads, fuel depots and observation posts, each believing it was on its own side of the LAC. Because the line is not marked, both had structures in places the other considered outside. That is how an unplanned confrontation happened between soldiers who did not want a war.

---

Q: Why did India's position become more important after 2008?
A. Because India's military grew dramatically
B. Because India's economy grew fast enough that the world's powers could no longer ignore it
C. Because India discovered oil
D. Because India joined NATO
> B. In a world moving towards multipolarity, a country growing at India's rate becomes strategically significant by default. This is why "Act East" began around 2014 — the world's attention was moving to Asia and India needed to move with it.

---

Q: What is India's "multi-alignment" strategy?
A. Joining multiple military alliances
B. Being friendly with the United States, China and others at the same time without joining any alliance
C. Only aligning with Japan
D. Endorsing one side in every dispute
> B. India deepened ties with the United States and Japan while keeping relations with China, without joining any alliance. The benefit is options and leverage; the cost is that India gets less from any single partner than a formal ally would.

---

Q: Why is India being courted by both superpowers a problem as well as an advantage?
A. It means India must pick a side
B. It means India is genuinely contested by both, and a country that refuses to commit gets less when decisions are being made
C. It means India is weak
D. It means both superpowers are friends
> B. India benefits from both wanting its friendship, but it also becomes a strategic objective for both. "At ease with no one" is the phrase used in India to describe the position — and it cuts both ways.

---

## TrueFalse

Q: The East India Company was an ordinary trading business with no special powers.
- True
- False
> False. Its 1600 charter gave it the right to use the British Crown's armed forces in Asia, which made it a trading business with its own private army backed by the state.

---

Q: Britain conquered India mainly through superior military strength in fair battles.
- True
- False
> False. Britain's decisive advantage was money — profits from trade, better guns, and the ability to bribe divisions inside enemy coalitions. At Plassey in 1757 the decisive factor was that officers had been paid to stand still.

---

Q: The revolt of 1857 ended British rule in India.
- True
- False
> False. The revolt was crushed and British rule continued for another ninety years. What it ended was the East India Company — direct Crown rule began in 1858.

---

Q: India's borders and wars were part of the wider Cold War.
- True
- False
> True. Pakistan was allied with the United States, which did not want a defeat, so when India fought Pakistan it was partly fighting a superpower's ally. And the superpowers both courted India because its non-aligned position gave it leverage.

---

Q: The First World War made British governments eager for another one.
- True
- False
> False. It made them desperate to avoid one. Appeasement in the 1930s was a rational response to a generation that had just watched millions of die, even though it turned out to be a catastrophic mistake.

---

Q: The Second World War could have been a fight between Britain and the Soviet Union rather than against Hitler.
- True
- False
> True (it was a real possibility, though it never happened). In 1939 Britain, France and the Soviet Union were negotiating a military alliance against Germany. The talks collapsed, and Stalin then signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact with Hitler.

---

Q: The atomic bombs were the reason Japan surrendered.
- True
- False
> False. The Soviet invasion of Manchuria in August 1945 was decisive militarily. What the bombs changed was the future — no government would dare start a major war against another major power again.

---

Q: The Radcliffe Line divided Punjab into a Muslim half and a Hindu half.
- True
- False
> False. Punjab's population was genuinely mixed everywhere, so no line could divide it cleanly. The line had to cut through mixed villages, which is exactly why so many people ended up on the wrong side.

---

Q: The Chicken's Neck dates from a decision about administrative convenience, not religion.
- True
- False
> True. The 1905 partition of Bengal was made on administrative grounds, not religious ones. That line determined where the corridor runs, so a bureaucratic decision still shapes Indian strategic geography.

---

Q: India's non-aligned policy meant it refused all alliances and had no defence ties with anyone.
- True
- False
> False. India signed the Indo-American Treaty of Alliance in 1954 and had a defence relationship with the United States from 2005. Non-aligned meant refusing to let any single power define India, not refusing everyone.

---

Q: India signed the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty in 1998.
- True
- False
> False. India signed it in 2008, as a recognised nuclear weapons state. It had tested in 1974 and 1998 and stayed outside the treaty in between.

---

Q: The 1991 reforms were an ideological conversion to free-market economics.
- True
- False
> False. They were a response to a genuine economic crisis. Manmohan Singh deliberately framed them as economic necessity rather than ideology — a good example of policy driven by interest rather than belief.

---

Q: After 1991 India became a fully free-market economy.
- True
- False
> False. The reforms were partial and gradual. Democracy, land ownership, the five-year plans and the public sector as an employer all remained.

---

Q: The Kargil War in 1999 happened partly because the Line of Control was not demarcated in that area.
- True
- False
> True. There were no posts on the LoC in that sector and no agreed line, which is why the crisis happened at all. The Lahore Declaration afterwards committed both countries to negotiation.

---

Q: China's economic rise made it more dependent on secure sea lanes.
- True
- False
> True. As the world's factory, China imports most of its oil and raw materials by sea through Hormuz and Malacca. That dependence is why it has built ports across the Indian Ocean rim.

---

Q: India imports a higher share of its oil from the Gulf than China does.
- True
- False
> True. China's Gulf dependence is about 45 to 50 percent of its imports, India's is much higher — well over 80 percent. That makes India more vulnerable in a Gulf crisis, not less.

---

Q: "Multipolar" means the United States is no longer the world's biggest military power.
- True
- False
> False. Multipolar describes the number of centres of power, not the decline of any one of them. America remains by far the largest military power; it simply no longer decides everything alone.

---

Q: The Galwan clash of 2020 was a planned invasion by China.
- True
- False
> False. It was an unplanned physical confrontation in an undemarcated area, between soldiers who did not want a war. Both sides' accounts of who arrived first differ, and both accept that soldiers died.

---

Q: India and the United States have a formal mutual defence treaty.
- True
- False
> False. The two are "strategic partners," which is not an alliance. There is no common defence treaty. India has deliberately stayed outside any alliance, which is why it can work with the United States while also staying in contact with China.

## ShortAnswer

Q: Why did Britain take two hundred years to conquer India, and what does that tell you about how empires actually work?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because it did not conquer India by force alone. It built a self-reinforcing cycle: trade profits bought an army, the army conquered territory, territory produced more revenue, and the revenue bought a bigger army. It also avoided fair fights by bribing divisions inside enemy coalitions — at Plassey in 1757 the decisive moment was won by paying officers to stand still. The general lesson is that empires rarely win by being simply stronger. They win by finding the divisions inside others and funding them.

</details>

---

Q: Partition killed roughly a million people in five months. Why does that number matter geopolitically today?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because it created a permanent reason for two nuclear-armed neighbours to see each other as an existential threat. The Radcliffe Line was published with almost no warning, so millions of families learned which country they were in by waking up to a different flag. Trauma of that scale does not heal in a generation, and it is why the Kashmir dispute has been active since the moment both countries existed. Geography did not cause this — the political decision to partition did.

</details>

---

Q: Explain how the Chicken's Neck connects a 1905 administrative decision to modern Indian strategic geography.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** In 1905 the British partitioned Bengal on administrative grounds rather than religious ones, and reversed it in 1911. But the line they drew in 1905 is what determined where the Siliguri corridor runs, and therefore where India's most contested internal choke point sits. So a decision about how to organise paperwork for a province became, eighty years later, the strategic geography of a nuclear-armed country.

</details>

---

Q: Why is India's oil dependence a bigger problem than China's, even though China is the larger economy?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because dependence is about the share, not the absolute amount. China imports roughly 45 to 50 percent of its oil from the Gulf, and it has alternatives — pipelines from Russia and Central Asia, and imports from Africa. India imports well over 80 percent from the Gulf, has no alternative suppliers who could replace it, and controls neither the Strait of Hormuz nor its own supply. So in a Gulf crisis India is more exposed than China, despite being the smaller economy.

</details>

---

Q: Why is "claim versus control" from Phase 1 the most useful idea in this phase?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because it explains almost every recurring problem in this course. India claims all of Jammu and Kashmir while administering only part of it. India claims Aksai Chin while China controls it. The Line of Control exists as a ceasefire line, not an agreed boundary. The LAC is claimed by both sides and marked by neither. In each case the legal claim and the physical control have diverged, and that gap is where wars, freezes and negotiations happen.

</details>

---

Q: A friend says "India chose neutrality, so it was responsible for what happened in 1962." How would you respond?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would push back on two counts. First, non-alignment meant refusing to join either Cold War bloc, not refusing all alliances — India had signed a defence treaty with the United States in 1954. Second, and more importantly, the 1962 defeat was substantially a failure of preparation and judgement, not a consequence of neutrality. India's forward positions in Ladakh were lightly held, and its Defence Minister dismissed intelligence warnings that China would attack. The lesson is about underestimating a threat, not about alliances.

</details>

---

Q: Why does India's multi-alignment give it both leverage and weakness?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Leverage comes from being wanted by both sides at once. Because India will not pick a bloc, both the United States and China compete for it, and India can take investment, technology and diplomatic backing from each. The weakness is that a country which refuses to commit gets less when a decision is being made. Allies get consulted first; India gets a seat after the fact. The phrase used in India — "at ease with no one" — describes exactly this double-edged position.

</details>
