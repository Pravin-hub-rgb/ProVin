The last file ended in 1857 and pointed out something: Britain had just become a formal colonial empire, and twenty years later it was fighting a war in Europe that would eventually drag in every empire on Earth — including India.

That war is the subject of this file, and the two World Wars are the hinge of the entire modern timeline. Almost everything you read about in Phase 1 — the Radcliffe Line, the Kashmir wars, the Cold War — is a **direct consequence** of what happens here.

# 2.2 1914–1945 - The Two World Wars and the End of Empires

## Why the world suddenly became one connected thing

Start with a fact that is hard to overstate enough.

**Before 1914, the world was not one system.** It was a set of separate empires that mostly did not interact. The British Empire ran India. Russia ran its own empire. France had its own. There was no global order, because there was no global arena.

**The First World War is what turned a set of separate empires into one connected world system.** And that system is the one we still live in today.

Here is how it happened, and why it matters for India.

## The long road to 1914

The First World War did not start over a single cause. It started from a **long build-up of tensions in Europe**, and if you understand that build-up, you understand most of the twentieth century.

The most important of those tensions was between **Germany and France.** Why? Because of geography and history.

In the 1870s, Germany was unified for the first time in centuries — and almost immediately, France, which Germany had defeated in the Franco-Prussian War, became its permanent enemy. In 1871, France took Alsace-Lorraine from Germany. Germany wanted it back. **That single strip of land poisoned European politics for fifty years.**

Around this core, the other European powers formed two blocs. **The Triple Alliance** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) on one side, and the **Triple Entente** (France, Russia, Britain) on the other.

**And this is the crucial point for understanding what came next: these alliances were defensive, but they turned any small local conflict into a global one.**

Because each bloc had promised to help the others. So if France and Germany went to war over Alsace-Lorraine — which is what finally happened in 1914 — then Britain, Russia and a dozen other countries were automatically pulled in.

**A local argument about a strip of land became a war involving most of the world's powers.**

That is the single most important lesson of 1914. **Alliances convert a small conflict into a global one.** Keep that in mind for Phase 3, because it is exactly how alliances work today too.

## The First World War, 1914–1918

**The war started in July 1914 and lasted until November 1918.** Four years of industrialised slaughter on a scale the world had never seen — trenches, machine guns, poison gas, and eventually aircraft and tanks.

**What matters for this course is not the battles. It is what the war did to empires.**

**First: it bankrupted Europe.** Britain, France and Russia all went bankrupt fighting it. They were no longer able to run the world.

**Second: it destroyed the old empires.** The German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman empires all collapsed. Millions died, including in famine and disease after the fighting stopped.

**Third: it brought the United States into world affairs**, which was the first time America had mattered globally — and that set up the entire post-1945 order.

**And fourth, the one that concerns us most:**

## India's role in the First World War

**India was not a combatant, but it was not a bystander either.**

**India supplied more troops to the British war effort than any other part of the Empire.** Around **1.5 million Indian soldiers** served in the First World War.

**And India paid for it in money too.** India was made to contribute a large share of the war's cost — through taxes, through forced loans, and through the export of food and materials at times when India itself faced shortages.

**Why did Indians fight for a war that had nothing to do with them?** This is an important question, and the answer is the one we will spend Phase 3 and Phase 4 on.

India was not a country with its own foreign policy. It was a territory under British administration, and its soldiers were in the British army. There was no Indian government that could have said no. That is precisely the point — **and this is what the independence movement spent the next thirty years attacking.**

The money extracted from India during the war created a political class of Indians who now knew exactly how much the country was being drained, and by whom.

## India between the wars, 1918–1939

The war ended in 1918. Then came twenty years of something much more complicated, and this is where modern Indian politics begins.

Two forces pulled against each other.

**The first was the independence movement**, led at first by **Mahatma Gandhi.**

**Who was Gandhi?** A lawyer who returned from South Africa and, from 1919, led a movement using a method nobody had tried before.

**What was that method?** **Satyagraha** — literally "holding firmly to truth." In practice it meant **non-violent resistance**: marches, fasts, boycotts, and deliberately accepting punishment rather than hitting back.

**Why was it so effective?** Because it was almost impossible to suppress. When thousands of people calmly march and then refuse to move, a policeman cannot solve the problem by hitting them — the whole point is that there is no fight.

Gandhi's method transformed the independence movement from something that looked like a small group of protesters into a mass movement. Three movements came together that had not before:

- **Moderates** — educated Indians who wanted reform within the British system.
- **Extremists** — like the revolutionaries in Bengal, who wanted to *throw the British out* by force.
- **Gandhi's non-violence** — a third path that united people who would never have agreed on anything else.

**The second force was Muslim politics**, organised around the **All-India Muslim League.**

**And here is where the world outside India forced itself in.**

## The First World War's leftovers created the Kashmir problem

**Britain won the First World War in 1918.** And then, with a Europe in ruins, the **Ottoman Empire collapsed.**

That collapse was the single most important event for India, and almost nobody realises it.

Because the British had made a set of promises to different groups during the war. And those promises, kept in the wrong order and the wrong way, produced exactly the disaster in Phase 1.

Here is the sequence, and follow it carefully:

1. The Ottoman Empire collapsed in 1918.
2. Britain had promised the **Arabs** an independent Arab kingdom.
3. Britain had also made promises to the **Jews** about a home, which became the **Balfour Declaration of 1917**, supporting a Jewish national home in Palestine.
4. The collapse of the Ottoman Empire created a question about **who would rule the Muslim Holy Land** — and in 1920 Britain split it, taking control under a **League of Nations Mandate for Palestine**.
5. **Britain needed a reliable local ally** to enforce the Mandate, and it chose the **Hashemite king of the Hejaz**, who ruled Mecca and Medina.
6. **But that king was deeply religious.** Under the influence of a scholar named **Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab** he rejected the loose arrangement Britain had wanted, and insisted on ruling the holy cities on religious terms.
7. **So Britain switched sides.** In 1922 it began backing a different Saudi ruler, **Ibn Saud**, giving him money and modern weapons.
8. **In 1925 Ibn Saud's forces captured Mecca** from the Hashemite king, and by 1932 he had united the Arabian Peninsula into what became **Saudi Arabia**.

**So the Saudi–Iranian rivalry has its origin in a 1920s British decision to back one Saudi tribe against another.**

That is not history trivia. **Saudi Arabia and Iran are the two great rival powers of the Middle East today**, and their competition runs through several conflicts, from the Iran–Iraq war to today's proxy fights. Understanding that this began as a British bet on one tribe over another removes a lot of the mystery.

## A war that could easily have gone a completely different way

Here is something most people never consider, and it makes the Second World War much less mysterious.

**In the 1930s, it was entirely possible that the "Second World War" would have been a fight between capitalism and communism — Britain and the Soviet Union on one side, not against each other at all.**

**Why was that possible?** Because in the 1930s, the Soviet Union under Stalin was widely regarded in the West as a greater long-term threat than Hitler.

**Why?** Because Russia had revolutionised: it had overthrown its own rulers, taken over private property, and refused to cooperate with the Western capitalist system. To many British and French politicians, the greater danger was not Hitler but Stalin.

**And there were real negotiations.** During the first half of 1939, Britain, France and the Soviet Union actually held talks about a military alliance against Germany.

**So why did it not happen?** Two reasons.

**First, Stalin's own calculation.** Stalin believed Hitler was a more immediate and more manageable threat than the combined capitalist powers, and in August 1939 he signed the **Nazi-Soviet Pact** — a non-aggression pact with Hitler — which is what left Hitler free to invade Poland without fear of a two-front war.

**Second, the Western powers' delay.** The negotiations collapsed over disagreement about Poland and the Baltic states, and by the time they were sorted out, **Hitler had already invaded Poland in September 1939.**

**This is the single most important lesson of the whole file, and it has nothing to do with battles.**

**The Second World War was not caused by fascism. It was caused by a series of choices, made under pressure, by frightened governments, in the space left by one broken negotiation.**

That is not history trivia. **It is exactly what Phase 3 is about** — how decisions made by frightened people with conflicting interests create outcomes nobody wanted.

## Appeasement, and why it looked reasonable at the time

**"Appeasement"** means making concessions to an aggressor to avoid war. It is remembered today as a catastrophic mistake.

**But the motivation was entirely rational at the time, and understanding that is the point.**

Britain and France in the 1930s had just lived through 1914–18. **They had seen, in their own lifetime, an entire generation of young men killed.** So when Hitler's demands came, the calculation in London and Paris was not "is he bluffing?" It was **"is this worth another four years of that?"**

**And in fairness to them, on the evidence of 1914–18, the answer looked like no.**

So they gave concessions. At Munich in 1938 they even allowed Germany to take the Sudetenland, a region full of Czech citizens, in the name of avoiding a general war.

**Appeasement was not stupidity. It was the trauma of the First World War.** A generation that had watched trenches and gas and millions of dead decided that peace at any price was safer.

**A decade after they had convinced themselves war was unthinkable, Hitler told them it was real.**

## The Second World War, 1939–1945

The Second World War began in **September 1939** and ended in **1945**.

**Why did it last six years when the first lasted four?** For three reasons:

**First: the expansion.** By 1939 Hitler already controlled Austria and most of Czechoslovakia. **The war in Europe started with him taking territory**, and it spread outward rather than starting at a fixed frontier.

**Second: the Eastern Front.** From June 1941 the **Soviet Union** was invaded — the largest military operation in history, fought in brutal winters, with civilian deaths in the tens of millions. **This absorbed and destroyed most of the German army.**

**Third: the Atlantic.** Britain was never invaded, but the **Battle of the Atlantic** ran for the entire war. German submarines tried to cut off Britain's supply line, and Britain's survival depended on American **Lend-Lease** supply, which began in March 1941.

**And it ended with two atomic bombs.** In August 1945, **Hiroshima and Nagasaki** were bombed, and Japan surrendered. The bombs were not what defeated Japan militarily — the Soviet invasion of Manchuria in August 1945 was decisive — but they changed the world permanently, and the next file will deal with them directly.

## The part that decided India's future

**Everything in this section matters for one reason: it decided India's future, and it did so through a piece of paper called the Atlantic Charter.**

**The Atlantic Charter** was issued in August 1941 by Roosevelt and Churchill, and it stated a principle that changed everything: **every country, including countries occupied by an enemy, had the right to determine its own government.**

**And here is the trap.** The Charter was written by two people who did not think about India at all. And yet it became the basis of the **Indian National Congress** rejecting the British offer in 1942, because the same principle applied to India too.

Because that principle, applied honestly, meant: **Britain had no right to rule India, because the people of India had not chosen to be ruled by Britain.**

And so the **Indian National Congress demanded a Constituent Assembly** — a body elected by Indians to write India's constitution.

**That demand, made in 1942, is why we have a Constitution of India at all.** And it is why the Constitution begins with the words **"Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic"** — words that came straight out of this decision.

**So the Second World War, fought entirely outside India, produced inside India the idea that India should govern itself.** That is not history trivia either. It is a direct chain from the Atlantic Charter to the Constitution of India.

## The bombs, and what they actually changed

The bombs killed somewhere between 100,000 and 200,000 people, mostly civilians.

But the more important effect was **not** the destruction. It was that **the bombs ended the possibility of a third world war.**

**Before 1945, no government would have dared start a war against another major power.** Because the other power would fight back, and both would end up destroyed.

**After 1945, everyone knew what the weapons could do.** And that is exactly why the world has had no full-scale world war in eighty years. Not because nations became peaceful — because they became certain of the cost.

**That is a very specific kind of peace, and it is the foundation of the modern world.** The next file picks up 1947 and explains how the world organised itself after this — which is how we get to today's geopolitics.

## What this file is not

**The World Wars were not caused by one thing.** There was no single cause. There were alliances, nationalisms, imperial rivalries, and economic depression feeding into each other. Anyone who tells you a single event "caused" the Second World War has simplified past usefulness.

**Nor was Britain's rise purely military.** Britain had an enormous navy and an industrial economy, but it also benefited from being an island, from being first to industrialise, and from alliances. No single factor.

**Nor was India passive in the wars.** India supplied about 1.5 million soldiers to the First World War and was financially drained to pay for it. That extraction is precisely what created the political class who demanded self-rule.

**And the most important correction: the World Wars did not create a peaceful world.** They created a more violent, more polarised one — two superpowers armed with nuclear weapons, proxies fighting wars on every continent, and a Cold War that lasted forty-four years. The next file picks up exactly there.
