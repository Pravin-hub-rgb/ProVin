# 2.4 1971–1991 - The Cold War and India

## The standoff: two nuclear neighbours who cannot fight

Let me start with the situation India faced after 1971, because it is genuinely new in human history.

**Before December 1971, India and Pakistan had fought wars** — in 1947, in 1965, in 1971. Conventional wars, with conventional losses.

**After December 1971, both knew the other had nuclear weapons.**

**And this created something historians call a "nuclear stalemate."** Two countries that cannot attack each other, sitting on the same border, both unable to resolve their disputes.

**Here is the crucial consequence, and it explains almost all India-Pakistan news you will see.**

**Because neither side can win a full-scale war, both sides have looked for ways to fight that are *below* that threshold.** This is why you will hear about:

- **Terrorist attacks in Kashmir.** Both sides have used non-state actors to fight, because that avoids a full war.
- **Border skirmishes and infiltration.** Small-scale, deniable, escalating slowly.
- **Military coups.** Both countries have repeatedly changed governments by army action, because a leader who is removed cannot sign away the border.

**That is the pattern to watch for.** Phase 4 will analyse it properly. For now, notice the chain: nuclear weapons → neither side can win → so both fight below the threshold instead.

## The Simla Agreement, 1972

**The most important thing India did in this entire period was stop.**

**In July 1972, Indira Gandhi and Pakistan's leader Zulfikar Ali Bhutto met in Simla, Himachal Pradesh — the same town where the McMahon Line was signed in 1914** — and signed the **Simla Agreement.**

**And here is what made it remarkable: Zulfikar Ali Bhutto came to India as a prisoner of war**, having been captured in the 1971 war. He was released, and instead of returning home in humiliation, he was invited to Simla as an equal.

**What did the agreement actually do? Two things:**

**First, it accepted the Line of Control.** Both countries agreed that the LoC would not be renegotiated. In exchange, India and Pakistan promised to respect each other's territorial integrity — meaning **Pakistan accepted that Kashmir was part of India.**

**Second, it committed both sides to peaceful settlement**, and established a framework for resolving future disputes.

**Why is this so important?** Because it is the reason India and Pakistan have not gone to war since 1971 — with the major exception of **Kargil in 1999**, which was a limited operation that both sides managed to contain.

**And the symmetry of the place is worth noticing.** The Simla Agreement of 1972 was signed in the same town where the McMahon Line was agreed in 1914. **The same town has produced two of the most consequential agreements in South Asian history — one drawing a disputed line, and one accepting it.**

## 1974 and 1998 — India becomes a nuclear power

Now the other huge story of this period.

**In May 1974, India conducted its first nuclear test** at Pokhran, in Rajasthan. It was called a "peaceful nuclear explosion."

**Why did India do this in 1974?** Because **Pakistan had already done it in 1974** — Pakistan's first nuclear test was in February 1974, and India's was in May, roughly three months later.

**Why did Pakistan test?** This connects directly to 1971. **India had demonstrated in the 1971 war that it could win a conventional war decisively.** Pakistan's conclusion was that the only way to guarantee survival was nuclear weapons.

**So the chain is:** 1971 war → Indian victory → Pakistan tests nukes → India tests nukes three months later → both now have them → neither can attack → stalemate.

**Then, 24 years later, India tested again.** In **May 1998, India conducted the Pokhran-II tests** — a much larger set, described as demonstrating the capability for a thermonuclear weapon.

**Why did India test in 1998?** Because of the previous year.

**The 1998 crisis.** India and Pakistan had come extremely close to a nuclear war, because India had conducted **nuclear tests** in January 1998, and **the financial markets collapsed worldwide.** India's rupee fell by almost 20 percent in a day, and foreign investment fled. The **Chamber of Commerce in the United States threatened sanctions on India.**

**And the decisive response was from a man named Atal Bihari Vajpayee**, the Indian Prime Minister. **In a radio broadcast to the nation, Vajpayee declared India a nuclear weapons state.**

**And here is what makes it one of the most significant moments in Indian geopolitics: India said so publicly, and did not ask for permission.**

**Why did Vajpayee do that?** Because it worked. **The sanctions threat evaporated.** Because it put the pressure back on Pakistan, and Pakistan responded by conducting its own nuclear tests in May 1998.

**And here is the crucial geopolitical consequence**, which ties back to 1971 and forward to 1999:

**India signed the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) only in 2008** — and as a "nuclear weapons state." Until then, India was outside the NPT entirely.

**NPT = full civil nuclear power in exchange for giving up nuclear weapons. India said no, and became a nuclear weapons state by choice — and argued, successfully, that it is a responsible one.**

## 1987 — The first time the world noticed India's economy

**In July 1987, India signed an agreement with the United States that opened its economy to foreign trade.**

**Why?** Because India's economy was in deep trouble. The **balance of payments crisis of 1991** would follow — India had almost no foreign reserves and could barely pay for imports, including oil.

**And this is exactly what Phase 0 and Phase 1 predicted.**

India has very little domestic oil — it imports roughly 85 percent of it. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, **oil prices rose enormously** after the Iranian revolution. **And India, importing nearly all its oil, could not pay.**

**So India opened itself up to the world economy not because it was enthusiastic, but because it had no choice.**

**That is the connection between geography and economics.** India cannot produce the oil it needs, so its oil price is decided in other countries' waters. **And when those prices rise, India is the one that suffers.** Phase 4 covers this in detail.

## What the Cold War meant for India

Now the bigger picture. **What was the Cold War?**

**After 1945, the world was divided into two camps:**

- **The US-led "Western" camp** — NATO (the North Atlantic Treaty Organization), the "Free World"
- **The Soviet-led "Eastern" camp** — the Warsaw Pact, the "Communist Bloc"

**They never fought directly.** That is what made it a "cold" war.

**And it was fought in three ways: an arms race, a space race, and proxy wars.**

**And this is the part that matters for India: the Cold War came to India.**

**Not as an invasion. As somebody else's fight, fought on Indian soil.** Because the Cold War was global, and India was strategically located, the superpowers both wanted it on their side.

**How did that shape India?**

**First, the superpowers both courted India.** And India's non-aligned position gave it leverage it had never had before — **because both sides wanted it, and it refused to commit.** The United States gave India technology and food aid in the 1960s. The Soviet Union built the **Bhakra Nangal Dam** in Punjab, which is still India's biggest power plant on the Sutlej.

**Second, and much more ugly: India was a proxy battlefield.** The United States is **believed** to have used Pakistan as a proxy to fight Soviet influence in Afghanistan, because Pakistan was the only country bordering Afghanistan. **The Soviet Union was believed to be arming the Mujahideen in Pakistan's North-West Frontier Province.**

**Third, the wars India could not win.** The wars of 1947–48, 1965 and 1971 were **part of the Cold War**, because Pakistan was aligned with the United States, and America did not want a defeat. **So when India fought Pakistan, India was also fighting America's ally.**

## 1991 — The year everything changed

**And then, in 1991, the Cold War ended. And it ended suddenly, without a war.**

**In 1991, the Soviet Union collapsed.** Fifteen republics became independent states. Boris Yeltsin, the President of the Russian Federation, formally resigned as Soviet leader on **25 December 1991** — and the Soviet Union ceased to exist.

**The Cold War had ended. And then the entire world order changed — in one year, completely.**

**Why did the Soviet Union collapse?** Three reasons:

**First: economic failure.** The Soviet command economy — where the government decided what to produce — simply did not work. By the 1980s, shortages were normal and the economy had effectively stopped growing.

**Second: the arms race.** The United States spent roughly the same amount on the military as the entire Soviet economy produced in total, in the 1970s. **The US military budget was larger than the whole USSR GDP.** To match it, the Soviets spent everything — which left no money for anything else. **So they starved their economy to match America's weapons.**

**Third: the fall of the Berlin Wall, November 1989.** This was the symbolic moment. The wall that divided Berlin for 28 years came down.

**And here is what this means for India, and it is the last point of this file.**

**When the Soviet Union collapsed, it took with it roughly a third of the world's GDP.** Which meant a massive global economic contraction.

**And India's response to that — Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao, in 1991 — was a profound economic reform. That is the next file.**

## What this file is not

**India's nuclear tests were not a response to an external attack.** They were a direct response to Pakistan, which tested first. It is a bilateral arms race between two neighbours, driven by mutual fear.

**Nor is India's nuclear doctrine simple.** India has never threatened nuclear first use, and it claims a policy of "No First Use" — meaning India would only use nuclear weapons in response to a nuclear attack. But India has built a huge arsenal designed for survivability, which is not contradictory, but it is more aggressive than the doctrine suggests.

**Nor was the Cold War purely ideological.** It was also about trade, resources, empire, and national pride. Reducing it to "communism versus capitalism" misses most of it.

**And the Cold War did not "end" so much as the loser gave up.** There were no peace negotiations, no treaty. The Soviet Union simply collapsed internally, and everyone else adjusted.
