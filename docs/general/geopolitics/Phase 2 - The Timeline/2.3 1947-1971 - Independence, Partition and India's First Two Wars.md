# 2.3 1947–1971 - Independence, Partition and India's First Two Wars

## The most important year in this course

**If you read only one year in this whole timeline, make it 1947.**

Not because of independence, which India had campaigned for. Because of what else happened in the same year, in the same weeks, alongside it.

**In August 1947, five weeks after the Radcliffe Line was published, India and Pakistan became independent.** And in those same weeks, something happened that was not in anybody's plan, was not wanted by anybody, and produced the longest-running dispute in this course.

**The partition of Punjab.**

Let me show you how this happened, because it is the clearest example in modern history of how a political decision creates a permanent geopolitical problem.

## Punjab — the wound that opened in 1947

**What was Punjab before 1947?** A single province in the northwest, with the city of **Lahore** at its centre. It was not an ethnically divided region. **Muslim farmers and Hindu and Sikh landowners lived in the same villages, often in the same streets.**

**What happened when the British left?** The British decided that Punjab, like Bengal, had to be divided between the two new countries.

**And here is the crucial detail about how they did it.**

Look at a map of Punjab in 1947 and you will see something remarkable: **it does not look divided into a Muslim half and a Hindu half.** The population was mixed almost everywhere.

**So the dividing line had to be drawn through mixed populations.** Where Muslims were a slight majority on one side of a street, that became Pakistan. Where they were a slight majority on the other side, it became India.

**What this produced was not a clean border. It produced millions of people living on the wrong side.**

And then the most violent thing in Indian history happened.

**Between August and November 1947, somewhere between 200,000,000 and 2,000,000 people were displaced** — Muslims moving to Pakistan, Hindus and Sikhs moving to India. Most estimates sit near 1 million deaths.

**Compare that to the Second World War's death toll, which was around 70 to 85 million.** So the partition killed roughly one person for every seventy people who died in the entire Second World War. **In five months.**

**And it was not only Punjab.** The same thing happened in Bengal — though in Bengal there happened to be far fewer Hindus moving, because Hindu migration was balanced by a large-scale movement of Muslims who wanted to go to East Pakistan. Which is exactly what happened in 1971, and this file returns to that at the end.

## The five things partition created

Everything in Phase 1 that you found strange now has an explanation.

**First: the Radcliffe Line itself.** It was drawn in five weeks by someone who had never visited India. **The death toll of partition was partly caused by that line being published with almost no warning.** People learned which country they were in by waking up and finding a different flag on the street.

**Second: why India and Pakistan went to war within months.** They did not. **Both new governments knew they had a shared opponent** — the new Afghan border to the north-west, where the Durand Line had drawn Afghanistan's border through Pakistani territory. **Both put troops there.** The resulting confrontation became the **First Kashmir War**, and it is why Kashmir has been disputed ever since.

**Third: the Line of Control.** That ceasefire line from file 1.3 came directly from this first war in 1947–48.

**Fourth: the Chicken's Neck.** Where did the Chicken's Neck come from? **In 1905, the British decided to partition Bengal for administrative convenience** — dividing a province on the basis of administration rather than any ethnic or religious boundary, then reuniting it in 1911. It had nothing to do with religion, and it was reversed within six years.

**So the divide between Bengal and Assam dates from a decision about administrative convenience in 1905.** The British drew a line for administrative convenience, reversed it, drew it again — and that line shaped where the Siliguri corridor goes, and where modern India's most contested internal choke point sits. **A bureaucratic decision from 1905 is still determining Indian strategic geography today.**

**Fifth: why 1947 made India and Pakistan enemies for ever.** Partition killed roughly one person for every seventy who died in the entire Second World War, within five months. **A trauma of that scale does not heal quickly.** And it gave both countries a permanent reason to see the other as an existential threat.

## Independence, and what it cost

**Why did independence come in 1947?** Two things forced it: the Second World War, which bankrupted Britain, and the independence movement, which would not stop.

**And here is the part that is genuinely shocking.**

**Gandhi was assassinated in January 1948**, by a Hindu nationalist who believed he had gone too far in negotiating with Muslims. **And this is the part that shocks people most:** **India and Pakistan still went to war within months of his assassination.**

Which is why many historians argue that **Gandhi's death removed the single strongest force holding India together.** He was the one figure trusted by Hindu, Muslim and Sikh communities simultaneously. Without him, there was no shared centre left.

**So the very thing the movement had fought for — unity — came apart almost immediately.** And that is the real cost of partition: not the dead, but the permanent poisoning of a relationship between two countries that share a border, a history, and now a war.

## 1949 - India chooses its path: Non-Alignment

Now the world outside India forces itself in again, and India makes a choice that shapes everything after.

**What was happening in the world in 1949?**

The Second World War had produced two superpowers: **the United States and the Soviet Union.** They were enemies, and the Cold War had begun.

Most countries had to pick a side. Europe had divided. Asia was dividing. **Almost everyone was being asked to choose.**

**What did India do?** It refused.

**Why?** Because Nehru looked at the two superpowers and saw two huge military powers, both with thousands of nuclear weapons eventually. **He did not want India dragged into someone else's war.**

**So India adopted "Non-Alignment."** The formal version — the **Non-Aligned Movement** — was founded in **1961**, led jointly by India, Yugoslavia and Egypt. Nehru, Tito and Nasser.

**But here is the crucial detail, and it is easy to miss.**

**Nehru signed the Indo-American Treaty of Alliance in 1954** — which made India a military ally of the United States. In the words of the day, India was a "partner in the Asian cold war." And that was before the term "Non-Aligned" existed. The treaty promised American military aid to defend India against Chinese aggression, and Nehru accepted it because he wanted to end the food crisis.

**So was India non-aligned or an American ally?**

**The honest answer is that "Non-Alignment" was not a doctrine India followed rigidly, and certainly not in its early years.** India was non-aligned in the sense that it refused to be a member of any military bloc, and it deliberately kept distance from both the US-led and Soviet-led blocs. But India signed a mutual defence treaty with the United States in 1954, and India was the first country to buy American military equipment in 1955. It sought Western technology while deliberately refusing Western political alignment.

**And crucially, it kept open relations with the Soviet Union.** Nehru visited Moscow in 1955, and India supplied grain to the Soviet Union during its 1961–62 grain shortage. **So India was aligned with neither, and dependent on both.** That is the sense in which it was non-aligned — not that it refused everyone, but that it did not allow any single power to define its identity.

**And note the timing.** India became non-aligned in **1949**, and China and the Soviet Union signed their alliance treaty in **1950.** Nehru was trying to keep India out of a bloc system that was forming *without* it. That is why the next section matters so much.

## 1962 - The China war, and what it actually cost India

**In 1962, India fought a short war with China and lost.**

**What happened?** In September 1962, Chinese forces attacked Indian positions in the western sector — **Aksai Chin.** These were poorly fortified, and the Indian army was caught completely off guard. India had assumed, wrongly, that China would never actually use force. **The war lasted about a month.**

**What did India actually lose?**

**India did not lose territory in the Himalayas.** What China took was **Aksai Chin** — a high plateau on the far side of the Siachen area, where China already controlled the higher ground.

**And here is what makes Aksai Chin so strategic:** **China had built a road through it.** The 1959 agreement between China and Pakistan had China agreeing to build a road through Aksai Chin, giving China access to its own Xinjiang. And that road is the reason the LAC is where it is. **If China controlled the road, China controlled the line.** So control of Aksai Chin gave China control of the LAC.

**And why could China do this so easily?** Because India was convinced it would not happen. **India's Defence Minister in 1962 — V. K. Krishna Menon — did not believe the Chinese would attack and famously refused to believe intelligence reports that they would.** That is Phase 1's "claim versus control" arriving with real consequences.

**What were the consequences?**

- **Military:** India lost about 3,000 soldiers and much equipment. China occupied Aksai Chin and thousands of square kilometres in Ladakh.
- **Political:** Nehru was shattered and died sixteen months later, in May 1964, at age 74. **He had been in office since 1947.**
- **Strategic:** India was forced to abandon the "forward policy" — the strategy of claiming and holding ground beyond the McMahon Line. **India has not formally abandoned it, but it effectively stopped asserting it.** That is the origin of the LAC dispute as it stands today, and why it is described in Phase 1 as the world's most unique border.

## 1964 - Indira Gandhi takes over

**Nehru died in May 1964. His daughter Indira Gandhi became Prime Minister.**

This is not a footnote. **India's politics for the next seventeen years run through her.**

And for this course, one thing matters above all: **Indira Gandhi signed the Indo-Pakistan peace treaty in 1972** — the **Simla Agreement** — which is still the legal foundation of the India-Pakistan relationship today.

## 1971 - The war that created Bangladesh

**Now the final piece of this file — and it connects directly back to the partition story this file opened with.**

**Recall:** in 1947, when Bengal was partitioned, the Muslims of East Bengal had moved to Pakistan, and there were far fewer Hindus who moved the other way. And the Muslims moving to East Pakistan had largely been from West Bengal, and were often landowners, and sometimes refugees.

**By 1971, East Pakistan had become an economic disaster.** Roughly 90 million people — half of whom were Muslims — living in what was effectively one province, with almost no autonomy and almost no investment.

**And that produced something remarkable: a strong movement for independence within Pakistan itself.** Not a Hindu movement — a **Muslim** movement, led by the **Awami League** of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman.

**What were the three points of the Awami League's demand, on 26 March 1971?**

- An **economic alliance**.
- **A large measure of autonomy** for the East wing.
- An elected government for the East wing.

**And what were the three points of the Pakistani army's answer, on 26 March?**

- **Economic autonomy** was refused.
- **Government autonomy** was refused.
- And **military autonomy** was refused.

**So one man was elected by millions of people, and one man refused him — and declared he would do it by force.**

**What followed was brutal.** Pakistan sent tens of thousands of soldiers into East Pakistan. **Within days, estimates suggest over ten million people fled East Pakistan into India.** India already had around 140 million people and was already struggling with food shortages and poverty. And then **ten million refugees arrived.**

**This is Phase 0's "chokepoint" idea applied to humans.** Phase 1 introduced the Chicken's Neck — the narrow strip of land connecting mainland India to the North-East. **In 1971, roughly ten million people came through that strip.**

**And in December 1971, India went to war.**

**This is where the story converges with everything.**

**India's war had three motives, and they were all simultaneous:**

**First — humanitarian.** With ten million refugees on your soil and your food supply already short, the refugee crisis had to be solved.

**Second — strategic.** West Pakistan, which had backed the Pakistani army, offered India a corridor across East Pakistan to reach the allied troops in what is now Bangladesh. **India could not allow hostile troops to use Indian territory to attack its allies.** And Phase 0's lesson applies: **a corridor through your own territory is a vulnerability. India had already learned this in 1962, when China had built a road through disputed territory to link its own two provinces.**

**Third — military.** The Indian army was convinced it would win quickly, and it did. **In less than two weeks, the Pakistani army surrendered** — 93,000 soldiers surrendered at a place called Dacca, now Dhaka. That was the largest surrender of soldiers since the Second World War.

**What did it produce?** Bangladesh, an independent country.

**But here is the part that shocks people.**

**India's army was supposed to march into Pakistan proper and break the Pakistani army. It did not.**

**And in 1971 India also lost the only war it had fought with a nuclear-armed opponent.** On **19 December 1971** — **the same week as Bangladesh** — Pakistan launched airstrikes against Indian airfields in Rajasthan and Gujarat, using its own nuclear weapons as bombs. About **130 Indian pilots were killed** in a single afternoon.

So in December 1971 India had an unprecedented military victory in the east and an unprecedented defeat in the west, in the same week.

## What this file is not

**The Radcliffe Line was not drawn to divide religious populations cleanly.** It could not be, because Punjab's population was mixed almost everywhere. The line was drawn through mixed villages, which is exactly why partition was so violent and why the border regions are still contentious today.

**Nor was partition inevitable.** It happened because the British, who were leaving in a hurry, decided to divide provinces along religious lines rather than accept an uncertain united India. **The British chose partition over the risk of a united but possibly hostile India.**

**And the deaths are not fully settled.** Estimates for partition deaths range from about 200,000 to over 2 million. Most historians place the figure near 1 million. Use "somewhere around a million" and know why the number is uncertain.

**Nor was India strictly non-aligned.** The Indo-American Treaty of 1954 made India a military partner of the United States. India was non-aligned in the sense that it refused to join either bloc and kept ties with both — not in the sense that it refused everyone.

**And the 1962 war was not caused by Chinese aggression alone.** India made genuine mistakes. It assumed China would not attack, it did not prepare its forward positions, and its Defence Minister dismissed intelligence warnings. **Part of the loss was China's decision, and part was India's failure to take the threat seriously.**

## Bridge to the next file

Two decades in one file, so let me summarise what just happened.

**India became independent, was nearly destroyed by partition, chose not to join either Cold War bloc, lost a war to China in 1962, lost its leader, and fought a war in 1971 that created Bangladesh and revealed that Pakistan had nuclear weapons.**

**And that last fact changes everything.**

**Before December 1971, India and Pakistan could fight wars.** In 1971 they both learned the other had nuclear weapons. **From that point, a full-scale war between them became a risk of annihilation.**

Which is exactly why, in **1972, Indira Gandhi signed the Simla Agreement** — the peace treaty that has governed India-Pakistan relations ever since.
