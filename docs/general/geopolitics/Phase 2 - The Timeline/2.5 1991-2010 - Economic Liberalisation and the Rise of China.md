The last file ended in 1991 with the Soviet Union collapsing and the world order changing in twelve months.

But the change that mattered most for India was not political. It was **economic** — and it produced the India that exists today.

This file also covers the other huge story of these years: **China.** Because by 2010, the geopolitical question had shifted from "who are the two superpowers?" to "the US and China are rivals — and where does India fit?"

# 2.5 1991–2010 - Economic Liberalisation and the Rise of China

## 1991 — India's financial crisis

**Let me start with how bad it actually was, because modern Indians often do not know this.**

In **1991, India faced a balance-of-payments crisis.** India had almost no foreign reserves — roughly two weeks' worth of imports.

**And here is the reason, and it comes straight back to Phase 0 and Phase 1.**

Phase 0 told us that **resources matter, and that a country cannot manufacture what it does not have.** Phase 1 told us that **India imports roughly 85 percent of its oil.**

**So when oil prices rose — and they rose enormously after the Iranian revolution of 1979 — India could not afford to pay.** India's oil import bill consumed most of its foreign currency earnings.

**And now add the Cold War ending.** When the Soviet Union collapsed, roughly a third of the world's GDP disappeared, and global trade contracted sharply.

**Put those two together:** a country that imports 85 percent of its oil, facing higher oil bills, in a collapsing world economy, with two weeks of reserves.

**That is how close India came to defaulting on its foreign debt.**

**And this is the direct link to geography.** India cannot produce the oil it needs, so its oil price is decided in other countries' waters. **When those prices rise, India is the one that suffers.** That is not an accident of history — that is the consequence of the resource position Phase 0 described.

## The 1991 reforms — what actually changed

**In July 1991, Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh began reforms.**

**Manmohan Singh is the key figure.** He had been the Reserve Bank of India governor, he had the reputation of an economist who could be trusted, and he wrote the budget that did it.

**Here is the crucial detail: these reforms were not ideological.** They were not a sudden conversion to free-market thinking. **They were what the government had to do to avoid the country running out of money.**

**And that distinction matters**, because Phase 3 will make a point about interests over ideology. **The great liberal reformer of Indian history did it because the country had no choice.**

**What actually changed, and what stayed the same.**

**What changed:**

- **The Licence Raj was abolished.** Before 1991, to start a business in India you needed permission from the government. That permission — the licence — was a monopoly. **Now, in most sectors, you no longer need it.**
- **Tariffs were slashed.** Import duties fell from over 300 percent in many cases to a much lower level.
- **Foreign investment opened up.** Multinational companies could invest in India. **This is the single biggest change.**
- **The rupee was devalued**, and the exchange rate was made more flexible.
- **Public sector companies were opened to competition**, partly by selling shares to the public in 1991.

**What did not change:**

- **Democracy.** No change.
- **The five-year plans.** India continued planning.
- **Land ownership.** No change.
- **The public sector as an employer.** No change.

**And that last point is a trap you should avoid.** People say "India became free market in 1991." **India did not become free market.** It opened up **partially, and gradually, and while keeping many controls.** Phase 4 covers the Indian economy properly.

**What did the reforms achieve?** Growth. **India's GDP growth rate went from around 3–4 percent a year before 1991 to over 8 percent on average in the following two decades.** For a large country, sustained 8 percent is extraordinary, and it is the foundation of India's current position.

**But here is the thing people miss: India is still a developing country, and the reforms made it richer, not rich.** India was still around the 130th country by GDP per capita in the 1990s. **Growth did not change the fact that hundreds of millions of Indians live on very low incomes.** Phase 4 covers this properly.

## 1999 — Kargil

**In 1999, India and Pakistan fought a short war for the third time.** This one was different in an important way.

**What happened:** In the spring of 1999, Pakistani soldiers and militants crossed the Line of Control into the mountainous area of **Kargil** in Ladakh, and took a number of high positions.

**India discovered it, and responded.**

**And here is the crucial detail: the Kargil War was limited on purpose.**

**Why?** Because India had nuclear weapons, and so did Pakistan, and both knew the risks.

**So both sides kept the war small.** India did not escalate to a full-scale war. It fought a limited high-altitude battle, and it restricted itself to **conventional weapons only.**

**And the war ended quickly, and mostly because of a diplomatic intervention by the United States.** Under intense pressure from Bill Clinton, Pakistan agreed to withdraw.

**But this creates a puzzle.** If both sides knew the risks of escalation, and if both limited the war, **how did fighting break out at all?**

**Because the problem was structural.** Neither country could say clearly where the LoC was — and the Kargil area had no posts on the line. **The Line of Control was not properly marked, and not agreed, in that sector.**

**And that is the link to Phase 1. In that part of the world, the LoC was unmapped and unoccupied.** The whole crisis came out of an undemarcated line.

**What did Kargil actually change?**

**Military:** Pakistan lost around 1,200–1,800 soldiers. India recaptured the positions.

**Diplomatic:** **The Lahore Declaration (February 1999)** was signed between the Indian Prime Minister Vajpayee and Pakistani Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif. **It committed both countries to the peaceful settlement of all disputes, including Kashmir.**

**And that is the significance.** Kashmir went from a standing armed conflict to a negotiation table. **The dialogue channel between India and Pakistan that opened in 1999 has been maintained, more or less, ever since.**

**And it is why, since 1999, there have been no more wars between India and Pakistan.** All the fighting since then has been from non-state actors — terrorist attacks and infiltration.

## The other great story — how China rose

While India was reforming in 1991, something far larger was happening in China. **It is arguably the most important economic story of the twentieth century, and it changed the world order entirely.**

**Here is the basic picture.** Until the 1980s, **China was one of the poorest large countries on Earth.** Famine, poverty, and economic stagnation defined it.

**In 1978, Deng Xiaoping launched economic reform.** He opened China to foreign investment and to the world market.

**And here is the crucial detail: China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001.**

**What does that mean? In one word — manufacturing.** When China joined the WTO (the World Trade Organization), it became the world's factory. **The West stopped putting import restrictions on Chinese goods**, and suddenly Chinese factories could sell to the world.

**And here's the sequence that explains China's rise:**

**First: foreign companies moved to China**, because Chinese labour was cheap. Nike, Apple, and thousands of other companies moved their manufacturing to China.

**Second: China built the world's largest manufacturing base**, and produced at a scale nothing had ever seen.

**Third: it became the world's second largest economy** by GDP, surpassing Japan around 2010.

**But here's the part that connects this to India, and it is not about economics.**

**When China became the world's factory, it needed two things on a massive scale:**

**1. Huge quantities of raw materials** — iron ore, coal, copper.

**2. Huge quantities of energy** — and China imports almost all its oil **by sea.**

**And where does that leave China? Geographically stuck.**

**China has a long coastline, but its oil comes from the Middle East** — which means all of it must pass through the **Strait of Hormuz** and the **Strait of Malacca.**

**And that is the crucial geopolitical point: China's rise in manufacturing directly drove its need for sea lanes.** Phase 0 said that control of shipping lanes equals power, and China's economic rise made it a shipping-dependent power.

**And it made India's position far more important than it used to be.** Because India sits in the middle of the Indian Ocean — between the Gulf and Malacca. **If India can hold that ocean, it can outmanoeuvre China.**

## Why this is dangerous for India

**And here is the thing people do not discuss: China's rise creates genuine danger for India.** Let me be specific, because vagueness here is useless.

**First, the border.** China's rise gave it money to modernise its army and build infrastructure along the LAC. The LAC has had far fewer military incidents since 2020 than in the preceding decade, partly because India has strengthened its positions there. **Both sides have built up significantly.**

**Second, the Indian Ocean.** China has been building **ports in countries around India** — in Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and Djibouti. Phase 4 covers this in detail as the "String of Pearls."

**Third, and most importantly: China's leverage over India's oil supply.**

**And here is the dangerous dynamic, stated as plainly as possible.**

**India imports roughly 85 percent of its oil, and most of it comes from the Gulf.** All of it must pass through the **Strait of Hormuz**, which is in the region of a country China is increasingly aligned with.

**So consider what happens in a crisis.**

- If China and the Gulf states are aligned against India, India's oil is at risk.
- **India has no control over Hormuz, no control over its own oil supply, and limited influence over shipping traffic in the Gulf.**
- **And it imports far more oil from the Gulf than China does.** China actually imports from Russia, from Central Asia, and from Africa.

**That is the strategic problem.** India's oil dependence on the Gulf is higher than China's, and India's oil has no alternative suppliers who could replace it. **India's dependence on the Gulf is its single biggest economic vulnerability.**

**And that is why the "String of Pearls" matters so much** — why India is building ports and access agreements in Oman, Madagascar, Sri Lanka, the Maldives, Myanmar and Vietnam. **Phase 4 explains all of this in detail.**

## The 2008 financial crisis

**In 2008, the world had a financial crisis.** And here is a fact that matters for India's trajectory.

**China was the only major economy that grew in 2008.** Because of its enormous stimulus programme, China grew in 2008 when almost everyone else in the West went into recession.

**This created a very specific impression, particularly in India.**

**India watched China grow through a global crisis, and concluded that China had made the right structural decisions and India had not.** **India's growth had been roughly 7–9 percent for a decade; China's was 10 percent or more.** India began asking what it was getting wrong.

**And that introspection produced a set of reforms, particularly in manufacturing and in the openness of the economy to foreign investment.** **This period also produced the criticism of India's "policy paralysis"** — and that's fair, and is not a political point, it's a factual one. India did underperform relative to its potential, and the data is not disputed.

**This matters for geopolitics too, because India's economy is its main source of international influence.** India cannot leverage its size or its history, because it doesn't threaten anybody. But if India's economy grows fast enough, it becomes something that others must accommodate.

## What this file is not

**The 1991 reforms were not an ideological conversion.** India did not suddenly decide capitalism was better. The reforms were a response to a genuine economic crisis, and they were what the government had to do.

**Nor did the reforms make India a free-market economy.** The licence raj was loosened, not abolished. Public sector firms were opened to competition, not privatised. Land ownership was unchanged. India opened up partially and gradually.

**And the reforms did not make India rich.** They made India grow faster. India was still among the poorest large countries by income per person in the 1990s. Growth and prosperity are not the same thing.

**Nor is India's position as a "neutral" trading bloc automatic.** India votes its own interests on most issues, and on some — particularly on non-proliferation and Iran — it does not vote with the West.

**China's rise is also not a mystery.** It is explained by exactly three things: Deng Xiaoping's reforms from 1978, joining the WTO in 2001, and cheap labour. Once you know those three, most of China's economic history follows from them. **And once you know how China depends on shipping, you understand why China's foreign policy is what it is.**
