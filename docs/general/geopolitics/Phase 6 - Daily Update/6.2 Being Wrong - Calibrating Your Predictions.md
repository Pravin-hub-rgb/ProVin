# 6.2 Being Wrong - Calibrating Your Predictions

## The problem with being right

**Start with something counter-intuitive: being right about geopolitics is not as valuable as it sounds.**

**Here is why.**

**Outcomes in geopolitics are mostly not determined by individual predictions.** Wars happen, economies shift, governments change — for reasons that include structural pressures, other countries' decisions, and luck. **Your forecast being correct may have almost nothing to do with the forecast.**

**But your calibration — how well your confidence matches your accuracy — is entirely about you.** It transfers. It improves. It applies to every question you will ever have an opinion about.

**So the goal is not to be right. It is to be usefully right, which is a different and more achievable thing.**

## The technique: predictions with confidence ratings

**Here is the whole thing, and it takes thirty seconds a week.**

**When you form a view on something that will resolve in a few months, write it down with a number from 1 to 10 for how confident you are.**

**"I am 7 out of 10 confident that India and China will have a further border standoff before the end of the year."**

**Then check what happened. And record both the outcome and how confident you were.**

**Do this for twenty events. Then look at the pattern.**

**That is it. That is the technique. Twenty entries will tell you more about your judgement than a year of reading.**

## Why this works when "just think about it" does not

**Because without a written confidence rating, you cannot distinguish a good guess from a bad one.**

**Think about what happens when you are wrong about something without having rated your confidence.** You simply update. The failure gets absorbed into your general sense of not being very good at these things, or you quietly reframe the prediction so it looks like it worked.

**Whereas if you wrote down "I was 8 out of 10 on this," the failure is now specific.** And specific failures can be diagnosed.

**This is why the rating matters more than the prediction.** **Anyone can make predictions. Almost nobody tracks them.**

## What you will find, and it is always the same three

**Here is what people discover, and it is remarkably consistent.**

**Error one: you overpredict escalation.**

**Almost every border standoff, every crisis, every provocation looks like it is heading somewhere serious. And then most of the time both sides de-escalate.**

**Why?** Because escalation is expensive for both parties, and the domestic and international costs of a real crisis are severe. **De-escalation is the path of least resistance for everyone involved, which is why it happens far more often than the drama of the moment suggests.**

**If your predictions consistently assume things get worse, you will be surprised repeatedly in the same direction.** And you will learn that the base rate for escalation is much lower than it feels — which is genuinely useful, because it stops you panicking.

**Error two: you underpredict boring continuity.**

**Trade will continue. Elections will happen. Governments will make unremarkable decisions. Disputes will remain unresolved without anyone doing anything dramatic about them.**

**Most of the future is this.** And if you predicted dramatic events and got boring ones right, your instincts are calibrated in a specific and correctable direction.

**Error three: you confuse loud stories with important ones.**

**You will predict outcomes for events that got enormous coverage and that turned out not to matter.** And you will fail to predict the quiet developments that did — a trade agreement, a court ruling, a bureaucratic change — because nobody wrote about them.

**That is file 5.3's base rate problem applied to your own forecasting, and it is worth expecting.**

## Calibration, explained properly

**Now the important concept, and it is worth ten minutes of your attention.**

**Calibration means your confidence matches your accuracy.** Not that you are always right — that when you say you are 80 percent confident, you are right about 80 percent of the time.

**Here is why this is the most valuable thing in this whole phase.**

**An overconfident person does not know when they are out of their depth.** They feel the same certainty about a well-documented historical fact and about a genuinely uncertain prediction about next year. **So they never slow down at the right moment.**

**A calibrated person knows.** They have a track record. **When a question falls outside their track record, they know to be careful.** And that is the whole skill — knowing which of your opinions are load-bearing.

**Which matters enormously in daily life, not just in geopolitics.** The person who knows they are good at reading transport policy and bad at predicting elections will take advice differently in those two situations. **That is calibration, and it is invisible when you have it.**

## A concrete tracking format

**Here is the whole system, if you want to use it.**

**A note, a table, a spreadsheet — anything. Four columns.**

| Date | Prediction | Confidence (1-10) | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 Mar | Standoff in Ladakh before year-end | 7 | Occurred in Sept |
| 11 Mar | India abstains on a Ukraine-related UNSC vote | 9 | Occurred |
| 18 Mar | No new India-Pakistan talks this year | 3 | Talks resumed in July |

**And then, after twenty or so entries, you sort by confidence band and count.**

**Your 9s and 10s: how many were right? Ideally nine in ten.**

**Your 1s and 2s: how many were right? Ideally one in ten.**

**If your 9s are right only six times in ten, you are overconfident by a wide margin — and now you know, which is the single most useful fact about your own thinking.**

**And there is a second, sharper test: separate the ones you rated 9 or 10 that you got wrong, and look at what they had in common.** In my experience it is nearly always the same thing — they were events that were moving fast, with recent news framing that made one outcome look inevitable.

## Why this is worth more than being right

**Let me make the argument properly, because it is the central idea of this phase.**

**Being right gives you confidence. Confidence without calibration gives you overconfidence. And overconfidence is the single most expensive mistake you can make in this subject.**

**Because being wrong about a low-stakes question costs nothing. Being wrong about a high-stakes question — where you warned your family, or made a financial decision, or concluded something about a country that shaped how you treat someone — costs a great deal.**

**And the mechanism by which wrong beliefs become expensive is almost always overconfidence rather than the belief itself.**

**The person who holds a shaky view but knows it is shaky will change it when they see contrary evidence. The person who holds the same view confidently will not.**

**So calibration is not a nice-to-have for a forecasting hobby. It is the difference between being someone who updates and someone who becomes more entrenched the longer they read.**

## The hardest part, and the honest admission

**Here is what nobody tells you about tracking predictions: you will find out that you are worse than you thought.**

**Almost everybody's first track record is embarrassing. 9s that were wrong. Confident calls that were badly wrong. And the standard response is to stop tracking.**

**Do not stop tracking. That is the moment it is worth doing.**

**Because the alternative — never checking — means you keep all the confidence and none of the information. **And that is the specific failure that turns a well-read person into a confident fool.**

**Here is the reframe that made this stick for me, and I am passing it on because it is genuinely useful: being wrong often is normal and being calibrated is the goal.** Twenty entries where your 9s come out 6-out-of-10 is not a failure. **It is twenty entries of knowing exactly how much to trust yourself on the next question.**

## What this file is not

**Calibration is not about being pessimistic.** Being well-calibrated does not mean saying "I don't know" about everything. It means knowing precisely where your confidence is warranted and where it is not.

**Nor does it require an interest in forecasting as a sport.** Twenty entries a year is enough. The point is not the tracking — it is that you now know something about your own judgement that you could not know by thinking alone.

**And it is not a reason to trust your predictions more.** It is a reason to trust your *uncertainty* more. **The valuable output is not the prediction; it is knowing which of your opinions deserve to change your mind.**
