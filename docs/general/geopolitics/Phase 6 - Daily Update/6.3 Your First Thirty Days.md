# 6.3 Your First Thirty Days

## Tomorrow morning

**This is the only part that requires a decision, and it takes two minutes.**

**Do these four things and nothing else.**

**One.** Turn off all news notifications on your phone. Not mute — off. **Everything.**

**Two.** Open a note. Bookmark four things: Reuters, Rest of World, The Wire, and a video channel from file 6.0. **That's your entire source list.** Do not add more.

**Three.** Write down your first prediction with a confidence rating. Anything you already have an opinion about. **Just one line: "I think X, I'm 6 out of 10 confident."**

**Four.** Decide when and where you will do the fifteen minutes. **Attach it to something you already do — after your morning tea, on the commute, after your evening meal.** Write the time down.

**That is the entire setup. Everything else in this course is optional.**

## The five-minute version

**For the days when fifteen minutes is not happening — and those will be most days.**

**Two minutes of headlines from one source. Two minutes locating the one story you could not place. One sentence written down.**

**That's it.** And it will keep the habit alive through weeks when nothing else would.

## Week one — the only goal

**Do not try to learn anything in week one.** That goal will fail and you will feel like the routine is not working when it is.

**The only goal for week one is: do not miss a day.**

**And remember the rule from file 6.1 — never miss twice.** On the day you would have skipped, do the five-minute version. **Not a reward for the days you did well. A minimum that keeps the chain intact.**

**That is all. Everything else is detail.**

## Weeks two and three — add one thing at a time

**Do not add things in week one, because you do not yet know whether the base routine survives.**

**In week two, add the weekly log.** One event a week, six fields, twenty minutes. **This is the habit that will matter most in three months, not the daily reading.**

**In week three, add predictions with confidence ratings** if you did not already start them on day one.

**One change at a time. Adding three things in week one is the most common way people break a routine — not by being lazy, but by making it too heavy to survive a bad day.**

## Month two — raise the difficulty

**By now the routine is either automatic or dead, and there is not much in between.**

**If it survived:** the fifteen minutes will feel easy, which means it has stopped teaching you anything. **So make it harder.** Add a second source. Move from video to a think-tank piece. Pick one country you know nothing about — Nigeria, Vietnam, Brazil — and learn its geography properly.

**If it did not survive:** do not restart. **Go back to the five-minute version and run that for two weeks.** It is not a lesser practice; it is the practice that survives contact with a bad month.

**That is the whole trick. Lower the bar until you are actually doing it, then raise it.**

## Month three — the review

**This is the only month-end task, and it is the one that makes the other eleven weeks worth anything.**

**Three things to write down.**

**One: what changed your mind?** This is the most important question and the hardest. **You will almost certainly have shifted a view during these three months. If you did not notice it happening, the routine was not working.**

**Two: which of your predictions was most wrong, and why?** Look at the reasoning, not the outcome. **Usually it is a base rate error — you assumed a pattern instead of checking a frequency.**

**Three: what is your calibration?** Sort your predictions by confidence band and count. **If your 9s are right six times out of ten, you now know something about yourself that thinking could never have told you.**

## And then: what does this become?

**After three months you have done something genuinely unusual. You have built a working model of how the world works, and — more unusually — a working model of how your own thinking works.**

**That second one is the part people underestimate.**

**Because the rest of your life contains opinions you will hold with varying confidence. Some you should trust fully, some you should update quickly. And after three months of tracking, you know roughly where the line sits.**

**That is a rare and transferable thing, and it has nothing to do with geopolitics specifically.**

## The honest ending

**You are at the end of the course, and I want to close honestly rather than triumphantly.**

**You will not remember most of what was here.** That is how memory works, and file 5.5 said so plainly. The content fades — the specific dates, the line lengths, the port names.

**What stays, if the routine holds, is smaller and stranger: four questions you reach for without thinking.**

*Where is this?*
*Who benefits if this is true?*
*How often does this actually happen?*
*How confident am I, really?*

**Those four questions will turn an event you would have found confusing into something you can reason about.** And that is the whole difference between consuming news and understanding it — not knowing more, but asking better questions faster.

**And one last thing, and it is the only instruction in this course I would actually ask you to follow.**

**Write down one sentence about what you now think differently. Not the course — your own view on something.**

**You will have changed your mind about something along the way. If you did not notice it happening, the habit was not working. That sentence is the only proof that it did.**
