# 6.1 Building Your Routine and Fighting Distraction

## Start with the honest obstacle

**The real reason people don't keep up with geopolitics is not that it's hard. It's that a phone is designed to make it hard.**

**This is worth stating plainly, because it means the difficulty is not a character flaw.**

**Every recommendation algorithm, notification system and infinite-scroll surface is built by extremely capable teams whose explicit goal is to maximise the time you spend. Your attention is the product being sold, usually to advertisers.**

**And geopolitics is the ideal content for that business model.** Unlike almost anything else you could watch, geopolitics content produces a genuine feeling of alarm, and alarm is the most reliable driver of continued attention there is. **This is not a conspiracy. It is simply what the incentives produce.**

**So the useful question is not "how do I become more disciplined." It is "what structure makes this easy by default."**

## Four rules that make habits stick

**There is genuine research on this, and the findings are surprisingly specific. Here is what actually predicts whether a habit survives.**

**Rule one: never miss twice.**

**This is the single most useful rule in habit research, and it is counter-intuitive.** People believe that one missed day ruins a streak and must be restarted. The research suggests the opposite — **doing a "minimum version" on the day you would have skipped is far more effective than maintaining a perfect streak.**

**What this means practically:** on your bad days, do the smallest possible version. **One headline read. One sentence written.** That's it.

**The logic is not motivational, it's structural.** A missed day is data — life got busy. **Two missed days is a decision.** And once it becomes a decision, you have quietly ended the habit without admitting it, and restarting requires rebuilding the whole thing.

**Rule two: attach it to something you already do.**

**Habits that are attached to existing routines survive far better than habits that require remembering.** "I will read news" fails. "I will read headlines after I make my morning tea" works.

**The mechanism is that you stop spending attention on the decision.** Every time you have to decide whether to do the habit, you re-evaluate whether it's worth it. **Attach it to something that already happens and the decision disappears.**

**Rule three: make it visible.**

**If you cannot see whether you did it, you will not know whether you did it, and the gap between the two will quietly become your memory of whether you are the kind of person who does this.**

**This can be as crude as it sounds.** A tick in a notebook, a note in your phone, a streak in an app. **The mechanism is that identity follows behaviour, not the reverse.**

**Rule four: reduce friction to near zero.**

**Every unit of effort between deciding and doing is a place to stop.** So: bookmark the sources now, subscribe now, set up the note now, download anything offline for flights.

**The goal is that starting the routine requires no decisions whatsoever.** Not "which source, what format, do I have time." Just open the bookmark.

## What to do about the phone

**This is the part most advice on habits gets wrong, because it focuses on willpower when the actual solution is structural.**

**Willpower is not the variable here. Default settings are.**

**So here are concrete changes that cost you nothing and reduce the problem substantially.**

**Turn off all news notifications.** Every one. **Not mute — off.** Because a notification is an instruction to interrupt yourself, and you do not need to give that instruction to a company.

**This single change is the highest-impact thing in this file.** Almost every person's news habit is being actively dismantled several times a day, and most people experience it as "I can't stop checking." **You are not failing at discipline. You are failing to remove an adversary from a fight you did not know you were in.**

**Create a separate place for news.** A bookmark folder, a specific app, a physical section of your desk. **The friction of opening a news app should be high.** If it takes three deliberate actions to get there, you will read deliberately. If it takes one, you will scroll.

**Remove the algorithmic feed.** This matters more than it sounds. **A chronological feed is a decision about what you see. An algorithmic feed is a decision made for you, optimised for engagement.** Both give you news. Only one of them is yours.

**If you want one practical example: setting an RSS reader (an app that gathers new articles from your chosen sources in one place) to a fixed list of sources turns news from something that happens to you into something you do.** It is unglamorous and it is the single biggest change most people can make.

**And the last one, which sounds harsh and is not: do not read news before you have done the day's actual work.** You are at your most suggestible in the morning, and news is specifically engineered to be consumed in that state. **Reading it last, once you are done, tends to take twenty minutes instead of two — because you are no longer being interrupted.**

## How to read when you have very little time

**Because some weeks you will have five minutes, not fifteen, and that is fine.**

**The five-minute version:**

**Two minutes:** headlines, one source, no clicking.

**Two minutes:** pick the story you could not place and find out where it is.

**One minute:** write one sentence about it.

**That is genuinely enough, and it will keep the habit alive through the weeks when you have no more than that.**

**And here is the thing worth knowing: the one-minute version, done for a year, will teach you more geography than any course — including this one.** Because what it trains is the single most valuable habit in the subject, which is noticing that you do not know where something is and then going and finding out.

## The three-month mark

**Around three months, most habits either have become automatic or they are dead.** There is not much in between.

**If yours has survived to three months, do something specific: change it.**

**Not abandon it — upgrade it.** By then the fifteen minutes will feel easy, which means it is no longer teaching you anything. **The right move at three months is to raise the difficulty.**

Add a second source. Start the prediction line from file 5.5. Move the reading from a video to a think-tank piece. **Take on one country you know nothing about and learn it properly.**

**This is the part people skip.** A habit that becomes easy stops producing improvement, and most people mistake the plateau for "this subject doesn't have much more in it."

## What this file is not

**This is not about becoming a disciplined person.** Some weeks you will do nothing, and that is fine. **The routine is designed to survive bad weeks, not to prevent them.**

**Nor is it about consuming less information as an end in itself.** The argument for fewer sources is that you will actually remember more — not that reading is bad. **If you genuinely enjoy reading fifty sources a day and can hold your views loosely, you will do better than someone following this file mechanically.**

**And none of this is about technology as an enemy.** Phones are not malicious. **But their incentives are not aligned with your intentions, and a good system accounts for incentives rather than relying on willpower to overcome them.**
