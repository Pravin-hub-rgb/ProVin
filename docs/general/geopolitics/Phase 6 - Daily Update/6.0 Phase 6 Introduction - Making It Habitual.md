Five phases of content. Maps, timeline, rules, India, and how to think.

**And this final phase answers a question none of the others bothered with.**

**What do you actually do, on an ordinary Tuesday, with twenty minutes?**

Because everything so far has assumed you have time. **And that is not how anyone's life works.** So this phase is deliberately practical: what to watch, what to read, how to do this in fifteen minutes rather than an hour, and where AI genuinely helps — and where it will confidently lie to you.

# Phase 6 - Daily Update

## The honest starting point

**Let me begin by admitting that Phase 6 is the least intellectually interesting part of this course.**

**The first five phases are ideas. This one is logistics.** There is no insight here you will not find elsewhere — it is a list of things worth your time and a set of prompts.

**And that is exactly why it matters most.**

**Because everything you have learned so far is currently inert.** It sits in files you have read. **It becomes knowledge only if it survives contact with your actual schedule, and your actual schedule is short, busy and gets interrupted.**

**So the standard for this phase is not "is this comprehensive." It is "will you still be doing this in three months."**

**Which means this phase is deliberately short.** Not fifteen sources, not fifty. **Six that you will actually use, and an honest account of why the other forty will make you worse rather than better.**

## The fifteen-minute version, and why it is enough

**Start here, because if you do nothing else in this phase, do this.**

**Here is the whole routine. Fifteen minutes, most days.**

**Minute 1–4: headlines.** One source, headlines only, no clicking. **Notice how many you cannot place on a map.** That number is your raw material and it is also your progress measure.

**Minute 4–7: place one.** Take the story you could not place and find out where it happened. **This is the habit that builds the map in your head.** It is three minutes and it is the single highest-value thing in this phase.

**Minute 7–13: one long thing.** A video essay, a think-tank piece, one chapter. **One thing finished beats five things skimmed**, and finishing is what makes it stick.

**Minute 13–15: one sentence.** What did you learn, and what still do not understand? **Write it down** — Phase 5.5 explained why the writing is the part that matters.

**That is the whole routine, and it is enough.**

**Because here is the thing about geopolitics that nobody warns you about: it is not a subject where you need to know everything.** There is no exam. There is no syllabus. Nobody is going to test you on the Line of Control.

**The value is not coverage. The value is that you stop being surprised.**

**And being surprised is expensive. Being surprised is what makes people anxious about the world, and anxiety is what makes people scroll.**

**Fifteen minutes a day that takes you from anxious to informed is an extraordinarily good return, and it is fifteen minutes.**

## The trap: adding more sources makes you worse

**Here is the most important thing in this phase, and it is counter-intuitive.**

**Reading more sources does not make you better informed. Below a certain threshold, it makes you worse.**

**Here is why.**

**Almost every news source in the world, taken together, agrees on about 95 percent of what is factually contested.** The disagreements are at the edges — where exactly a line runs, what a particular military facility is for, whether a claim about a treaty is accurate.

**So if you read one careful source, you learn almost everything. If you read four adversarial sources, you learn the same facts plus a large amount of noise that you now cannot distinguish from signal.**

**And the noise is not evenly distributed. The sensational is more available than the careful, so four sources gives you three opinions and a data table.**

**The practical rule: one careful source beats four loud ones, every time.**

**And "more sources" is also the reason people end up feeling constantly besieged by geopolitics.** If you read fifteen outlets on one event, you will get fifteen slightly different frames, and you will conclude the situation is chaotic and unclear. **If you read one careful source, you will conclude you know something real. Both are defensible; only one is accurate.**

## What to watch, and what to skip

**Video channels. Six, and a rule for how to use each.**

**Caspian Report** — map-based geopolitics, clear, and unusually balanced for the format. **Start here if you have never watched anything on this.**

**RealLifeLore** — genuinely the best channel on why borders are where they are. **This is the channel that makes Phase 1 make sense.**

**GeoWizard** — cartography and the physical geography underneath politics. **Watch this and Phase 0 will retrospectively explain itself.**

**Wendover Productions** — logistics, infrastructure, and how the world physically works. **The best channel nobody in geopolitics watches**, and arguably more useful than most of the ones that do.

**CGP Grey** — borders and geopolitics, thoughtful, and willing to complicate a simple question.

**Practical History** or **Kings and Generals** — military history, properly researched, for when you want the historical depth behind a conflict.

**And the rule: use a video to understand a topic, never to verify a specific fact.** For a figure, a date, or exact wording, you need a written source you can quote.

**One category to avoid with a clear head.**

**Channels that present geopolitics as a series of confident pronouncements — "China has completely encircled India," "the global south is being sold out," "this single decision will decide the century" — are almost always engagement products, not analysis.**

**Notice what they have in common: certainty, a strong framing, and an implication that you should be alarmed.** That combination is a business model, not a school of thought. **The content is often not false. The framing is engineered, and the certainty is the product.**

## What to read

**Wire services and international press. These four are enough.**

**Reuters** — the most systematically neutral wire service in the world, and the reason most international news organisations use it. **Start here when you want a fact.**

**Associated Press** — similar, and notably strong on non-Western and domestic coverage that Western outlets miss.

**BBC** — careful about attributing contested claims, which is a small thing that matters a lot.

**Rest of World** or **Caixin** — pick one. Technology outside the West, and independent Chinese business journalism respectively. **Both are models of original reporting, and both cover regions the others barely touch.**

**On India specifically, because you will want depth the wire services do not carry:**

**The Wire** — genuinely high quality, and the best Indian outlet on security, policing and Kashmir. **It does investigative work rather than explaining the day's news, which is why it will occasionally be ahead of a story everyone else is covering late.**

**Scroll.in** and **The Hindu** — detailed, and ideologically different from each other, which is what makes reading both useful.

**And one that is genuinely different: Mint or Business Standard** for economics, because most Indian political coverage treats economic detail as background and it usually is the story.

## The sources worth spending money on, if you spend any

**You do not need to pay for anything. That is important to say, because the paid-intelligence industry has spent decades implying otherwise.**

**But if you want to spend money, here is where it actually goes.**

**The Economist** — outstanding, and openly ideologically leaning. **Read it knowing that, which is exactly why it is worth reading.** The knowledge and the slant are separable, and separating them is the skill.

**Foreign Policy, The Diplomat, or Rest of World** — long-form, well-edited, and better on region-specific depth than the daily press.

**FT or Nikkei Asia** — if your interest is economic rather than military, these are the best in the world, and Nikkei is independent of China in a way most people assume.

**And the single most valuable paid resource, if you can justify it to yourself: SIPRI's data.** Military expenditure, nuclear arsenals, arms transfers — all public, all checkable, and the actual primary source rather than analysis of it.

## Now the AI question

**You came here because you wanted to be able to use AI to keep up with events you slept through. That is a legitimate use, and it is worth doing.**

**But it needs a specific warning first, because the failure mode is not subtle.**

**Where AI is reliable:** explaining a concept you do not understand, giving you the basic structure of an issue you have never encountered, generating the questions you should be asking, and summarising a document you have already found.

**Where AI is unreliable, and this is the important list:** specific dates, specific numbers, specific quotes, the current state of a fast-moving situation, and anything about a dispute where the two sides use the same words to mean different things.

**The reason is straightforward: language models learn from text, and text contains both accurate and false statements in similar proportion. They learn the pattern of an answer, not the reliability of a claim.**

**So an AI asked "when was the Simla Agreement signed" will give you an answer with total confidence — and that answer will usually be right, because 1972 is in thousands of sources.**

**But asked "what is the current troop deployment along the LAC," it will give you an answer with the same total confidence, and that answer will probably be invented.**

**The distinction is not about accuracy of the model. It is about whether the specific fact you need is widely and consistently reported.** Widely reported stable facts are usually fine. Contested, recent, specific details are where it will invent with total fluency.

**And the deeper problem: it cannot tell you when it is doing this.** There is no warning. It produces the invented answer with the same formatting, the same confidence, and the same apparent sourcing as the accurate one.

**So here is the rule that actually works, and it is short: use AI for structure and explanation, and never for facts you intend to repeat.**

**If you need a fact, ask it to tell you how to find the fact — ask for the primary documents, the sources, the agencies that would report this. That use is genuinely excellent, because AI is very good at knowing what the landscape of sources looks like.**

## The prompts

**These are the prompts worth having. Each has a note on why it works, because a prompt you do not understand is a prompt you will misuse.**

**First, the event breakdown — the core one.**

```text
I know nothing about this and have been avoiding it. Explain the situation in the Middle East shipping lanes.

Structure the answer as:
1. The geography — where exactly is this, and why does the location matter
2. The history in one paragraph — how did we get here
3. The main actors — every party with a real interest, including ones you might not mention
4. What each side actually wants, in their own framing
5. The single most important thing a beginner usually gets wrong about this

At the end, tell me specifically which of your claims I should verify before
I repeat them to anyone else, and where to verify them.
```

**Why that last line matters.** It is the most useful part of the prompt. **You are asking the model to identify its own weakest points, which focuses your checking.** And it costs nothing.

**Second, the map prompt — for placing things you cannot locate.**

```text
I have been reading about [EVENT] and I cannot work out where this is.
Give me the specific location — country, region, and what it is near or
between. Then explain what that location means strategically: what
does being there allow, and what does it prevent? Use plain language
and do not assume I know any geography.
```

**Third, the steelman prompt — the most valuable one for this course.**

```text
Help me understand an argument I disagree with.

State [COUNTRY'S POSITION] in its strongest and most serious form, as
their best analysts would put it. Use the vocabulary they actually use,
not a translation into my framing. Then tell me what the strongest
counter-argument is, and honestly, what evidence would settle it.

Do not soften either side, and do not tell me which to agree with.
```

**Why it works.** This is file 5.2's exercise, automated. **And telling it explicitly not to soften either side and not to say which side to take is what keeps it from drifting into false balance.**

**Fourth, the check-your-understanding prompt.**

```text
Quiz me on [TOPIC] one question at a time. After each answer, tell me
what specifically I got wrong and what the correct version would be.
Start easy and get harder. Ask me about the things people commonly
get wrong rather than only the facts.
```

**Fifth, the "what am I missing" prompt — for when something feels off.**

```text
I have been following [TOPIC] for a few months and something still does
not sit right for me, though I cannot say what.

Ask me five questions that would help me work out what my blind spot
is. Do not give me information yet — only ask the questions. Wait for
my answers before explaining anything.
```

**Why that one is clever.** It gets the model to diagnose your thinking rather than feed you more information. **And the instruction to wait is important — without it, it will just start explaining.**

**Sixth, the primary sources prompt.**

```text
For [EVENT/TOPIC], tell me which primary sources I should go and read
directly — official statements, treaties, court rulings, satellite
imagery, parliamentary records, and the specific think tank reports
that actually cite documents rather than summarising coverage.

Rank them by how reliable they are and say why each one is reliable.
Do not summarise the sources for me. I want to read them myself.
```

**Why this is the most valuable prompt in the list.** Because it converts AI from a source into a **research assistant**, which is a much better use. **A model is very good at knowing what the landscape of sources looks like, and much worse at telling you what is in a source it cannot currently read.**

## What this phase is not

**You do not need any of this.** You can follow geopolitics through one good newspaper and one YouTube channel, and you will be better informed than most people who follow fifty sources badly.

**Nor is the AI section a substitute for reading.** It is a way of deciding what to read, and of understanding what you have found. **Used the other way round, it will make you confidently wrong, and confidently wrong is worse than simply uninformed** — because you will act on it.

**And the honest thing to close on: this phase is the least valuable content in the course and the most likely to be the reason any of it survives.** That is not a criticism of it. It is an accurate description of how habits work. **Nobody changes their life because of an insight. They change it because of a routine that fits into the life they already have.**

## The last file

**You are at the end of the course. And the honest thing to say about the whole thing is this.**

**You will not remember most of it.** That is how memory works, and file 5.5 was honest about it. What you will remember, if the routine holds, is not the content — it is a handful of tools that you reach for without thinking: where is this, who benefits, what is the base rate, how often does this actually happen.

**And that is enough. Because those four questions will take an event you would have found confusing into something you can reason about — and that is the entire difference between consuming news and understanding it.**

**One final thing, and it is the only instruction in this course I would ask you to actually follow.**

**Write down one sentence about what you now think differently. Not the course — your own view on something.** You will have changed your mind about something along the way, and if you have not noticed it, the habit was not working.
