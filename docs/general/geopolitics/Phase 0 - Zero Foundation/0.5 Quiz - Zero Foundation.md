# 0.5 Quiz - Zero Foundation
## MCQ

Q: The Montevideo Convention's four criteria for being a state include a permanent population, a defined territory and a government. What is the fourth?
A. A standing army recognised by neighbouring countries
B. The capacity to enter into relations with other states
C. A written constitution adopted after independence
D. Membership of at least one international organisation
> B. The fourth criterion is the capacity to enter into relations with other states. It is the only criterion conferred from outside, which makes recognition partly constitutive — a place cannot decide for itself that it is a country.

---

Q: Roughly what percentage of Earth's surface is covered by ocean?
A. About 29 percent
B. About 50 percent
C. About 71 percent
D. About 90 percent
> C. Ocean covers roughly 71 percent of the surface and land roughly 29 percent. The common mental picture inverts this by treating land as the default and sea as background.

---

Q: Which continent is the largest by area, and which is the second largest?
A. Africa, then Asia
B. Asia, then Africa
C. Europe, then Antarctica
D. South America, then North America
> B. Asia is largest at about 44.6 million square kilometres and Africa second at about 30.4 million. Note that Africa has about a fifth of the world's people, which shows why the area and population rankings look nothing alike.

---

Q: India extends between which latitudes and longitudes?
A. 8°4′N to 37°6′N and 68°7′E to 97°25′E
B. 8°4′S to 37°6′S and 68°7′W to 97°25′W
C. 0°N to 37°N and 68°E to 97°E
D. 21°30′N to 37°N and 68°E to 97°E
> A. India runs from about 8°4′ north to 37°6′ north and 68°7′ east to 97°25′ east. Option D is the trap — 21°30′ is roughly the latitude of Delhi, not the southern edge of the country.

---

Q: India uses a half-hour time offset, IST at UTC+5:30, while most countries use whole hours. Why?
A. It preserves daylight in the northern plains
B. India sits at about 82.5° east, halfway between 75° and 90°E
C. British colonial administration standardised it for rail timetables
D. It aligns Indian trading hours with the London market opening
> B. The Earth rotates fifteen degrees per hour, so time zones are fifteen degrees wide. At roughly 82.5° east India falls exactly half an hour past a whole time zone, which is where UTC+5:30 comes from.

---

Q: What is the only continent with no permanent population?
A. Asia
B. Australia
C. Antarctica
D. Europe
> C. Antarctica has no permanent population and no recognised national claims, because the Antarctic Treaty freezes territorial claims. It is one of the few cases where the international system prevented a land grab rather than complaining about one afterwards.

---

Q: The Southern Ocean was formally recognised as a distinct ocean in which year, and on what basis?
A. 1945, along with the founding of the United Nations
B. 1991, at the end of the Cold War
C. 2000, by the International Hydrographic Organization
D. 2015, under the Paris Agreement
> C. The IHO recognised the Southern Ocean in 2000, defining its northern edge at 60° south. That legally separated Antarctica from the other three oceans, which is why it matters beyond a technicality.

---

Q: The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 divided much of Africa along straight lines. Why were the lines drawn straight?
A. Colonial powers believed ethnic groups did not exist in the interior
B. Following rivers and coastlines required surveying, which was slow and costly
C. Treaties prohibited following natural features along colonial borders
D. The lines were later simplified by cartographers for printing
> B. Straight lines required only a compass and an agreement, while surveying was slow and expensive. The convenience was optimised for the empire at the time, and the cost fell on populations not consulted for another century.

---

Q: Which statement best describes the difference between an artificial and a natural border?
A. Natural borders are legitimate, artificial borders are colonial
B. Natural borders follow physical features, artificial borders follow agreed lines on a map
C. Natural borders exist in Africa, artificial borders exist in Europe
D. Natural borders are only found between maritime nations
> B. A natural border follows a physical feature such as a river or mountain range, while an artificial border follows a line people agreed on. Almost every real border is a mixture of both.

---

Q: Why are artificial borders said to generate more political conflict than natural ones?
A. Artificial borders are always longer than natural ones
B. They were typically imposed without local consent, so someone is always owed something
C. Natural borders are always defended by stronger armies
D. Artificial borders have never been ratified by any treaty
> B. The grievance has an address: a line drawn by outsiders over the heads of the people it divides creates a lasting claim. Natural borders are politically calmer but practically harder because rivers move and mountains are ambiguous.

---

Q: Bhanga, the largest Indian enclave inside Bangladesh, is significant because it illustrates which problem?
A. A country can administer territory it cannot physically reach
B. A border drawn without survey
C. A river that changed course after a treaty was signed
D. A country with a coastline but no ports
> A. About a hundred and ten Indian enclaves sit inside Bangladesh, so residents are Indian citizens living inside Bangladesh and cut off from India. The legal arrangement created an administrative problem that has never been resolved.

---

Q: Which four things does this course identify as what countries compete over?
A. Land, population, resources and position
B. Land, ideology, population and military size
C. Resources, trade, alliances and population
D. Territory, economy, history and geography
> A. Land, people, resources and position. The most useful habit is asking what a state has a lot of, what it lacks, and who fills the gap.

---

Q: Switzerland is wealthy despite having no coastline, few natural resources and under nine million people. What explains it?
A. It maintains one of the world's largest standing armies
B. It converted its landlocked position into high-value services that need no bulk shipping
C. It controls a major seaport on the Rhine
D. It has the largest land area in Europe
> B. Landlocked states cannot move bulk goods cheaply, so Switzerland specialised in banking, insurance and arbitration — value that can be delivered without physical cargo. This is the classic case of position defeating size.

---

Q: Why is Singapore rich despite having almost no land, no farmland and no natural resources?
A. Its oil reserves are larger than Russia's
B. It sits at the narrowest point of the Strait of Malacca, one of the world's busiest shipping passages
C. It has the world's largest merchant navy
D. It is a major oil exporter
> B. Position at a chokepoint is the entire basis of Singapore's economy. Being at the narrowest point rather than merely near it is what turned a small island into a wealthy state.

---

Q: The comparison between Norway, Venezuela and oil-rich parts of Africa is used to show that resources produce wealth when what else about a country?
A. Its borders are widely recognised internationally
B. Its institutions and income distribution are strong
C. Its population is under ten million
D. It has a large coastline
> B. The same resource has produced durable wealth in Norway and collapse in Venezuela, so the variable that matters is institutions and how widely income is distributed. Resources shape incentives rather than delivering outcomes.

---

Q: The United States does not fit neatly into the four dials of land, people, resources and position. What does this suggest?
A. The four dials are an incomplete framework for measuring power
B. The US is weaker than its reputation suggests
C. Some states are strong because of what others need from them, not what they hold
D. Military capability is the only true measure of power
> C. The US relies on alliances, a financial system others depend on, and technical capability. Recognising whether a state is strong from what it holds or strong from what others need is a genuine analytical skill.

---

Q: A map uses a Mercator projection and compares the size of Russia to the size of India. What is the problem?
A. The two countries have never been measured accurately
B. Mercator inflates high latitudes, so Russia appears far larger than it is relative to India
C. Mercator only works above the equator
D. The map is using an outdated border for India
> B. Mercator's scale factor is one divided by the cosine of latitude, so it roughly doubles at 60°N and climbs steeply beyond. Russia is exaggerated relative to equatorial countries, which makes size comparisons meaningless on such a map.

---

Q: On a Mercator projection, Greenland appears roughly comparable in size to Africa. In reality, how do they compare?
A. Africa is about twice the size of Greenland
B. They are genuinely close in area
C. Africa is about fourteen times the size of Greenland
D. Greenland is about fourteen times the size of Africa
> C. Africa is roughly 30.4 million square kilometres against Greenland's 2.16 million, about fourteen times larger. This single comparison is the clearest demonstration of area distortion.

---

Q: Which projection preserves area at the cost of shape, and has therefore been politically contested?
A. Mercator
B. Natural Earth
C. Gall-Peters
D. Equirectangular
> C. Gall-Peters is equal-area, so relative sizes are honest but shapes are badly distorted. Its use in education has been read by some governments as a critique, as in the Bosnian textbook case under the Dayton settlement.

---

Q: Natural Earth projection is described as a compromise. What does that mean for someone reading a map?
A. It preserves shape and area exactly, like a true map of the world
B. It keeps shapes recognisable and relative sizes roughly honest, accepting moderate distortion of both
C. It is a decorative projection with no practical use
D. It is accurate only below the equator
> B. It is a choice, like every other projection. It is a sensible compromise, not a neutral one, and that is worth remembering when reading any map at all.

---

Q: Why does the centre of a world map matter when reading it?
A. It determines which countries are measured more precisely
B. What is centred reads as central and what sits at the edge reads as marginal
C. Centred countries always have larger populations
D. It is purely a printing convention with no effect on perception
> B. Centring works on intuition rather than argument, which is what makes it persuasive. Shifting the frame from the Atlantic to the Pacific changes the implied centre of the world without changing anything in reality.

---

Q: Why can a Mercator projection not represent the poles honestly?
A. The poles have no permanent population
B. The poles are infinitely far from the equator on a Mercator projection
C. Mercator projections only work in the Northern Hemisphere
D. The poles have no coastline to trace
> B. Distance approaches infinity as latitude approaches ninety, so Antarctica has to be drawn enormous or dropped. A projection that cannot draw part of the planet is not a neutral formatting choice.

---

Q: The Line of Actual Control between India and China is described as existing physically while being agreed by nobody. What does that illustrate?
A. That maps are always accurate in disputed regions
B. That the Montevideo criteria cannot resolve competing claims to the same ground
C. That mountain ranges move over time
D. That the UN has withdrawn its recognition of the border
> B. Both sides satisfy the criteria as states, and neither has an agreed line on the ground. The criteria tell you they are states; they say nothing about who owns which ridge.

---

Q: What is the most useful question to ask about a map being used to make a geopolitical argument?
A. Does it use satellite imagery rather than a drawn outline
B. What projection is it, whose borders does it draw, and what is centred
C. Was it published by a government or a newspaper
D. Does it include country names and capitals
> B. Projection, borders and centre point cover the main ways a map carries an argument invisibly. The other features are presentation choices that do not change what the picture is claiming.

## TrueFalse

Q: Recognising a state implies approving of how its government treats the people inside it.
- True
- False
> False. Recognition is about sovereignty and external relations, not about domestic conduct, which is why states with very different systems all sit as equal UN members.

---

Q: Antarctica has no permanent population and its territorial claims are frozen by treaty.
- True
- False
> True. The Antarctic Treaty freezes existing claims rather than resolving them, and no state maintains a permanent population there.

---

Q: A continent is a unit of political power because it has a single government.
- True
- False
> False. Continents have no government, no population count and no foreign policy. Only countries sign treaties and control territory, which is why geopolitics analyses states.

---

Q: The three dials of competition are land, population and resources only.
- True
- False
> False. There are four, and the fourth is position — where a country sits relative to trade routes and chokepoints. Position explains outcomes that size and resources cannot.

---

Q: A large population becomes a strategic resource only when paired with health, education, infrastructure and energy access.
- True
- False
> True. Population is potential rather than asset. Without those supports, the same number becomes a liability and any interruption to food, water or energy flows becomes a crisis.

---

Q: Rivers make reliable international borders because their courses are fixed over centuries.
- True
- False
> False. Rivers shift, and the Ganges has moved enough to take whole districts with it, which is why the Farakka Barrage remains a live dispute between India and Bangladesh.

---

Q: The Radcliffe Line was drawn by British authorities in the weeks before Indian independence in 1947.
- True
- False
> True. Cyril Radcliffe chaired the Boundary Commission and the award was published in August 1947, creating India and Pakistan — and it was redrawn in 1971 to create Bangladesh.

---

Q: A map projection can preserve both the shape and the area of every region simultaneously.
- True
- False
> False. Every projection sacrifices at least one of shape, area or distance. That trade-off is the definition of the projection.

---

Q: One degree of latitude measures roughly the same distance everywhere on Earth.
- True
- False
> True. It is about 111 kilometres at any latitude, unlike a degree of longitude, which shrinks by the cosine of the latitude and vanishes at the poles.

---

Q: India is the country with the largest population in the world as of 2023.
- True
- False
> True. India passed China in 2023 with a population above 1.4 billion, and India's much younger median age gives it a time-limited demographic advantage.

---

Q: A country can claim territory that it does not effectively control.
- True
- False
> True. Kashmir is the clearest example, which is why the phrase "effective control" exists in international usage to acknowledge the gap between claim and administration.

## ShortAnswer

Q: A friend says "China is huge and Russia is small, so China will always win." How would you correct this using the four dials?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would point out that size is only one dial, and often the weakest. Russia is the largest country by area but its land is largely empty, hard to defend and contributes little to its economy. What actually determines outcomes is the combination of what a country has a lot of, what it lacks, and who supplies the gap — plus things the four dials do not cover at all, like alliances, institutions and technology. So I would ask what Russia actually lacks and who supplies it, rather than comparing areas.

</details>

---

Q: You see a social media post comparing Greenland and Africa to argue about immigration or land rights. What would you say?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would ask what projection the map uses. If it is Mercator, the size comparison is meaningless, because the projection inflates high latitudes and Africa is genuinely about fourteen times Greenland's area. The distortion is not a conspiracy — it is four and a half centuries of a projection built for navigation — but it is a distortion, and using it to make a point about land is an error even if the conclusion happens to be one I agree with.

</details>

---

Q: Why does "effective control" exist as a phrase in international law rather than countries simply claiming all their territory?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because control and claim diverging is common, and international language needs a way to describe the reality without pretending the divergence does not exist. Recognising effective control lets both sides acknowledge a ground reality without conceding the legal claim, which keeps diplomacy possible. Without such a phrase, the situation would be describable only as an unresolved war or as full sovereignty, and neither description is accurate.

</details>

---

Q: One country has abundant resources but is poor and unstable. Another has very few resources and is wealthy and stable. What has happened, according to this framework?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** The resource has not been the deciding variable. What differs is the institutions surrounding it and how widely the income is distributed, which is exactly the Norway-versus-Venezuela comparison. Resources shape incentives and make a country strategically important to others, but what a state does with the revenue depends on everything else about it, and no framework that treats extraction as a scorecard will predict the outcome.

</details>

---

Q: How would you explain the difference between an artificial and a natural border to someone who says "the Himalayas are India's natural border with China"?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would agree that the Himalayas are why that boundary exists where it does, but point out that a mountain range is a wide belt of peaks and passes rather than a line, so "the crest" still has to be decided by people. That is why a line was drawn in 1914 and why it is still disputed. Nature sets the region; the agreement sets the line, and the two rarely match — which is the real reason the phrase "natural border" should not be treated as settling anything.

</details>
