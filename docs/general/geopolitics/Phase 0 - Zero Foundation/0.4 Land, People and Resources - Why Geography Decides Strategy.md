The previous file closed by suggesting that the thing a country actually needs might not be land at all — it might be people, or oil, or food. That is the right question to ask, because it explains why the largest countries on a map are frequently not the most powerful ones, and it is the question that turns geography from something you memorise into something that predicts.

# 0.4 Land, People and Resources - Why Geography Decides Strategy

## Four things, not one

If you take a single framework away from this phase, make it this one. Countries compete over four things, and every geopolitical move you will ever read about is an attempt to increase, protect or defend access to one of them.

The first is **land** — physical territory, which provides space, farmland, mineral deposits and a buffer against neighbours.

The second is **people** — population, which provides labour, consumers, soldiers and legitimacy.

The third is **resources** — water, food, energy, and the industrial minerals that modern manufacturing runs on.

And the fourth is **position** — where you sit relative to trade routes, chokepoints and other people's territory. This is the one most often overlooked and disproportionately powerful.

Each of these four can be plentiful while the others are scarce, and that mismatch is the engine of most international friction. The interesting question about any country is never simply "how big is it," but the specific shape of its imbalance: **what does it have a lot of, what does it lack, and who can supply the gap?**

## Land: size is the weakest of the four

The instinct on seeing a large country is to assume power, and that instinct is often wrong.

Russia is the largest country on Earth by area, roughly 17.1 million square kilometres, and Canada is close behind. Neither has ever been the most powerful state on the planet. The reason is that land by itself does nothing. Land that nobody lives on, cannot farm, cannot mine and cannot cross is mostly a liability, because it is expensive to defend and contributes nothing to defend.

What large empty countries actually get from their size is a buffer. Russia's size has historically meant that no invader could reach Moscow without first defeating an enormous stretch of territory, and that has shaped Russian strategy more than any other factor. Canada has never needed to fight anyone, which is a function of geography and of being next to the United States.

India sits at the interesting middle. At about 3.29 million square kilometres it is the seventh largest country in the world, covering a little over two percent of the planet's land surface. That is substantial but not commanding. India's land is productive, densely settled across much of the subcontinent, and sits in a position with coastlines on both sides — which turns out to matter more than the area itself.

## People: the resource that can turn into anything

Population is the most ambiguous of the four, because the same number can be a strength or a burden depending entirely on what happens to those people.

India passed China as the most populous country in 2023, sitting above 1.4 billion people against China's roughly 1.41 billion. On current projections India will add more people over the next two decades than the entire population of most large countries. That produces a strategic picture with no close historical parallel.

The optimistic reading is a **demographic dividend** — a large working-age population relative to dependents, which in principle means cheap manufacturing, a large domestic consumer market, and a military that is hard to exhaust.

The pessimistic reading is that a billion-plus population with limited resources requires enormous, reliable flows of energy, food, water and jobs, and that any disruption to those flows becomes a crisis.

Both readings are correct at once, and which one dominates in any given decade depends on infrastructure and institutions rather than on the headcount.

What makes India's demographic position genuinely unusual is the *combination* of a very large population and a relatively young age structure. India's median age is in the high twenties, while China's is around forty. China has already begun dealing with the consequences of an ageing population and a shrinking workforce, a problem India has not yet reached. That is one of the most underappreciated strategic advantages India currently holds, and it is a time-limited one.

The key insight to carry forward is that people are not automatically a resource. People become a resource when they have health, education, manufacturing capacity and access to energy. People without those are a liability — and the difference between those two outcomes is decided by policy and infrastructure, not by geography.

## Resources: what you do not have, someone else controls

Resources are the clearest case of strategic dependence, because a country cannot manufacture oil, lithium or fertile soil on demand.

India is a striking example because it is not well endowed. Despite having some of the world's largest coal reserves and a reasonable share of arable land, India has to import roughly eighty-five percent of its crude oil, most of its natural gas in liquid form, and the large majority of the cobalt, lithium and nickel used in batteries and electronics. Many of these come from countries far away — the Gulf for oil, and for minerals from places like the Democratic Republic of Congo, Chile and Australia. This is not a failure of planning; it is the ordinary condition of a large industrial economy, because most countries are resource-poor in something.

The strategic consequence runs both ways, and it is easy to see only your own side of it. Being dependent means vulnerability: a price shock, an embargo or a closed sea lane hits you hard. But for the supplier it means leverage: a state sitting on a resource others need has an interest that outlasts most other alliances. That is a large part of why Gulf states have mattered so consistently in global politics despite modest military power, and a large part of why disputes over oil transit routes are settled with unusual seriousness.

There is also a trap here that is well documented and worth knowing, because it shows that resources can be a liability when the rest of the state's structure is weak. When a country's national income depends overwhelmingly on extraction, the money often flows to a small elite while the majority get very little, and the result is corruption and instability. Oil-rich countries with strong institutions and a small population, such as Norway, have used the same resource to build durable wealth. Oil-rich countries with weak institutions, in Venezuela and parts of Africa, have used it to collapse. **The resource is the same. The difference is everything else about the country.**

## Position: the one that is hardest to see and hardest to fake

The fourth dial is position, and it is the one that generates the most surprising outcomes. Two examples make it obvious.

**Switzerland** has no coastline, no colonies, a population of under nine million, and no significant military. And it is, per person, among the wealthiest countries on Earth. It is landlocked — meaning it depends entirely on its neighbours for access to world markets — and it turned that apparent weakness into a position by specialising in the one thing a landlocked country can do without leaving: high-value services that can be delivered without physical bulk. Banking, insurance, arbitration, and diplomacy.

**Singapore** is the mirror image. It is a city-state of about six million people on a single island of a few hundred square kilometres, with no natural resources, no farmland and almost nothing you would call strategic. It sits at the narrowest point of the Strait of Malacca, which is one of the busiest shipping passages on Earth. **Being at that chokepoint (a narrow sea passage ships must use, giving its controller leverage), rather than merely near it, is the entire reason Singapore exists as a rich country.** It has an economy larger than that of many nations many times its size.

Neither outcome could have been predicted from size, and both could have been predicted from position. This is the single most useful thing to understand about the geography of power: **where you are can matter more than what you have.**

The Indian Ocean basin is where this becomes concrete for an Indian reader. The overwhelming majority of India's trade moves by sea, and the routes that carry it run west toward the Gulf and east through Malacca. India does not own most of the coastline along those routes, it does not control the straits at either end, and it is not even the biggest economy drawing on this basin — China and Japan depend on the same water far more heavily. That dependence is the reason the Indian Ocean has become the central arena of Indian foreign policy, and it is the subject of Phase 4.

## Putting the four dials together

The useful habit here is to run all four dials on any country you read about, especially ones you have no connection to. Asking what a state has a lot of, what it lacks, and who fills the gap takes about thirty seconds and tells you more about its foreign policy than an hour of commentary will.

There is one important addition to the framework, because it explains the superpower that fits none of the dials cleanly. The United States has no overwhelming resource wealth, its land was settled a long time ago, and its population is a small share of the world's. What it has instead is a network of alliances, a financial system everyone else needs, and unmatched technical and military capability — none of which is on our list, and all of which is more durable than oil. Some countries are strong because of what they hold, and some are strong because of what others need from them. Recognising which kind you are looking at is a real analytical skill, and we will return to it when we look at power in Phase 3.

## What this file is not

None of these four dials is permanent. Countries find deposits, lose them and invent substitutes; populations grow and shrink; and positions can be made irrelevant by new technology, which is exactly what happened when shipping containers made many traditional naval chokepoints far less strategically decisive than they had been. A framework that treats any of the four as fixed will mislead you.

It is also not a ranking. There is no formula that combines the four dials into a score called national power, and anyone offering one is dressing up a guess. These are lenses for thinking, not inputs to a calculator.

And the most common misreading of all: none of this says that resources make a country dangerous or that their absence makes it weak. It says resources shape what a country will try to do. Whether it succeeds is a question of capability and circumstance, and the countries with the most resources have frequently been the ones least able to use them well.
