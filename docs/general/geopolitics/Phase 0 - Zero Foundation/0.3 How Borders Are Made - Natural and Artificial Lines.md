The last line of the previous file promised something specific: most of the borders that cause trouble in the world did not come from geography, they came from somebody with a ruler.

That sounds wrong at first, because the Himalayas really are between India and China, and the Atlantic really is between Europe and America. Geography obviously draws borders. So what exactly does "somebody with a ruler" mean, and when does it override a mountain?

# 0.3 How Borders Are Made - Natural and Artificial Lines

## Where borders actually come from

There are two broad families of border, and confusing the two causes more bad analysis than any other single idea in this course.

A **natural border** follows a physical feature — a mountain range, a river, a desert, a coastline. It has one enormous advantage: it is visible on the ground. Two communities separated by a mountain can both point at the same ridge and agree where the line goes. The feature exists whether or not anyone drew it.

An **artificial border** follows a line that people agreed on, usually in a treaty, often by drawing a straight line on a map and then forcing people to live with it. It has one enormous disadvantage: it exists only on paper, in one specific language, in one specific year, and the people affected often had no say in it.

The crucial insight is that natural borders are not actually natural in the sense of being stable and obvious. Rivers move. Mountain ranges are wide belts of peaks and passes, not lines. Deserts are gradients, not edges. The moment you zoom in, nature turns out to be vaguer than a border needs to be, and somebody has to draw a precise line somewhere. The result is that almost every border on Earth is a mixture: nature sets the region, people draw the line, and the two rarely agree perfectly.

That hybrid nature is the whole problem, and it is best understood through India's own longest border, which we will study in Phase 1. For now, a cleaner way to see the distinction is to look at the two extremes.

## The artificial extreme: lines drawn in an afternoon

The clearest examples of artificial borders are the ones drawn by Europeans in the nineteenth century, when they were partitioning territory they had no intention of ever living in. The Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885 did this across Africa, and the straight lines it produced are still visible on a map of the continent today. Nobody at the conference had visited most of the places being divided, and the people who lived there were not represented.

The reason those lines were drawn straight was practical rather than cynical. Following a coastline or a river required surveying, which was slow and expensive. A line of latitude and longitude required nothing except a compass and an agreement. So empires optimised for convenience, and the cost of that convenience was paid by populations that would not be consulted for another century.

India inherited three of the most consequential lines of this kind in its immediate neighbourhood, and all three will be studied in detail in Phase 1: the Radcliffe Line drawn in 1947 to divide British India, the McMahon Line drawn in 1914 between British India and Tibet, and the Durand Line drawn in 1893 between British India and Afghanistan. Each has a specific date, a specific document, and a specific set of people who did not consent to it.

The visible result is that India, Pakistan and Bangladesh have shapes that no natural feature produced. Bangladesh is almost entirely surrounded by India — on three sides and across a river on the fourth. That single geometric fact has had an enormous effect on its politics, its trade, its foreign policy options, and its relationship with its much larger neighbour, and none of that follows from the subcontinent's terrain. It follows from a line drawn in about five weeks in the summer of 1947.

The other consequence of straight lines is that they cut through people. When a line ignores ethnic, linguistic or tribal distribution, it splits communities that were one. That does not automatically produce a war, but it produces a permanent grievance with a specific address, which is much more dangerous than a general sense of injustice. Borders drawn by agreement tend to be stable for centuries. Borders drawn over the heads of the people living there tend to be contested for as long as both groups remember.

## The natural extreme: borders that move on their own

If artificial borders are too neat to be real, natural borders are too messy.

Take the India-Bangladesh border, which is unusual and instructive because a large part of it genuinely is made of rivers — the Padma, the Meghna, the Teesta and others. Both governments have argued for decades over which channel is the legally recognised boundary, because the rivers have moved. The Ganges in particular has shifted its course substantially over the last century, taking whole districts with it, which is why a barrage built on one bank in the 1960s remains a live diplomatic issue. When you build a structure on a river and the river then leaves, you have a dispute that no treaty drafted a century ago could possibly have anticipated.

Mountains are the same story at a different scale. A mountain range is not a wall, it is a belt, sometimes hundreds of kilometres wide, full of passes and valleys and settlements. When a border is said to "follow the Himalayan crest," that is shorthand for an argument that is still going on about which crest. The line along the India-China boundary in the western sector is the clearest live example, and it has its own file in Phase 1.

Then there are the oddities that occur when a moving river or a straight line leaves a piece of territory cut off from its parent.

An **enclave** is a piece of one country completely surrounded by another. An **exclave** is a piece of a country separated from its main territory by a third country.

The Bangladesh case is remarkable: roughly a hundred and ten Indian enclaves sit inside Bangladesh, and around fifty Bangladeshi enclaves sit inside India. The largest Indian one, Bhanga, is about ten square kilometres and contains around four thousand people, all of whom are Indian citizens living inside Bangladesh and reachable from India only by passing through Bangladesh.

These are not historical curiosities. They are still administered, they still create customs and policing problems, and they still generate headlines.

## Why one kind produces more trouble than the other

Once you see both families clearly, a pattern emerges that is worth carrying through the rest of this course: **artificial borders produce more political conflict, and natural borders produce more practical difficulty.**

The reason is about consent and changeability.

An artificial line is politically charged because it was imposed by people with no local mandate, which means somebody is always owed something. A natural line is politically calmer, because it is at least defensible to both sides as "the natural thing" — but it is practically difficult, because rivers drift and mountains are ambiguous, so practically there is always friction about exactly where the line falls.

This is a genuinely useful diagnostic, and you can apply it to any new dispute you read about. When a border conflict is fundamentally about *who is entitled to this territory*, look for a line drawn by outsiders. When it is fundamentally about *where exactly this river runs*, look for a natural feature and expect the argument to be technical, legalistic and occasionally absurd, involving hydrology and survey reports rather than armies. Both are real conflicts. The difference in their character is not a difference in seriousness — it is a difference in cause.

There is one more source of borders worth naming before we move on, because it is the one most relevant to modern Europe and therefore to a lot of the news. Borders are also created by **collapse**. When a state breaks up — the Soviet Union, Yugoslavia, Czechoslovakia, the Ottoman Empire — the successor states inherit whatever lines existed at the moment of the breakup, usually lines drawn decades earlier by people with no idea this would happen. Several of the most persistent conflicts in Europe today are essentially arguments about whether a border drawn in 1919 or 1945 was the correct one at the moment everything fell apart.

## What this file is not

Natural does not mean fair, and artificial does not mean illegitimate. A straight line drawn by an outsider is not automatically wrong — some straight lines are the least bad available option, and drawing a border along an ethnic line through a valley where two communities have fought for a century would be worse. The question is usually not whether a line is straight or crooked, but whether the people it divides had a say. Keep that distinction available, because a great deal of commentary abandons it.

Nor is any border truly permanent. Borders change constantly through treaties, wars, arbitration and quiet administrative adjustment. The phrase "natural border" does not mean a border that will never move. Rivers that are borders move, and where they move, the border can be renegotiated — which is exactly why river borders are so frequently the subject of international tribunals.

And a border's existence does not mean the relationship is hostile. Plenty of countries share a long border and have been close allies for decades. Sharing terrain tells you where two countries meet, not what they feel about each other. Geography creates the conditions; politics decides what happens in them. We will come back to this in Phase 4 when we look at India and China, who share a contested boundary and are simultaneously each other's largest trading partners.
