# 0.1 The World Map - Seven Continents and Five Oceans

## Start with a very simple question

Most people think this file will be boring and skip it. **Do not skip it.** This file is the foundation for everything that follows.

Here is the question: **where on Earth are we?**

It sounds like a silly question. But try thinking about it for one second — do you actually know how big the world is? The world is a sphere, and we are sitting on one small piece of it. Stand still for a moment and think about how much space is around you... it is probably a lot more than you have imagined.

That is what this file is about.

## The first big surprise — the world is mostly water

Most people carry a picture of the world in their head that is wrong at its most basic level. We tend to imagine continents as big solid chunks, with the ocean as the flat background they sit on — roughly how a school map looks, with land drawn bright and sea drawn pale.

The reality is almost the reverse. **About 71 percent of Earth's surface is water. Only about 29 percent is land.** The land itself is so heavily concentrated in the northern half of the planet that in large stretches of the south, there is nothing but ocean.

This matters for a practical reason before it matters for an interesting one. When you look at the Indian Ocean region, India looks like a wall between two very different waters — the Arabian Sea on one side and the Bay of Bengal on the other. That is not decoration. The peninsula is the reason sea trade, naval power, energy shipping and commercial pressure all concentrate where they do in the Indian Ocean. The shape of the land is the reason the shape of the water matters.

## What a "continent" actually is

The word comes up constantly, so let us define it properly.

When someone says "these two places are on different continents," they mean: **the land of the world has been divided into large chunks, and each chunk has been given a name.**

Picture a very large sheet of paper with the whole world drawn on it. Now tear that paper into seven pieces. Each piece is separate from the others. Now name the pieces: Asia, Africa, Europe, and so on. **That is all a continent is.**

So a continent is simply a large chunk of the earth's land.

And one important point: **these chunks are not a fact, they are a convention.** The world's land is one continuous thing. We have chosen, for convenience, to divide it into seven. No science says there must be exactly seven. Someone else could divide the world into six pieces and they would not be wrong — they would have simply chosen a different convention.

## The seven continents

Here they are:

**Asia** — the largest chunk. It contains India, China, Pakistan, Japan, Russia, the Koreas, and part of the Middle East. India sits inside Asia and shares a landmass with China, Pakistan, Nepal, Bhutan, Myanmar, Bangladesh and Afghanistan. That fact alone has enormous consequences, which Phase 1 will unpack properly.

**Africa** — the second largest, and the only continent that sits in every single hemisphere. It touches the north, the south, the east and the west.

**North America** — Canada, the United States, Mexico and Central America.

**South America** — Brazil, Argentina and the rest. Brazil on its own is a genuinely major military and economic power.

**Antarctica** — the odd one out. **It is the only continent with nobody living on it.** No towns, no cities. No country claims ownership of it, because a treaty specifically prevents that. It is a genuinely useful precedent to know, because it is one of the rare cases where the international system successfully blocked a land grab instead of merely complaining about one afterwards.

**Europe** — the second smallest continent by area, and the most crowded. A large number of countries are packed into a small space, and most of them have fought each other repeatedly. Small crowded areas produce dense conflicts, and dense conflicts produce exactly the kind of contested lines you will meet all over this course.

**Australia** — Australia is a whole continent that is one country. It is also often called Oceania when the smaller Pacific island nations are included.

Now something important that this list hides, and it is worth pulling out separately because the whole course will lean on it:

**When people say "size," there are two completely different sizes — area (how much land) and population (how many people). And the two rankings look nothing alike.**

Europe is the second smallest continent by area and holds a very large share of the world's wealth. Africa is enormous in area and has about a fifth of the world's people. Asia contains both the most populous countries on Earth and, increasingly, the largest national economies, while also containing hundreds of millions of people who are still very poor.

When you hear arguments about international institutions, voting shares, reforming the UN Security Council, or who gets to write the rules — this table is what is actually being argued about underneath the language.

| Continent | Approx. area (million km²) | What an Indian reader should note |
|---|---:|---|
| Asia | 44.6 | Contains India, China, Pakistan, Japan, the Koreas, and the eastern edge of the Middle East |
| Africa | 30.4 | Spans every hemisphere; the Indian Ocean is one of its four coastlines |
| North America | 24.7 | Includes Canada, the USA, Mexico and Central America |
| South America | 17.8 | Home of Brazil, a first-rank military and economic power |
| Antarctica | 14.2 | No permanent population, and territorial claims frozen by treaty |
| Europe | 10.2 | Small, wealthy, and unusually full of borders drawn by war |
| Australia | 8.6 | A continent-sized country; a swing player in the Indo-Pacific |

Two things stand out in that table, and both are worth remembering.

The first is Antarctica — the only continent with no people on it, governed by a treaty that explicitly freezes territorial claims so nobody can take it. It is one of the rare cases where the international system prevented something rather than arguing about it afterwards.

The second is Europe: the smallest continent bar the southern two, but packed with countries that have repeatedly gone to war with each other. Small crowded areas produce dense conflicts, and dense conflicts produce contested lines.

## The five oceans

If continents divide the land, oceans divide the water — and this division is much newer than most people assume.

Traditionally there were four oceans. In 2000 the International Hydrographic Organization formally recognised the Southern Ocean as a distinct fifth, defining its northern edge as the 60th parallel south. That sounds like a bureaucratic curiosity, and it is not, because it legally cut Antarctica off from the other three oceans.

The Pacific is the largest at roughly 165 million square kilometres — about a third of the entire ocean surface. The Atlantic is second at about 106 million. The Indian Ocean comes third at about 70 million. The Southern Ocean is about 20 million, and the Arctic is the smallest at about 14 million.

That the Indian Ocean is only the third largest is worth pausing on, because it is the ocean that matters most to India and one of the least discussed in global media, which concentrates overwhelmingly on Europe, the Middle East and the US-China relationship.

## Why the oceans matter more than the continents

It is natural to assume land is what counts, because land is where people live and where borders get drawn. But for the things that actually generate international tension — energy, manufacturing supply chains, and military force — the water usually matters more than the ground.

Oil does not care which flag it was extracted under. It cares which strait it has to pass through to reach a buyer. A factory assembling electronics from a dozen different countries cares about shipping time and shipping cost, not about sovereignty. Submarines and aircraft carriers are far cheaper to hide on open water than on land.

This is why so much of modern geopolitics is really maritime geopolitics, and why terms like "Indo-Pacific" and "String of Pearls" exist at all. **The most valuable real estate on Earth is not the most fertile land. It is the handful of narrow water passages through which most of the world's oil and trade must pass.** We meet those properly in Phase 1.

## What this file is not

The seven-continent split is not a law of nature. It is a teaching convention that varies from country to country, and you will find six-continent models where Eurasia is counted as a single landmass, and models that leave Antarctica out entirely. Nothing in physics requires seven. If someone uses a different number, they have not made a mistake — they have picked a different convention, and the argument is usually about which convention makes a particular region easier to see.

Nor is the ocean empty or neutral. When we say a stretch of water belongs to nobody, we mean no state has sovereignty over it — not that no state has power there. Large parts of the ocean are divided into exclusive economic zones of two hundred nautical miles, and beyond those the remaining high seas are legally shared but practically contested by whoever can project force. The sea has no governments, and it is a long way from being ungoverned.

And "continent" does not mean "country." Asia is not a country, India is not a continent, and the fact that India shares a landmass with China tells you nothing on its own about whether the two are allies or adversaries. That confusion — assuming that physical proximity or shared terrain implies political alignment — is one of the most common errors in early attempts at this subject, and it is worth naming now so you can catch yourself doing it later.

## Bridge to the next file

We now have a world with continents, oceans, and India sitting in South Asia. But notice what the map still cannot tell you.

It does not say which of those shapes are countries, which are parts of countries, which are claims rather than facts, or what makes one border legitimate and another disputed. A continent is a geographical convenience. It has no government, no population count and no foreign policy, which means it cannot be a unit of geopolitics at all.

That is the gap the next file closes, and it closes it more rigorously than you might expect. `0.2 What Is a Country - Sovereignty, Population, Government and Recognition.md` looks at the actual legal test for whether something on a map counts as a country, why a strip of land with two million people can be a state while a mountain with nobody on it is not, and why "country" and "nation" are not interchangeable words.
