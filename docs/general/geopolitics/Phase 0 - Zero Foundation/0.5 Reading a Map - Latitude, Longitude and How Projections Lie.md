# 0.5 Reading a Map - Latitude, Longitude and How Projections Lie

## The problem nobody can escape

The Earth is a sphere. Maps are flat. Something has to give, and there is no arrangement of the world onto a rectangle that avoids the problem entirely.

Any flat map has to distort either the shapes of places, their relative areas, or the distances between them — and which one it distorts is the entire definition of a **projection**.

It is worth sitting with how strange that is. The map of India you used at school, the world map on the news, the weather map on your phone — all of them are false in a specific, deliberate, chosen way. They are not accidentally wrong. Somebody chose which distortion to accept, usually for a reason that had more to do with history and convenience than with accuracy.

This is not pedantry. In this course we will be reading maps that emphasise certain countries, that inflate the apparent size of polar nations, and that draw disputed borders one particular way. Being able to see the distortion is the difference between reading a map and being read by one.

## Latitude and longitude: the grid underneath everything

Before the distortions, the basic coordinate system, because it is genuinely simple and it has one delightful payoff for an Indian reader.

**Latitude** is measured north or south of the equator, which is defined as zero degrees. It runs from zero to ninety in each direction, and the poles sit at ninety.

**Longitude** is measured east or west of the prime meridian, which is the line running through Greenwich in the United Kingdom. It runs from zero to one hundred and eighty in each direction, and the one-hundred-and-eightieth line is where the International Date Line roughly sits.

India's own coordinates are worth memorising, because they will keep reappearing. India extends from about **8°4′ north to 37°6′ north**, and from about **68°7′ east to 97°25′ east**. That is a compact answer to a surprisingly common question, because India is one of the smaller countries by longitude width even though it feels large — most of a world map's width is longitude that does not belong to us.

Now for the part that actually explains something you already know.

One degree of latitude is always about 111 kilometres, everywhere on Earth, because the distance per degree of latitude is constant along a meridian. But one degree of **longitude** is not constant at all: it is about 111 kilometres at the equator and shrinks to nothing at the poles, because all the meridians converge there. The formula is roughly 111 × cos(latitude).

That single fact is the mechanical cause of nearly every area distortion in this file.

It also has a lovely consequence in the opposite direction. The Earth rotates fifteen degrees every hour, which is why time zones are fifteen degrees wide. India sits at roughly **82.5° east**, exactly halfway between 75 and 90, and therefore at exactly half an hour past a whole number of hours. That is the entire reason India uses **IST = UTC+5:30** while almost every other country uses a whole hour. Our odd half-hour time zone is not an administrative quirk — it is 82.5° east.

## The three things a map must sacrifice

When cartographers project a sphere onto a flat surface, three properties compete, and no projection can preserve all three at once.

The first is **shape** — preserving angles so a place looks like what it actually looks like. Maps that do this are called conformal, and this is what sailors cared about, because on a conformal map a straight line drawn on the chart is a straight line you can actually sail.

The second is **area** — making regions look correctly proportional to their real size. Maps that do this are equal-area projections.

The third is **distance** — making the distance between two points on the map equal to the real distance between them, which only some pairs of points can ever have.

You get two out of three at best, and every projection in history is a different answer to which one to sacrifice. The two that matter most for you are these.

**Mercator**, from 1569, preserves shape and is genuinely excellent for navigation — this is not a bad map, it is a good map for a specific purpose. Its problem is that it preserves shape by inflating area, and it inflates far more severely the further you go from the equator. The scale factor is one divided by the cosine of your latitude, which at sixty degrees north is already about double, and it climbs steeply from there.

The result is the single most distorted thing about the standard world map. **Greenland looks roughly the same size as Africa. Africa is about fourteen times larger than Greenland in reality.** The northern countries are visually inflated and the equatorial ones visually shrunk, and the countries that happen to be large, cold and northern are the ones being flattered.

**Gall-Peters**, by contrast, sacrifices shape entirely to preserve area. Continents look stretched and strange, Africa and South America balloon outward, and high-latitude regions look pinched. But it is the one projection that tells the truth about relative size, and that is exactly why it has been politically contested. In 1992, as part of the Dayton settlement ending the Bosnian war, the agreement required that the Serbian label be removed from an ethnographic map in the Bosnian school textbook — a small, strange footnote that captures how directly map choices get used in political disputes. The Gall-Peters projection has been adopted by a number of educational systems specifically because it presents the countries of the Global South at a size closer to their real weight, and that has been read by some governments as a critique.

The projection used on this course's other maps is Natural Earth, which is a third option: it preserves shapes well enough to be recognisable and areas well enough to be honest, accepting moderate distortion of both. It is the cartographic equivalent of a sensible compromise, and it is worth knowing that a map like this is a choice too.

## What this has to do with geopolitics

The reason to care about any of this is that maps carry arguments, and the arguments are usually invisible.

Area distortion translates directly into perceived power. If a country looks enormous on the map you grew up with, it registers as powerful before you have thought about it at all, and if it looks small and squeezed, it registers as marginal. Projection choice is one of the quietest ways a perspective gets built into a picture, and it operates on intuition rather than argument, which makes it hard to argue back against.

Where you centre the map matters for the same reason. A Mercator map centred on the Atlantic puts Europe and North America in the middle and the Pacific at the edges. Centred on the Pacific, the Americas sit at the edge and East Asia occupies the centre. Nothing about the world has changed, but the picture now reads as though the centre of the world is where China, Japan and Korea are. Neither framing is false, and most of the history of map projection is a quiet argument about which one to use.

Then there is the fact that a Mercator projection cannot represent the poles at all. The poles are infinitely far from the equator on a Mercator map, which is why Antarctica has to be drawn enormous or cut off entirely, and why this course's Mercator map simply drops it. A projection that cannot draw part of the planet honestly is not a neutral formatting choice.

And behind all of it sits the point established earlier in Phase 0: whoever's data was used, the map draws *their* version of every disputed border. Projection distortion and political framing stack on top of each other, and a picture can be misleading in several directions at once.

## A practical checklist for reading any map

Once the above is internalised, you have a habit that most viewers of maps do not have. Before trusting any map — especially one being used to make an argument — run four quick questions.

Ask **what the projection is**, and if nobody says, assume Mercator, because that is what most of the internet uses. If it is Mercator, never compare the sizes of two countries, and be especially suspicious of any comparison involving a northern country.

Ask **what is in the centre**, since what is centred reads as central and what is at the edge reads as marginal.

Ask **whose borders are drawn**, remembering that a disputed border on a map is one country's claim drawn as if it were a fact.

And ask **what the map is trying to show you**, since every published map was made by somebody with a reason for choosing that particular framing.

None of this makes you cynical about maps. Maps are useful, and this course depends on them. It makes you the kind of reader for whom a map is a source of claims to be evaluated, rather than a picture to be absorbed.

## What this file is not

Map projections do not encode a conspiracy. Nobody sat in a room deciding to make Russia look big. Most distortions are old accidents of mathematics and navigation that stuck around because they were convenient, and the Mercator projection has been used by navigators for four and a half centuries precisely because it works well for the job it was built for.

Nor is Gall-Peters the "correct" map. It distorts shape far more severely, and for most practical purposes it is less useful than Natural Earth. It is included here not as a winner but as proof that the choice exists and has consequences, which is the actual point.

And none of this means the geography is fictional. Russia really is the largest country, Africa really is fourteen times Greenland, and India really is between 8 and 37 degrees north. The coordinates are exact. What varies is how faithfully a flat picture can represent a curved world, and how much of that variation is a decision rather than a limitation.
