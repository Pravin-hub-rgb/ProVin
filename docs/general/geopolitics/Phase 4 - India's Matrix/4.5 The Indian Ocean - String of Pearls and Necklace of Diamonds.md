Every file in this phase has circled one question. This is it.

**India and China are both Asian powers. The Indian Ocean is where they actually meet — and it is the ocean India's entire economic future depends on.**

# 4.5 The Indian Ocean - String of Pearls and Necklace of Diamonds

## Why this ocean, and why now

**Let me start with the scale, because people underestimate it.**

**The Indian Ocean is the third largest ocean, and it is the only ocean on Earth named after a country.** For most of history it was not a strategic arena at all. It mattered commercially, but nobody competed for it.

**That changed in about twenty years, and here is why.**

**Three things happened at once.**

**First, the oil trade moved into it.** The Persian Gulf — the largest oil reserve on Earth — empties into the Indian Ocean. **And the oil has to go somewhere: east, to Asia.** For most of the twentieth century the oil went west, to Europe and America. **Now it goes east.** Asia imports enormous quantities of Gulf oil, and all of it crosses the Indian Ocean.

**Second, China became the world's factory.** Phase 2 covered this. A factory needs raw materials in and finished goods out, all by ship. **And China's route to the Indian Ocean runs past India's doorstep.**

**Third, sea lanes are actually important.** Phase 0 said that a flat map distorts area, but it also said the real strategic geography is the one you can ship through. **The Indian Ocean is where more than half the world's shipping goes.**

**So what happened is that an ocean nobody fought over became the central strategic question in Asia.**

**And here is the point that makes it uniquely Indian.**

**In the Atlantic and the Pacific, India is one of several powers. In the Indian Ocean, India is not one of several — it is the largest country, the only significant navy in the region, and the only littoral state that could project power anywhere.**

**India does not need anyone's permission in the Indian Ocean. It needs to keep it.**

## The String of Pearls — what China is building

**The phrase "String of Pearls" was coined by an American analyst, Robert Dali, in 2004.** It described Chinese plans to build a chain of ports across the Indian Ocean — a string of pearls scattered from the Arabian Sea to the South China Sea.

**Those ports are largely now real. Here are the main ones.**

**Gwadar, Pakistan.** On the Arabian Sea coast, in Pakistan-administered Kashmir. **China built and operates it.** Its significance is hard to overstate: Gwadar is a deep-water port, and Phase 1 established that **you cannot run an oil economy without somewhere to put the oil.** Pakistan has oil and coal resources but almost no usable port capacity. **Gwadar solves that.**

**And there is a second significance.** Gwadar is about 100 km from the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz. **China does not need to use Gwadar to monitor Hormuz.** It already has a base in Djibouti, at the western end of the Gulf of Aden, and satellites. But a port on the Arabian Sea is a permanent position adjacent to one of the world's most important chokepoints. **That is what matters.**

**Hambantota, Sri Lanka.** On the southern tip of Sri Lanka, in deep water, in the open Indian Ocean. **China built and operates it.** And then, in 2017, **China took a long lease on it — ninety-nine years — in exchange for bailing out Sri Lanka's debt crisis.**

**This one caused genuine shock in India.** A Chinese facility, on a strategically perfect location, on a ninety-nine-year lease, in a country India has historically considered within its sphere.

**Kyaukpyu, Myanmar.** On the Bay of Bengal, deep water, on the western coast of Myanmar. **China is building this.** Its location is significant: **it is on the route from the Indian Ocean to the South China Sea** — directly on the shipping lane between the Indian Ocean and Malacca.

**Chittagong, Bangladesh.** China operates a container terminal in Bangladesh's main port. **This one matters less strategically but is still part of the pattern.**

**Djibouti.** This one is not a port at all. **In 2017 China opened its first permanent overseas military base in Djibouti**, on the Gulf of Aden, at the southern entrance to the Red Sea. **And it sits directly across from India's own base there — Operation Alnisar at Chaken.**

**Two permanent foreign military bases, a few kilometres apart, at one of the world's most important maritime chokepoints.**

**That is the most serious single development in this file.** And it is not much discussed in India.

**And note what the String of Pearls is not. It is not a military alliance network. China is not garrisoning these ports.** They are commercial infrastructure. **And a commercial port is a far harder thing to justify publicly than a military base — which is exactly why they are built as ports.**

## The Necklace of Diamonds — India's answer

**India's response has an equally famous name: the "Necklace of Diamonds."**

**The phrase was coined by Air Commodore Manpreet Singh in 2012 — which is notable, because it shows Indian strategists were thinking about this before it became official discussion.**

**The logic is clean. China's string is a chain of ports around India's neighbourhood. India's answer is a chain of access agreements on the far side — through the Indian Ocean, to Africa and beyond. So that if China's network is ever used for anything other than commerce, India's response routes do not pass through the Indian neighbourhood.**

**Here is what India has actually secured:**

**Oman.** A 2016 agreement giving India access to the port of **Duqm**, on Oman's southern coast, with logistics and berthing rights. **This is the single most important one.** **Duqm sits outside the Strait of Hormuz, in the Gulf of Oman.** Phase 1 established why that location matters: **a facility outside Hormuz is not subject to the same risk as one inside it.** So in a Hormuz crisis — the scenario India worries about most — Duqm remains accessible.

**Mauritius.** Agreements signed in 2016 to develop **Agalega** and **Mauritius** as facilities. **Agalega is strategically located** — it is directly north of the main Mauritius island, and so is in the path of shipping from the Suez Canal to East Africa. **But the practical outcome has been slow.** The Agalega agreement has not delivered the operational facility that was envisaged.

**Chagos Archipelago — Diego Garcia.** This is the important one that surprises people. **Diego Garcia is an atoll in the middle of the Indian Ocean, and it hosts one of the largest US military bases in the world.** India granted the US a long lease on it in the 1970s. **Its sovereignty is disputed — the Chagossians were expelled from their own islands — and the International Court of Justice ruled against the UK in 2019, ordering the UK to end its administration.**

**But India's position has been consistent for decades: that it does not support the return of the Chagossians as it stands.** It has argued that a legal dispute should not be allowed to create a strategic vacuum, and that the US presence at Diego Garcia is the price of keeping the Indian Ocean open to international shipping.

**That position is genuinely unpopular in Indian domestic politics — and there are real criticisms of it. But it is strategically coherent.**

**And there is a growing tension here that India has not resolved.** It has argued that the Western countries' position on Diego Garcia should apply to every island with an indigenous population — while itself signing long-term agreements for access to islands with indigenous populations. **That is a real inconsistency, and it is one that India's own civil society has pointed out.**

**Seychelles.** A 2022 agreement for a maritime facility. **Madagascar and Mozambique** have also been discussed or agreed.

**And then the most recent and most interesting one: the Agalega model applied again.**

**In 2024, India signed an agreement with Oman — the same country as Duqm, the same country from file 4.3 — granting access to three facilities: Duqm, the Port of Salalah, and an airfield at Masirah.**

**That is a third base in Oman, alongside Duqm and Salalah.** And it is a reminder that Phase 4.3's point about Bhutan applies here too: **India's relations with small strategic partners involve both genuine partnership and real asymmetry.**

**So: is the Necklace of Diamonds a serious strategy, or is it a list of announcements?**

**Honest answer: partly both.** The Duqm agreement is genuinely significant and operationally real. **But several of the other agreements have produced very little.** The Mauritius one is a good example — announced with great confidence, and delivered very slowly.

**And the reason is not weakness. It is that "access agreements" are much harder than port construction.** China builds a port. **India writes a treaty and hopes the treaty produces a facility.** Those are different timescales and different difficulties.

## The naval reality — what India actually has

**Because the ocean strategy is ultimately a naval question, let me be honest about India's naval position.**

**The Indian Navy is, by any serious assessment, the strongest naval force in the Indian Ocean.** No other regional navy comes close. It operates two aircraft carrier groups, a large and growing submarine fleet, and a blue-water capability that is genuine rather than aspirational.

**India is one of only a handful of countries globally with real blue-water navy capability, and in its own ocean it is dominant.**

**But there are real weaknesses.**

**Submarines.** India's submarine fleet is a mix, and its nuclear-powered submarine programme — the INS Arihant class — is behind where India would like it. **Submarines are the most important naval weapon in the Indian Ocean, because they are the only thing that can threaten a surface fleet without being seen.**

**Surface fleet.** Large, but with real gaps in anti-submarine warfare and in long-range maritime strike.

**The two-carrier problem.** India's two carriers are genuinely useful, but they were designed in the 1980s for a different world, and the cost of keeping them operational is very high.

**And the biggest one: basing.** India's navy needs to operate far from its home ports, in a very large ocean, and **India's friends in the Indian Ocean are small states with limited infrastructure.** Where does an Indian carrier refuel? **That is exactly what the Necklace of Diamonds is for.**

## What this file is not

**The "String of Pearls" is not a Chinese military base network.** It is overwhelmingly commercial infrastructure. Treating it as purely military is the single most common analytical error, and it produces the wrong conclusion — because it makes China look more aggressive than the evidence supports.

**Nor is China building these ports to attack India.** Phase 4.3 covered this: China's port-building is global, not India-specific. China needs the network because its western provinces are landlocked. **The problem is not the intention — it is the effect.**

**And India's response is not purely defensive either.** Signing access agreements with Oman, Mauritius, Seychelles and Madagascar is presented as a way to keep the Indian Ocean free of Chinese influence, but it is also **a way to extend Indian reach into the western Indian Ocean**, which has its own objectives. "Strategy" and "expansion" are sometimes the same thing.

**Nor is China's position as strong as it appears.** Every one of those port agreements faces local politics. Bangladesh has had elections. Sri Lanka has oscillated. Myanmar has a coup. Pakistan's finances are weak. **These ports are not politically secure, and several of them have faced public controversy in their host countries.**

**And the biggest thing this file is not: nothing here means war is likely.** Every one of the facilities described is commercial or defensive. **The realistic scenario is not conflict — it is dependency, and the slow loss of Indian influence in a region where India spent decades establishing it.**

## Bridge to the next file

You now have India's position in the world: its two hard relationships, its six neighbours, its relationships with the great powers, and its ocean.

**And there is one thing left, and it is the question that makes all of this strange.**

**How much of any of this is actually foreign policy?**

**Because foreign policy is supposed to be India's relationship with other countries. And a very large part of what determines India's external behaviour is decided inside India** — by elections, by coalition mathematics, by what an Indian government can survive politically.

**The Kashmir dispute survives because no Indian leader has the domestic space to compromise. The neighbourhood policy is shaped by governments in Dhaka and Thimphu and Malé, not Delhi. The Russia relationship exists because of domestic political calculations about energy prices. The Quad participation has domestic critics.**

**So the final file of this phase is the most uncomfortable one, because it turns the analysis on India itself.** And it is the one most likely to change how you think about the subject.

That is `4.6 India's Internal Politics and Its Foreign Policy`.

## A final word on this phase

**You have now seen India's position from every angle: China, Pakistan, the neighbourhood, the great powers, and the ocean.**

**And the honest summary is this.**

**India is genuinely rising, and its assets are real: a huge young population, a growing economy, a dominant navy in its own ocean, nuclear weapons, and democracy.**

**Its weaknesses are also real: heavy oil dependence, no security guarantee, a neighbourhood it cannot fully control, and a domestic politics that frequently produces incoherent foreign policy.**

**Which means India will neither rise as fast as its admirers expect, nor fall as far as its critics predict.** It will do something messier — advancing on some fronts, conceding on others, and doing so with the permanent anxiety of a country that is powerful enough to matter but not powerful enough to be safe.

**That anxiety is what non-alignment was designed to manage. And Phase 5 — the last substantive phase — is about how to think about all of this yourself.**
