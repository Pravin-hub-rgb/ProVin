# 4.6 India's Internal Politics and Its Foreign Policy

## The uncomfortable argument

**Let me put the claim plainly first, because you should know what you are about to read and be able to disagree with it.**

**Foreign policy is supposed to be about other countries. But a large share of India's external behaviour is decided by Indian domestic politics** — by what an Indian government needs to survive, what a coalition partner demands, and what a voter will punish a government for.

**This is not unique to India.** Every democracy is like this. **But India is an unusually strong case**, and there are specific reasons why that matters.

**And this file is not a criticism of Indian politicians.** It is a description of a structural situation. **Understanding it does not mean blaming anyone.**

## Reason 1: Kashmir survives because of domestic politics

**Start with the most important example, because file 4.2 ended on exactly this point.**

**India and Pakistan have not settled Kashmir in seventy years. Both countries have nuclear weapons. And no serious analyst in either country expects a settlement in the foreseeable future.**

**Here is the structural reason, and it is genuinely important.**

**No Indian leader has ever had the domestic political space to give up any part of what India administers in Kashmir.**

**Consider what that means concretely.** Kashmir is the only place in India where an organised separatist movement has had sustained armed support from outside, and where the political status is genuinely contested. **Any Indian government that formally conceded anything on Kashmir — even a procedural thing, even a review clause — would face immediate and severe domestic backlash.**

**And the same logic runs in reverse.** Any Pakistani government that formally abandoned the Kashmir claim would be accused of selling out, because that claim is foundational to Pakistani national identity.

**So we have a situation where the domestic politics of two countries make agreement impossible, regardless of what either country's leaders actually want.**

**Which means the Kashmir dispute is not a negotiation that has failed. It is a negotiation that domestic politics prevents from starting.**

## Reason 2: Coalition mathematics has actually shaped foreign policy

**This is the least known of the reasons and possibly the most concrete.**

**India has frequently had coalition governments, and coalition partners have had leverage.** The most famous case: from 2011 to 2014, the United Progressive Alliance government under Manmohan Singh was, by the admission of many insiders, effectively run not by the Prime Minister but by the parties keeping it alive.

**And that had foreign policy consequences.** In 2011, following the American request, **India abstained from condemning Bahrain's crackdown during the Arab Spring — in significant part because of domestic coalition management.**

**Then, in 2014, the government collapsed over allegations of corruption and kickbacks in a telecom spectrum allocation — and a specific government decision that angered coalition partners was widely cited as a contributing factor.**

**This is the honest point: a domestic political crisis, not a strategic decision, ended India's government.**

**And you can trace real foreign policy through such moments.** Non-alignment itself was shaped by domestic politics. India's nuclear testing in 1974 was partly driven by domestic political competition. **Nehru's non-alignment required sustained political capital that later governments could not always find.**

**So India's non-alignment was not a doctrine imposed from outside. It emerged from domestic coalition arithmetic — which is why it held so long, and why weakening it has been so contested.**

## Reason 3: The governments are not Atlanticist, but the establishment is

**This one is genuinely uncomfortable and it is well documented within India.**

**India's strategic community — retired officials, diplomats, academics, military officers — is substantially Atlanticist.** They believe India's future is with the West, that China is the principal strategic threat, and that India should align more closely with the United States.

**And successive Indian governments, including the current one, have been considerably more cautious about this than their own establishment.**

**The reason is domestic.** **India has a large body of public opinion that is not enthusiastic about aligning with any bloc.** Partly because of a tradition of non-alignment that is genuinely part of how Indians understand their country. Partly because of a suspicion — often unarticulated but real — that formal alignment means being a second-order partner rather than an equal.

**So the policy India pursues is a domestic compromise.** Practical cooperation with the West, careful maintenance of relations with Russia and China, and refusal to commit to any bloc.

**And the internal critique of that compromise is serious.** The argument is that it produces the worst of both worlds: technology and investment from the West, discounted Russian oil, and neither as a real partner.

**Whether that critique is valid depends on what you think India's strategy should be. And that is a legitimate debate.**

## Reason 4: Borders that exist because of administrative decisions

**Something in this course deserves repeating, because it is genuinely striking.**

**India's most contested internal geography traces to decisions taken for administrative reasons, not strategic ones.**

**The Chicken's Neck exists because the British partitioned Bengal in 1905 for administrative convenience — a partition they reversed in 1911. The line they drew still determines where India's most vulnerable internal chokepoint is.**

**And the border disputes exist because boundaries between states were drawn during the states' reorganisation — lines that were not surveyed carefully and that are still being litigated decades later.**

**The political problems in the North-East are partly a consequence of how the states of India were defined.** Many of the boundaries were drawn on the basis of ethnic and linguistic groups that then found themselves sharing a state with another group they did not choose to live with.

**The point is this: India's domestic political problems and India's external vulnerabilities are not separate things.** **They come from the same history.**

## Reason 5: Economy as foreign policy, and the manufacturing gap

**Here is one that is discussed more openly and matters enormously.**

**India is the world's fifth largest economy and the largest provider of IT services.** **But India's manufacturing base is far smaller than its economic size suggests.**

**The reason is the colonising British preference for an India that supplied raw materials and imported finished goods.** That economic pattern survived independence, and later the licence raj protected it.

**And that has a very direct foreign policy consequence.** If India wants Western investment and technology, it needs a good business environment. **And that means open markets and economic liberalisation.**

**But the political incentives in India point the other way.** The 2014 slogan "Make in India" was partly about domestic manufacturing and partly about economic nationalism — about making India produce domestically rather than import.

**And there is a real tension here.** "Made in India" and openness pull in different directions.

**The evidence is not one-sided.** India's economic growth has been robust. But manufacturing's share of employment remains modest relative to its share of GDP, **and India remains heavily import-dependent for critical inputs, including some from China.**

**So India's economic policy is genuinely constrained: it wants Western technology and investment, and Chinese inputs, while political incentives push towards domestic production.**

**That is the trade-off, and every Indian government faces it.**

## Reason 6: Public opinion as a real constraint

**One more, and the most recent.**

**India's foreign policy is genuinely shaped by public opinion in ways that are often dismissed as noise.**

**Take the Ukraine war.** India's abstention was presented by some as pure strategic calculation. **But it also reflected public opinion — the war has been genuinely unpopular in India, and the government's position aligned with what Indians actually think.** Phase 3's framework would call this an interest, and that is fair. **But it was also a genuine domestic constraint on the government's room to move.**

**And the reverse also happens.** Indian governments have taken positions abroad that they knew would be unpopular at home.

**So public opinion is a real constraint, not a fig leaf.**

## What this file is not

**This is not a criticism of Indian politicians.** Every government operates within constraints. **The point is structural: the constraints are large, and understanding them changes how you evaluate any Indian foreign policy decision.**

**Nor is it an argument that domestic politics matters more than international reality.** International constraints are real — you cannot negotiate Kashmir in any abstract sense; the international legal position, the geography and the nuclear dynamics all bind.

**And "coalition politics shaped foreign policy" does not mean any particular decision was wrong.** Some decisions have good reasons that happen to be domestic.

**Nor is this unique to India.** Every democratic foreign policy is shaped at home. **India is a stronger case because of the combination: coalition arithmetic, a large ideological tradition of non-alignment, and a strategic community that disagrees with successive governments.**
