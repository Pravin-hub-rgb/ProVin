India's neighbours all look inward. This file is about the relationships that do not.

**India is being courted by the United States, and India is also close to Russia. And those two relationships pull in different directions.**

The question is whether India can be Western enough to get the technology and investment it needs, without being aligned enough to lose the independence it values.

# 4.4 The United States, Russia and the West

## Start with why this question exists at all

**India and the United States were enemies.**

**Not allies, not rivals — enemies, for twenty years.** India was close to the Soviet Union throughout the Cold War. The United States had backed Pakistan throughout that period. **And when India tested nuclear weapons in May 1974, the United States imposed sanctions on India.**

**Those sanctions were severe — worse than they were on Pakistan, which had tested at almost the same time. The United States refused to acknowledge India's nuclear test, and it cut off food aid at a time when India faced famine.**

**So for most of India's history, American influence on India was associated with the threat of starvation.**

**And then, in 2001, everything changed.** The September 11 attacks. **And India became, almost overnight, the United States' most important partner in the region.**

**This is the single most dramatic reversal in Indian foreign policy, and it is worth asking why it happened.**

**The answer is straightforward, and it is the interest framework from Phase 3 working perfectly.**

**After 2001, the United States needed help in Afghanistan, and needed someone who could restrain Pakistan. India was the only country in the region with both the standing and the relationships to do both.**

**So America needed India. And that — more than any shared ideology — is why the relationship improved.**

## What India actually gets from the United States

**Let me be specific, because "close ties with the West" is too vague to be useful.**

**First: technology.** This is the most important one and it is genuinely crucial. **India's IT sector, its pharmaceuticals, its engineering — all of it depends on imported technology**, much of it American or American-derived. And in defence, India's most advanced equipment — the F-16s it ordered, the missile technology — comes from the US or derived from it.

**Second: trade and investment.** Indian companies operate extensively in the United States — the IT services business, the outsourcing business — and that access is worth enormous amounts of money to India's economy.

**Third: the defence relationship.** After the 2001 shift, India has signed a series of agreements: a ten-year defence framework agreement, two-plus-two dialogues, and importantly the 2008 civil nuclear agreement that opened India's civilian nuclear sector to foreign technology.

**Fourth: diaspora links.** Millions of Indians live in the United States, and that is a channel of goodwill and soft power.

**Now — the parts that are less comfortable.**

**India has significant trade deficits with the United States.** The United States has complained about this in recent years, and India's high tariffs on American goods are a recurring source of friction. **This is the "tariff politics" from file 3.4 in action.**

**India has also faced American pressure on a range of issues** — from human rights, to the Russia relationship, to Iran. **And in the case of Iran, America successfully pressured India into cutting oil purchases**, which file 3.4 covered.

**The pattern: India gets technology and access, and in exchange gives up some freedom on issues where it disagrees with Washington.**

## The Quad — and why India is in it

**This is the most significant development in Indian foreign policy in decades, and it needs to be understood carefully because it is routinely described wrongly.**

**What the Quad actually is:** a grouping of four countries — the United States, Japan, India and Australia — that has existed since 2007, and has been described as a "quadrilateral security dialogue."

**What it is not: a military alliance. There is no mutual defence treaty. No member is obliged to defend another.**

**Why India joined, and what that means, is the interesting part.**

**India has never said the Quad is anti-China.** But it has also never denied it. **And the context is hard to miss: it was revived in 2017 — the year of the Doklam standoff — and it has since become the main forum through which India engages with the United States, Japan and Australia.**

**So what does India get from it?**

**Practical things.** The Quad is where India discusses maritime security, supply chains, technology, and infrastructure — and several of India's largest infrastructure projects, from railways to ports, have been announced in Quad frameworks.

**Strategic things: deterrence.** Being in the Quad means China has to think about India in a group context rather than bilaterally. **And it means India gets closer to the two countries in the Pacific that matter most to it — Japan and Australia.**

**But there is a real cost, and India knows it.**

**China has responded to India's Quad participation.** Chinese trade pressure on India increased in the years after India joined, and at one point Chinese exports to India fell by a significant percentage. **India has not joined an alliance and is not going to — but it has accepted real economic cost for the Quad.**

**And that cost is exactly what the previous file was about.** India cannot please the United States and maintain its relationship with its neighbours at the same time. **That is a structural contradiction, not a choice.**

## Russia — the relationship that got awkward

**India and Russia have a long, warm, and increasingly uncomfortable relationship.**

**Historically it was close.** Russia has been a major supplier of defence equipment to India for decades. Indian engineers trained in Russia. There is a genuine cultural affinity.

**But there are two tensions that have grown.**

**The first is Pakistan.** Russia has been close to Pakistan. In fact Russia is one of the few countries Pakistan has never had serious trouble with. **And Russia has sold arms to Pakistan** — and India has raised concerns about the technical transfer involved, particularly around the S-400 missile system. **India has been worried that the technology Russia sold to Pakistan might eventually reach China.**

**The second, and much bigger, is Ukraine.**

## The Ukraine problem — and why it matters so much

**In February 2022, Russia invaded Ukraine. The United Nations Security Council voted to condemn it — and India abstained.**

**Not voted against. Abstained. And this is worth being precise about, because India is not a Russian ally and does not act as one.**

**India's position is consistent: the invasion was wrong, but so was the NATO expansion that preceded it. India has argued for years — publicly — that the West provoked this by expanding into Russia's border.**

**Here is what India actually said at the Security Council, and it is worth hearing because it is genuinely different from Western framing.**

India's argument was that the world should ask why, in 2014, NATO expanded into Ukraine — that Russia's objection was serious, and the West's response was to expand further rather than negotiate. **And it asked whether the West was prepared to apply the same rules it applies to China — about sovereignty and borders — to its own alliance actions.**

**That is an uncomfortable argument to hear if you are European, and it is an entirely coherent one.**

**India abstained on the vote condemning the invasion, and it abstained on the vote requiring Russia to stop.**

**But India has also been clear that it does not support the invasion, and it has repeatedly called for dialogue.** Sceptics of India's position should weigh these two things together — the abstention, and the fact that a country that genuinely supported the invasion would not be publicly calling for negotiations.

**The costs have been real, and they are the reason India's foreign ministry has moved so carefully.**

**The United States has been openly critical of India's position.** Some American officials have gone further and threatened trade consequences. **That is a serious strain on a relationship India has spent twenty years building.**

**India's oil imports from Russia have risen dramatically — to the point where Russia is now among India's largest suppliers.** And the Western sanctions on Russian oil have made Indian purchases more expensive, since Indian refiners have had to buy at a discount, sometimes from intermediaries.

**So India is now, through circumstance rather than choice, buying discounted Russian oil that the West does not want sold.** **And that is a fact that complicates any simple account of India as a neutral party.**

**But it also creates a real vulnerability.** That oil, whatever the discount, is not free. **The shipping is done by tankers that are, in some cases, sanctioned.** India is now exposed to US sanctions on the very thing it most depends on — energy.

**And the deeper problem: a large part of India's critical manufacturing inputs come from the Gulf, not Russia.** So buying discounted Russian oil does not solve India's energy vulnerability — it diversifies slightly, and nothing more.

**The strategic picture is genuinely complicated, and anyone who tells you India is simply "pro-Russia" or "neutral" has not looked at it.**

## Japan — a relationship that got better

**This is the relationship that has genuinely surprised people, because it was so bad for decades.**

**India and Japan were close in the 1950s and 60s.** Japan was a significant source of investment and technology. **And then India nationalised Japanese assets in the 1980s and refused Japanese investment for over two decades.**

**Japan turned to Southeast Asia and China instead. And India and Japan were barely on speaking terms.**

**Since the early 2010s, this has completely reversed. And why is interesting.**

**India needed Japan's technology and capital. Japan needed a partner as China rose.** **And they share a fundamental interest that neither will state plainly but both act on: neither wants to be dependent on China.**

**The practical cooperation is now substantial.** Japan has become one of the largest sources of investment into India, particularly into infrastructure. There are two-plus-two dialogues. And in 2022, Modi and the Japanese Prime Minister signed a document pledging cooperation on critical supply chains and technology — a deliberate diversification away from China.

**And there's a genuine convergence on the Quad and on Indo-Pacific security.**

**India is now, in many ways, closer to Japan and Australia than it has been since the 1960s.** That is a genuine shift, and it happened fast.

## The awkward truth about Indian Western relations

**Here is the part that is rarely said, and it should be.**

**India is sometimes thought of as a Western ally. In most practical respects, it is not.**

**And in some respects, India's foreign policy elite is considerably more Western than the government that leads it.**

**This is a well-documented fact inside India.** The strategic community — the retired officials, diplomats, academics and military officers who advise on foreign policy — is strongly Atlanticist. **They believe India's future is with the West, that China is the principal threat, and that India should align more closely with the United States.**

**And successive Indian governments, including the current one, have been more cautious about this than their own establishment.**

**The reason is domestic.** India has a large body of public opinion that is not enthusiastic about aligning with any bloc. **And the government knows that an overtly Western foreign policy would cost it domestically — which is the subject of file 4.6.**

**So the policy India pursues is a compromise: deep practical cooperation with the West, with a deliberate maintenance of relations with Russia and China.**

**And the internal critique of that compromise — that it produces the worst of both worlds — is serious.** The argument is that you get technology and investment from the West, and you get Russia's oil at a discount, and you get neither as a real partner.

**Whether that is true depends on what you think India should actually be doing. And that is a legitimate debate.**

## What this file is not

**India is not a Western ally, and it should not be described as one.** There is no mutual defence treaty, and India has explicitly refused alliance. If America were threatened, India would not automatically fight beside it.

**Nor is India "pro-Russia," and abstaining on Ukraine does not make it a Russian ally.** India's position has consistently included calling for negotiations, and it has never endorsed the invasion.

**Nor is India's abstention on Ukraine a cynical calculation, though it is also a genuine strategic position.** The argument about NATO expansion is coherent and is made in the West too — by people who do not support Russia's actions.

**And India's Western relationships are not simply about China.** Technology, energy, trade and diaspora matter, and would matter even if China were not rising.

**But the biggest thing this file is not: India's position is not weak because it is ambiguous. It is ambiguous because the alternatives are worse.** Given a choice between alignment with the United States — which would bring enormous security benefits and enormous constraints — or maintaining genuine independence, most Indian strategic thinkers would choose independence, and would accept the costs that come with it.
