India-China is the most *complicated* relationship India has. It is not the most dangerous.

**The most dangerous is older, simpler, and has killed more people in your lifetime than any other.** It also has nuclear weapons on both sides, and a dispute that nobody seriously believes can be negotiated away.

This file is about that relationship, and it is the one to read carefully.

# 4.2 India and Pakistan - Wars, Water and the Question of Kashmir

## Why this one is different from every other rivalry

**Let me start with the thing that makes India-Pakistan unlike anything else on the planet.**

**They have fought three full wars.**

1947–48, 1965, and Kargil in 1999. **And both have nuclear weapons.**

**Now compare that to the rest of the world.** Countries with nuclear weapons rarely fight each other directly. The United States and the Soviet Union never fought — they had thousands of nuclear warheads pointed at each other and managed forty-four years of standoff without a shot.

**India and Pakistan have fought two conventional wars *after* both became nuclear powers** — in 1965 Pakistan was years away from nukes, but in 1999 both had them, and they fought anyway.

**That is genuinely unusual, and it tells you something about how much Kashmir matters to both countries.**

**But here is the deeper point, and it is the one I want you to hold onto for the rest of this file:**

**India-Pakistan is not a negotiation. It is a situation.**

Let me explain what I mean, because it is the single most important idea in this file.

**In the India-China relationship, there is a bargaining problem.** Both sides want different things and they could in principle trade. **India-Pakistan has no such structure.** The dispute arose from a hurried partition in 1947. Both countries claim the same territory. Both have nuclear weapons. Both have internal politics that make compromise politically impossible for any government that attempts it.

**Which means: no deal is available. And therefore the dispute is permanent.**

**And this is why the world has largely stopped trying.** There was a period when there was real optimism — after the 2008 Mumbai attacks, the composite dialogue began again, and many people expected a settlement. **That process has largely stalled, and almost nobody serious expects a Kashmir settlement in the foreseeable future.**

## The three wars

**Let me be precise about each, because the details explain the present.**

**The First War, 1947–48.** This is the one that created the Kashmir problem, and it was unusual in a way that still shapes everything.

**Here is what happened.** In 1947, when the British left, Kashmir was a princely state with a Muslim majority but a Hindu Maharaja. **The Maharaja delayed deciding which country to join.** Meanwhile, tribal fighters from Pakistan's North-West Frontier Province crossed into Kashmir. **The Maharaja asked India for help, and India agreed on the condition that he sign the Instrument of Access — joining India — which he did.**

**So India's position has always been that Kashmir legally acceded to India in 1947.**

**Pakistan's position is that this was not a valid accession**, and that the conflict should have been settled by a plebiscite — a vote by the people of Kashmir.

**The result was a war, and a ceasefire in 1949 that left approximately one third of the former princely state under Pakistani administration and two thirds under Indian administration.** **That line is the Line of Control.**

**Now here is the part that is genuinely uncomfortable and that you should know: the plebiscite was promised.**

**In 1948, the UN passed Resolution 8, which said that the plebiscite should be held once the fighting stopped.** But it was never held, and India's position is that circumstances changed — the war, the UN's own position on Kashmir as a dispute, and forty years of inaction all mean it is no longer possible.

**So there is a specific, documented, unresolved international commitment sitting underneath this entire dispute.** That is worth knowing, and it is more complicated than either side's public version.

**The Second War, 1965.** This one is interesting because **India won it, and it made relations worse.**

Under President Ayub Khan, Pakistan launched an operation across the international border in Kashmir. India counter-attacked, and there were major battles — one of the largest tank battles in history, at Phillora, which is in Punjab.

The ceasefire came in September 1965, roughly as it stood at the start. **India ended up with territory it had not held at the start, including areas around Lahore and Rawalpindi, which it then had to hand back.**

**Why is that significant?** Because **it broke the relationship and pushed India firmly into a long period of isolation.** After 1965, India was estranged from both the West and China for years. And Pakistan — which won nothing, but was not the loser — **became the US's closest partner in the region during the Cold War.**

**So the war reshaped the entire strategic balance of South Asia.** That is the Phase 3 lesson in action: what looks like a border war is really about alliances.

**The Third War, Kargil, 1999.** We covered this in Phase 2. Let me add what it showed.

**This was the most limited of the three wars, and it was limited precisely because both sides had nuclear weapons.**

Pakistan's army crossed the Line of Control in the Kargil sector, and took high positions. **India responded, fought a limited high-altitude battle, and restricted itself to conventional weapons.** The US pressured Pakistan into withdrawing.

**And the war ended with a peace process, not a settlement.** The **Lahore Declaration** of February 1999 committed both countries to resolve all disputes including Kashmir peacefully, and a composite dialogue began that September.

**So the significant thing about Kargil is that it produced talks rather than a solution, and those talks have continued in fits and starts ever since.**

## The nuclear dimension — and the paradox

**Both countries tested nuclear weapons in 1998, within three months of each other.**

**India tested in May. Pakistan tested in May.** Each tested a few days after the other. Neither wanted to be second.

**And here is the paradox that makes this relationship genuinely unique.**

**Since 1998, India and Pakistan have not fought a full-scale war. At the same time, India-Pakistan is the only nuclear-armed pair in the world that has fought wars in living memory, and has fought repeatedly since 1947.**

**How has that been possible? Through a device called a "nuclear doctrine."**

**India's doctrine, published in 2003, is "No First Use."** That means: **India will never use nuclear weapons first. India will only use them in response to a nuclear attack.**

**And India backs this with a doctrine of massive retaliation.** If India is attacked, India says, the response will be overwhelming and not proportionate.

**So the two together mean something specific and slightly horrifying: India is willing to launch a full-scale nuclear attack in response to a small one, because if you are going to be attacked, you will not stop at proportional retaliation.**

**That is a genuinely destabilising doctrine, and it is why nuclear scholars worry about India-Pakistan more than most other nuclear pairs.**

**Now here is the genuinely remarkable fact, and it is the one that surprises everybody.**

**India and Pakistan have kept this peace anyway. For twenty-five years now, through multiple crises.**

**How?** Because of what Phase 3 called **crisis hotlines and back-channel communication.**

**After 1999, India and Pakistan established a series of measures specifically to prevent the two armies from stumbling into a nuclear war.** Hotlines between military commanders. Notifications of missile tests. Agreements not to attack nuclear installations. Regular foreign secretary talks.

**And this is the crucial insight: they never settled Kashmir, and they may never settle it. But they built enough infrastructure to avoid the one outcome nobody wants.**

**That is a real and under-rated achievement of both countries' bureaucracies, and it deserves more credit than it gets.**

## What each side actually wants

**Now let me put the interests plainly, because they are simpler than the rhetoric suggests.**

**India's interests, honestly stated:**

**First, that Kashmir remain under Indian administration.** Not because of what happens in Kashmir — but because if India accepted Pakistani sovereignty over Kashmir, that would raise the question of why it holds any territory, and would be read across the whole subcontinent as a defeat.

**Second, that the Siachen Glacier situation stay frozen.** Siachen is the highest battlefield in the world, where India and Pakistan's forces face each other at about 22,000 feet. It is technically frozen, and both sides have treated it as a stalemate.

**Third, that Pakistan not acquire depth against India.** Pakistan's "strategic depth" doctrine — the idea that Pakistan can balance a larger India by using Afghanistan and, historically, China — is the frame for this.

**Pakistan's interests, honestly stated:**

**First, that Kashmir be resolved in Pakistan's favour, or at least internationally recognised as a disputed region.** Pakistan has repeatedly said it will accept a negotiated solution that includes a plebiscite, which would be the minimum acceptable outcome.

**Second, that India not consolidate its position in Afghanistan.** This has become Pakistan's central anxiety since 2001, and file 4.3 covers it.

**Third, that the Kashmir issue remain internationally salient.** Because an unresolved Kashmir means Pakistan's security concerns remain on the international agenda — and Phase 2 showed how effectively India has historically used that agenda to block UN action on Kashmir.

## Afghanistan — the issue that dominates modern India-Pakistan relations

**This is the most important thing to understand about the relationship today, and it is recent.**

**Here is the timeline, and it is short and devastating for Pakistan.**

**2001.** The September 11 attacks happen in the United States. **The United States asks Pakistan for help.** Pakistan agrees, and gives the US access to its airspace and its intelligence.

**And here is the central irony: the man the US calls "Osama bin Laden," responsible for the 9/11 attacks, was living in Pakistan, under the protection of the Pakistani state.**

**Pakistan's calculation was that siding with the US would bring it large economic aid and international standing.**

**What it instead brought it was two decades of being told what to do**, and the collapse of its relationship with the United States by the 2010s — including the death of Osama bin Laden in a US raid in Pakistan in 2011, carried out without Pakistani consent, which was deeply humiliating for Pakistan.

**2021.** The United States withdraws from Afghanistan after twenty years. **The Taliban returns to power.** The entire apparatus Pakistan built over two decades collapses.

**And here's where India's interest appears, and it explains India's current position.**

**India had built a relationship with the Afghan government and established a diplomatic presence in Kabul. Then the Taliban returned, and India did not recognise their government, while Pakistan did.**

**India reopened its embassy in Kabul in 2023, under Taliban rule, sending a message to Pakistan that India intends to have a presence in Afghanistan. And Pakistan, which borders Afghanistan by land, sees this as an existential concern.**

**Why existential?** Because if India gains a presence in Afghanistan, and if India is an enemy of Pakistan, and if that presence is even a few hundred kilometres from the Pakistani border, then Pakistan's entire "strategic depth" doctrine fails.

**So: India's interest in having a presence in Afghanistan is small and symbolic. Pakistan's interest in preventing it is enormous.** **And that asymmetry is the real driver of the relationship today.**

**India knows this. And it has proceeded carefully — because a confrontation over Afghanistan would put India in a place it is deliberately avoiding: a second front.**

## The other thing India and Pakistan agree on — and nobody notices

**This is worth mentioning because it is genuinely unusual and rarely reported.**

**India and Pakistan, despite fighting three wars, share a common interest that neither will admit publicly.**

**Both India and Pakistan want a stable, unified, internationally recognised Afghanistan.**

**Why?** Because a fragmented Afghanistan — or one under the control of India's ally India — is bad for both. **Both want Kabul to be stable, and both want the international community to keep large-scale foreign forces out.**

**This is a genuine shared interest, and it is one of the very few India-Pakistan share. And it has kept their border quiet in a way that surprises people who only know the Kashmir story.**

## What this file is not

**Kashmir is not going to be settled soon, and believing it will be is a way of avoiding thinking clearly.** There is no mechanism, no agreed framework, and no domestic political space for any Indian or Pakistani government to make a deal. **Expecting a settlement is not optimism — it is a failure to understand the structure.**

**And the Line of Control is not a peace agreement.** It is a ceasefire line from 1949 that both sides maintain with active military positions and constant friction. The agreement that governs the relationship is the Simla Agreement of 1972, and it does not resolve the dispute.

**Nor is the Indo-Pakistan relationship "hot" in the way most people assume.** It is not, except in Kashmir. **Trade has grown steadily. Diplomats talk. There are hotlines. And in twenty-five years of mutual nuclear capability, neither side has escalated to a nuclear exchange.**

**And — the hardest thing to say — Kashmir is not really about Kashmir.** No Indian leader has ever had the domestic political space to give up any part of what India administers, and no Pakistani leader has had the space to give up the claim. **The dispute survives because of domestic politics on both sides, which is the subject of file 4.6.**
