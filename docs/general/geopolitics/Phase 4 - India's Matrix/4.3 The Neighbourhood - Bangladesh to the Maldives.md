India has two difficult relationships and six smaller ones.

**But the smaller ones are harder than they look, and that is what this file is about.**

In 2014, the Indian Prime Minister travelled to Japan and announced a policy called **"Neighbourhood First."** It sounded simple. It has proved to be close to impossible.

Here is why.

# 4.3 The Neighbourhood - Bangladesh to the Maldives

## The structural problem nobody designed

Let me start with a geography fact that explains almost everything that follows, and that you learned in Phase 1 without noticing it mattered.

**India is the largest country in the world by population, and every single one of its neighbours is smaller than it — and most of them are economically dependent on it.**

**Bangladesh depends on India for electricity, trade access and water.** Nepal depends on India for access to its only land route to the sea. Bhutan depends on India for its currency, its market, and essentially its entire external relationship. The Maldives depends on India for its economy.

**So this is a relationship where one side is enormously powerful and the other side cannot function without it.**

**And here is the structural problem with that: asymmetry breeds resentment, and resentment breeds friction.**

**From the Indian side, it looks like: "We are a great power. We should lead. Why aren't these countries behaving?"**

**From the neighbour's side, it looks like: "We are a small country next to an enormous one, we depend on them for everything, and they do whatever they like."**

**Neither side is lying. And that is precisely what makes it hard.**

**And now add the third factor — China — and it becomes genuinely difficult.**

## The China factor in the neighbourhood

**Here is the uncomfortable reality that is often left out of Indian accounts.**

**China is India's largest trading partner. China has signed agreements with almost every Indian neighbour.**

**But the specific claim made by some Indian strategic commentators — that China is systematically "encircling" India — deserves examination rather than acceptance.**

**Let me put the actual facts out.**

**China has, over roughly two decades, signed agreements to build or operate ports in or near:**

- **Pakistan** — Gwadar, on the Arabian Sea, in Pakistan-administered Kashmir
- **Sri Lanka** — Hambantota, on the southern tip of Sri Lanka
- **Myanmar** — Kyaukpyu, on the Bay of Bengal
- **Bangladesh** — a container terminal at Chittagong
- **Djibouti** — a military base, in the Horn of Africa at the mouth of the Red Sea

**These are all real. And they form a pattern, because they are all on the sea routes into the Indian Ocean.**

**But here is the honest counterpoint that is usually missing.**

**How much of this is actually about India?** China is building ports across the developing world — in East Africa, in the Mediterranean, in Central Asia. **China's strategy is global, not specifically anti-Indian.** Port building is what Chinese firms do when they expand, and Indian analysts read every one of them through an Indian lens.

**So the honest assessment is somewhere in the middle.** China is building a global network that happens to include India's doorstep. That is not the same as a deliberate encirclement plan — but it also is not innocent of the effect.

**And the counter-counterpoint: China needs this network regardless.** China's western provinces are landlocked. Its trade routes run through the Indian Ocean. **So the ports would be built anyway, and the only question is whether India is part of that system or bypassed by it.**

## Bangladesh — the biggest neighbour, and the most entangled

**Bangladesh deserves its own file in any serious account, and this is it.**

**Start with the scale.** Bangladesh has **more than 160 million people in a country smaller than Tamil Nadu.** It is one of the densest countries on Earth.

**And it is surrounded by India on three sides.**

**The relationship has three faces and all three are true at once.**

**The face of trade and interconnection.** Bangladesh's garments industry — which employs millions — depends heavily on access to Indian markets. **Cross-border trade between the two countries is enormous.**

**The face of water sharing.** This is the real problem. **The rivers that flow through Bangladesh originate in India**, and the sharing of their water has been contested for decades. **The Teesta river agreement has been pending since the 1960s** — India has not signed it, and this is a long-running irritant in the relationship.

**The face of distrust.** Bangladesh's political history is turbulent, and **India's hand in Bangladeshi politics is widely perceived — by the people of Bangladesh, not just by outsiders — as heavy-handed.** The release of Sheikh Hasina, the 2007–08 elections, and the 2024 political upheaval and Hasina's departure all sit in this frame.

**Now add the most important factor of all, which most people do not think about: China.**

**Bangladesh is the one neighbour where China's economic presence is genuinely significant.** China is Bangladesh's largest trading partner, largest creditor and largest source of investment. **And in 2022, when Bangladesh needed help with its energy crisis and dollar shortages, China offered assistance and India did not.**

**So Bangladesh is genuinely courted by both. And Bangladesh's own position is a careful balance.**

**But — and this is worth saying plainly — Bangladesh's alignment is driven largely by its own domestic politics.** Its governments in Dhaka have repeatedly come to power on anti-India platforms, because that is a reliable vote. **Bangladesh does not dislike India. It is suspicious of it, and it uses that suspicion domestically.**

**And this creates a specific problem for Indian diplomacy.** India cannot easily cultivate Bangladesh without appearing to interfere, and interference is exactly what it is accused of.

## Nepal — a genuine sovereignty problem

**Nepal is the most constitutionally interesting relationship in this entire file.**

**Here is the key fact, and it is genuinely unusual.** **Nepal's constitution states explicitly that Nepal is an independent, sovereign, indivisible nation, and that no power — including India — has any authority over it.**

**This is not diplomatic language. This is a constitutional statement, and it is the only such statement in the Indian subcontinent.**

**Nepal's relationship with India over the last three decades has been tense, and for reasons worth understanding.**

**In 2006, Nepal's King was deposed and Nepal became a republic, with a Maoist-led government.** That government pursued a policy of asserting sovereignty that had not existed before.

**Since then Nepal has oscillated between governments hostile and friendly to India.** And every Nepali government, regardless of its ideology, has asserted the same thing: **Nepal is sovereign.**

**Now the practical side. Nepal is landlocked — it has no access to the sea.** And there are essentially two ways out: through India, or through China.

**For decades India was the only option, and Nepal's entire economy depended on that transit.** And then China built roads and rail through Tibetan Nepal, offering an alternative.

**India and China have also disagreed about Nepal's constitutional system** — India preferred the monarchy restored in some form, China preferred the republic.

**So Nepal is a genuine battleground, and it is a small country with real agency.**

**One further complication: the border dispute.** Nepal and India have an unresolved boundary dispute. **A section of land near the Kalapani area is claimed by both.** And Nepal has sometimes cooperated with China on this — and with China involved, an unresolved boundary question can stall the border settlement indefinitely.

## Bhutan — the closest relationship, and the most complex

**Bhutan is India's closest partner in the neighbourhood, and this is unusual.**

**The relationship is close to the point where "ally" is a reasonable description.** India handles Bhutan's foreign relations, provides its defence, and its currency — the Indian rupee — is legal tender in Bhutan. **Thousands of Bhutanese work in India.**

**And there is a unique element.** In 2008, India unilaterally withdrew its military presence from Bhutan. **This was done to prove a point: that India did not occupy Bhutan, and that Bhutan was genuinely sovereign.**

**Now the complication, and it is a serious one.**

**India's interest in Bhutan is largely about China.** Bhutan sits on the Line of Actual Control, and it is the only Indian neighbour that shares a border with China.

**And India's primary concern with China has historically been not Chinese troops, but Chinese infrastructure** — roads, rail lines and other construction in Bhutan, which India regards as a strategic threat regardless of who builds it.

**So Bhutan is a small, sovereign country whose territory India cannot discuss with China without Bhutan objecting to.**

**And in 2024 there was a real diplomatic incident.** India expelled a Bhutanese diplomat it alleged was involved in espionage. **Bhutan denied the allegation**, and its then Prime Minister said publicly that he did not believe it.

**Why mention this?** Because it illustrates the fundamental tension perfectly. India and Bhutan are as close as two countries can be, and yet India has repeatedly treated Bhutanese sovereignty as secondary to its own China policy. **That is the fundamental contradiction of India's neighbourhood policy, and it is not a criticism of individual actors.**

## Myanmar — the neighbour India is losing

**Myanmar is the neighbour where India's position has deteriorated most.**

**Why is Myanmar important to India? Three reasons.**

**First, geography.** Myanmar sits on the eastern flank of India, and is the bridge to Southeast Asia.

**Second, the Bay of Bengal.** Phase 1 established that the world's shipping routes run through the Bay of Bengal. Myanmar's coast faces that route directly.

**Third, and the real reason: China.** Myanmar is China's most important connection to the Indian Ocean. China is building ports, railways and pipelines — and after the 2021 military coup, China has become Myanmar's principal economic partner.

**India's position is weak here, and honestly so.**

India had substantial influence in Myanmar during the reform years. **The 2011 onward liberalisation brought Indian investment and a free trade agreement that significantly increased India-Myanmar trade.** And then the 2021 military coup ended all of it.

**India recognised the junta, and this was controversial. The junta's human rights record is severe, and it has imprisoned political leaders including Aung San Suu Kyi. Recognising it means engaging with those responsible.**

**But the alternative — not engaging at all — means China becomes the only power in Myanmar. And India's calculation has been that it cannot afford that.**

**So India is in an uncomfortable position: its options are bad, and it has chosen the less bad one, and it knows it.**

**And the Rohingya are the other story here.** Almost a million Rohingya refugees have fled Myanmar to Bangladesh, and most live in Cox's Bazar. **Bangladesh has hosted them under international pressure, and is now under real strain.**

## The Maldives, Sri Lanka and the Indian Ocean

**These two belong together, because they are about the ocean rather than about borders.**

**Sri Lanka.** Historically the most militarily aligned of India's neighbours — India was its largest supplier of military equipment and a significant source of military aid during the civil war. **But since the civil war ended in 2009, Sri Lanka has become notably cautious.** It was one of the few countries that publicly backed China's position on the referendum on Sri Lankan Tamils.

**And the shift has continued.** Sri Lanka has refused a US request to allow its warships to dock, on the grounds that it would violate its agreements with China. **And Sri Lanka has been careful to stay on good terms with both India and China.**

**But the defining issue is the Indian Ocean itself**, and file 4.5 covers that. **For now, one fact: the Indian Ocean is the only ocean where India is not a small player.** In the Atlantic or the Pacific, India is one of several. In the Indian Ocean, it is the largest country and the only littoral state with real strategic weight.

**Which means: India does not need to ask permission in the Indian Ocean. It does need to keep it.**

**The Maldives.** This is a small island nation of about 500,000 people, and it has become strategically significant out of all proportion to its size.

**Why? Because it sits in the middle of the Indian Ocean**, and because it has become a site of competition between India and China.

**India has a defence agreement with the Maldives** and can operate military aircraft and use facilities there. **And in 2025, under the new Maldivian administration, this arrangement came under significant strain.** The Maldivian government, which had initially pursued an "India Out" policy, later moved toward a more balanced position. **And during the tensions in April 2025, India temporarily suspended the supply of currency in circulation — the Maldivian Rufiyaa — which the Maldivian government publicly criticised.**

**That is a genuinely instructive case.** India has, for decades, provided enormous economic support — including currency, which many Maldivians use — and has a legitimate security interest in the Indian Ocean. **But it has repeatedly acted as though its security interests outrank Maldivian sovereignty, and this is the central criticism of India's neighbourhood policy.**

**Which brings us to the honest assessment.**

## Why "Neighbourhood First" has been so hard

**The policy was announced in 2014, with three pillars: connectivity, security and prosperity.**

**It has had real results. Trade with the neighbourhood has grown, and infrastructure connectivity — rail, roads, ports — has expanded. But the strategic picture is mixed, and for structural reasons:**

**First: India has been asking for vulnerability, not submission.** When India asks Bangladesh to share the Teesta's water, Nepal to allow transit, or the Maldives to allow defence facilities, what it is asking for is a reduction in those countries' defensive options. **Every one of those requests asks for trust, and trust does not follow from size.**

**Second: India's domestic politics make some of this impossible.** Elections in Bhutan and the Maldives have produced governments explicitly hostile to Indian involvement. **And these governments have democratic mandates.** India cannot simply ignore them, because the alternatives are worse — and being seen to ignore them damages the relationship precisely where India needs it most.

**Third, and this is the deepest one: India cannot simultaneously be the neighbourhood's hegemon and its neighbour.**

**If India uses its power to extract concessions, it generates resistance.** If it leads by consent, it is slower and less decisive. And the events of 2017 onward — Doklam, the Maldives, Afghanistan, Sri Lanka's caution — are, in a reasonable reading, not failures but the predictable result of a power asymmetry that India has not resolved.

**India has been in the position of having to choose between strategic control and good relations. It has chosen control, mostly, and paid for it in goodwill.**

**Which is not necessarily wrong.** But it is a choice, and it should be understood as one.

## What this file is not

**India does not "encircle" China, and China does not "encircle" India.** The encirclement framing is the most politically useful myth on both sides, and it simplifies complex histories. China's ports are built globally, not specifically against India. India's infrastructure in the neighbourhood is for development, not solely for military use.

**Nor is India a regional hegemon.** India cannot override its neighbours' preferences, does not get its way in Nepal or the Maldives or Bhutan, and does not get its way in Afghanistan. **The idea that India can simply decide is the central failure of assumption in most Indian analysis.**

**And the biggest thing this file is not: China is not going away as a factor in the neighbourhood.** The relationship between China and these countries is economic and deep, and no amount of Indian engagement will undo it. **The realistic goal is not to keep the neighbourhood away from China, but to keep the neighbourhood close enough that its alignment with China does not become hostile to India.**

**That is a much more modest goal than the one usually stated, and it is probably the achievable one.**

## Bridge to the next file

You now have India's two hard relationships and its six small ones.

**But all of those relationships have been in India's own neighbourhood — and there is a much bigger strategic question outside it, which the Chinese and Pakistani cases both point to.**

That is: **India is being courted by the United States, and India is also close to Russia. And those two relationships are pulling in opposite directions.**

India has to decide, in practice, how to be Western enough to get the technology and investment it needs, without being aligned enough to lose its independence. **That is the central strategic question of Indian foreign policy in the 2020s, and the next file examines it directly.**
