Three phases of preparation, and this is where it pays off.

**India and China is the relationship that will dominate your lifetime.** Not because it is the most important relationship in the world, but because it is the one you live inside.

And it is genuinely strange, because the two countries are simultaneously **rivals, partners, and something that fits neither word.**

# 4.1 India and China - The Long Border

## First, the strangeness

Let me start with the fact that makes this relationship unique, because it is not obvious and it is not normal.

**India and China share a 3,488 km border — the longest and most disputed land border in the world.**

**And yet:**

- They have fought a war on that border.
- Chinese troops and Indian troops have killed each other, most recently in 2020.
- There is **no agreed border line** between them.
- **And they trade enormous amounts of goods.**

**That last point is the strange one.** Countries do not usually fight a war and then massively expand trade. But India and China did.

**Let me put the numbers on it, because they are genuinely hard to believe.** In recent years, China has been India's **largest trading partner by value of goods** — larger than the United States. Roughly **a hundred billion dollars a year and more**, in both directions.

**So China and India fight soldiers at one end of a border, and ship each other phones and machinery at the other.** Any framework that says "rivals or partners" cannot hold both of those facts.

**The explanation, which is the key to this entire file, is this:**

**India and China have almost nothing in common and no shared history of conflict as nations.** There was never a war between an Indian state and a Chinese state. **They are neighbours because of geography — the Himalayas put them next to each other — and neither chose that.**

**That is why this relationship is so different from India-Pakistan, which shares a long history of actual conflict. India-China is not a grudge. It is two large societies being forced into proximity by mountains.**

**And that has a practical consequence you should hold onto: because there is no shared history of actual war between the two peoples, there is room to negotiate.** India-Pakistan has no such room, and file 4.2 shows why that matters enormously.

## The border: three segments, three different problems

**China's border with India is not one border. It is three, and they are separated by the state of Sikkim.**

**This is worth pausing on, because it is genuinely unusual and it caused a diplomatic incident.**

**Sikkim sits between Nepal to the west, Bhutan to the north, and Tibet to the north-east.** So the India-China border runs in two separate sections — one to the west of Sikkim, and one to the east of it. **These two sections do not connect at all.** They are separated by Sikkim's territory.

**And there was a period — 2005 to 2006 — when India and China could not communicate about border matters at all, because there was no direct diplomatic contact between their border districts.** They were negotiating about a place that was not directly reachable from either side. It is an absurd situation, and it is real.

**Now, the three segments:**

**The Western sector — Aksai Chin and Ladakh.** This is the militarily most sensitive. Both countries have built heavily fortified positions here. **The Line of Actual Control runs through it.** This is where the 1962 war happened and where the 2020 Galwan clash happened.

**The Middle sector — Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand.** Less militarised, but this is where the **Doklam** standoff happened in 2017 — a confrontation in Bhutan's territory, which was unusual and significant because it involved a third country.

**The Eastern sector — Arunachal Pradesh.** This is where the **McMahon Line** runs, and it is the most populous of the three. This is the sector China claims most of, and it is covered in detail in file 1.3.

## The war of 1962 and the 20 years after

**We covered 1962 in Phase 2, but let me add the part that changes everything now that you understand the relationship.**

**India lost that war, and lost it in about a month. And that loss shaped thirty years of Indian politics and continues to shape it today.**

**But the crucial thing to know is this: the border was not settled by 1962.** Both countries moved troops afterwards. Both countries claimed more than they controlled. **And for twenty years after 1962, the two countries had almost no diplomatic contact at all.**

**Then, in 1988, Rajiv Gandhi — Prime Minister at the time — became the first Indian Prime Minister to visit China in 38 years.** That visit opened everything, and it is where India's China policy of the 1990s and 2000s began.

**And the reason it worked is worth understanding: it was a turning point that both sides wanted.**

**For China,** the 1980s were a difficult decade. Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms were under way, and China needed foreign investment and trade more than it needed a confrontation with India.

**For India,** after 1962 the relationship had been frozen for a generation. Opening it was a natural thing for a new Prime Minister to try.

**So both sides wanted the same thing at the same time, which is why it worked.** That is Phase 3's "interest" framework working in practice.

## The 1990s and 2000s — a genuinely close relationship

**This is a period most people in India do not know about well, and it is important to understand because it explains why the relationship is the way it is today.**

**In the 1990s, India and China became genuinely close.** Not friendly — they are not friends — but **cooperative on specific issues**, and much closer than at any point before or since.

**Specifically:**

**Bilateral trade expanded enormously** as China's economy grew and India opened up.

**India and China worked together on the Non-Aligned Movement.** They were both members, and China — not the Soviet Union — was India's largest trading partner by the mid-1990s.

**And on border matters, they negotiated seriously.** In **1993**, they signed an **Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquillity Along the Line of Actual Control.** In **1996**, they signed a further agreement on **Confidence Building Measures** — arrangements to reduce the risk of accidental conflict, like prior notification of military exercises near the border.

**Now — and this is the crucial point — those agreements have been violated repeatedly.** Both sides have violated them. Military exercises near the border are routinely conducted without the prior notification those agreements require. The armies are in constant friction. In 2020 they killed each other.

**So the 1990s cooperation produced paperwork, not trust.** That is a genuine and useful observation, and it will be the subject of a later file on how this relationship actually works.

**And here is where the two most important documents come from, and both come from 2012.**

**In May 2012, India and China signed the "Bodnath" agreement** — formally, the Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination on Border Management, agreed during Manmohan Singh's visit to Beijing.

**And in 2012, India published its first ever written national security doctrine.** It was called the **Long Term Strategic Vision for China**, prepared by the Ministry of External Affairs.

**And this document is remarkable, so let me quote from it:**

> "India-China relations are guided by a framework of mutual respect for each other's core interests, because both parties have articulated that an India-China partnership would be a natural and positive relation."

**And in the same document, India states plainly:**

> "The border should be respected as a national boundary, and disagreements and differences should not be allowed to create an adverse atmosphere."

**And critically:**

> "India does not seek an asymmetrical or unequal relationship."

**Read those words carefully, because they are the whole thing.** "Mutual respect for core interests" is the standard diplomatic formulation for "we acknowledge your concerns." "Does not seek an asymmetrical relationship" is India saying: **we are not going to be a junior partner or a supplicant, no matter how much you trade with us.**

**That is a clear-eyed document, and it was written by a government that had just faced Chinese restrictions on the export of critical technology, and a year before the Doklam standoff.**

## What China wants from India — and what it will not get

**Let me put China's position plainly, because it is often misreported in India.**

**China officially frames its India policy as a partnership, and has repeatedly said it does not seek confrontation.** That is not obviously false. **China has repeatedly described India as a "partner" and said the two countries should not compete for development.**

**So the question is: what does China actually want from the relationship?**

**Here is the honest assessment.**

**What China probably wants:**

**First, that India not align with the United States.** China has repeatedly said it does not want India to join any Western alliance, and has offered partnership instead. This matters to China because it considers the US its main strategic competitor.

**Second, that India's relationship with Taiwan stay limited.** India follows a "one China" policy and has no formal diplomatic ties with Taiwan, which is significant because Taiwan is the most likely flashpoint between China and the US.

**Third, access and presence in the Indian Ocean.** China's Belt and Road runs through the Indian Ocean, and India's coastline is the barrier it has to get around. This is file 4.5.

**And what China will not accept:**

**A formal alliance between India and the United States.** The **Quad** — the United States, Japan, India and Australia — is the clearest test. China has been openly hostile to the Quad, and has pressed India not to join it.

**And here is the strange and revealing part.**

**India officially does not describe the Quad as an alliance.** It is not a military alliance. There is no mutual defence treaty. But it has clearly been discussed as one — and in 2023, the Quad leaders agreed to make it "a more operational partnership" with a timetable for further cooperation.

**So we have a situation where India joined something China considers hostile, and China has responded with a mix of pressure and persuasion. This is the central tension of India's China policy, and it is not going to be resolved.**

## And what India has given up to keep this balance

**This is where I want to be honest about a cost, because it is often left out.**

**India has taken a lot of pressure from China in order to avoid confrontation, and India has largely absorbed it.** Some examples:

**India banned Chinese apps** after 2020, affecting hundreds of millions of users who lost popular apps.

**India restricted Chinese investment.** Indian rules now require government approval for investment from countries sharing a land border with India — a rule written specifically about China.

**India's exports to China have been hit** by restrictions and by what appears to be a deliberate campaign against Chinese goods in India.

**And India has faced a real problem with critical supplies.** As India's economy and its dependence on imported components has grown — especially electronics — India has become exposed to China's near-monopoly on several critical manufacturing inputs. **This is a genuine strategic vulnerability, and it is one of the most serious problems in this relationship.**

**So the cost of not confronting China is that India has quietly accumulated economic exposure to it.** And the cost of confronting China is that India has no security guarantee and could be on its own. **That is the bind this file has been describing, and it is genuinely difficult.**

## What India actually wants — its own stated position

**It is worth stating India's position in its own terms, because it is coherent even if you disagree with it.**

**From the 2012 strategic vision document, and consistently since:**

**India wants three things from the relationship.**

**First, that the border be settled through agreement.** Not imposed. India's position is that the LAC was never agreed and must be mutually agreed — and this is worth noting, because it is a genuine position, not propaganda.

**Second, that the two economies coexist.** India wants trade to continue, because it needs Chinese inputs and China wants a market.

**Third, that neither country constrain the other's strategic space** — no encirclement, no alliance against the other, no military presence in the other's neighbourhood.

**And here is the tension: China's and India's third points are in direct conflict.** China's first point, India not aligning with America, is a direct constraint on India's foreign policy. **And India's third point, no alliance against the other, is exactly what the Quad is.**

**So each side's third demand rules out the other's first demand.** Neither has found a way around this, and neither has shown willingness to.

## What this file is not

**China is not India's enemy, and India is not a Western ally.** Both of those simplify something genuinely complicated. **India is closer to China on trade than to the United States, and closer to the United States on security than to China.** Neither of those facts is hypocrisy — it is the actual result of being in the middle.

**Nor is the relationship reducible to "border disputes."** The LAC is real and unresolved, but the deepest issue is China's presence in the Indian Ocean, which is the subject of file 4.5.

**And India's assertion that the LAC must be mutually agreed is a genuine position, but it is also self-interested.** India claims more territory than it controlled in 1962 — it held forward positions in Arunachal that it lost. **So "settle the LAC by agreement" means negotiate from a position where India claims territory it never actually held.** That is not an accusation — it is a fact you should hold alongside India's position.

**And the 1990s cooperation did not fail because the leaders were weak.** It failed because both sides' domestic politics moved faster than the relationship. **China's rise made it confident; India's capacity grew; and both outgrew the arrangement they had signed.**

You now have the China relationship: the border, the history, the documents, the trade, and the bind.
