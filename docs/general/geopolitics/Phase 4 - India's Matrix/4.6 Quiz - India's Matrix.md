# 4.6 Quiz - India's Matrix
## MCQ

Q: What makes India-China structurally different from most rival relationships?
A. India and China share a long history of war as nations
B. India and China have no shared history of conflict as nations — they are neighbours purely because of geography
C. China and India are both communist states
D. India and China fought the 1962 war over territory they both controlled
> B. No Indian state ever fought a Chinese state. The two are neighbours because the Himalayas put them next to each other, and neither chose it. This matters practically: because there is no shared grudge between the two peoples, there is room to negotiate. India-Pakistan has no such room.

---

Q: Why was there no diplomatic contact between India's and China's border districts for a period?
A. Because Sikkim closed the border
B. Because Sikkim separates the two sections of the border, so the districts could not reach each other
C. Because of a trade embargo
D. Because Nepal blocked the route
> B. Sikkim sits between Nepal, Bhutan and Tibet, splitting the India-China border into two disconnected sections. For a year there were no direct contacts between the Indian and Chinese border districts, because neither could reach the other — a genuinely absurd diplomatic situation.

---

Q: What were the 1993 and 1996 India-China agreements?
A. Trade agreements
B. Peace and tranquillity along the LAC, and Confidence Building Measures to reduce accidental conflict
C. Border demilitarisation agreements
D. Currency agreements
> B. The 1993 agreement committed both to peace and tranquillity along the LAC; the 1996 agreement added Confidence Building Measures such as prior notification of military exercises. Both were repeatedly violated by both sides.

---

Q: What does India's "Long Term Strategic Vision for China" say, and why does it matter?
A. That China is a threat to be contained
B. That India does not seek an asymmetrical or unequal relationship, and the border should be respected
C. That the border dispute should be referred to the UN
D. That India will resume the 1962 war
> B. It commits India to "mutual respect for each other's core interests" but also states India "does not seek an asymmetrical or unequal relationship" and that the border should be respected as a national boundary. It matters as India's clearest written statement of what it will not accept. It was written in 2012, a year before Doklam.

---

Q: What does China want from India, and what will it not accept?
A. Trade concessions, and it will accept any Indian alliance
B. That India not align with the US, limited ties with Taiwan, and Indian Ocean access; it will not accept a formal India-US alliance
C. That India withdraw from the Quad
D. Nothing in particular
> B. India's own third demand — that no alliance be formed against the other — is exactly what the Quad is. So each side's third demand rules out the other's first, and neither has found a way around it. India, being the weaker party, has more reason to accommodate.

---

Q: What has India given up economically in order to avoid confronting China?
A. Nothing significant
B. App bans, investment restrictions, and heavy exposure to Chinese critical manufacturing inputs
C. Its entire trade relationship
D. Its membership of the WTO
> B. India banned Chinese apps affecting hundreds of millions of users and restricted Chinese investment. More seriously, it has accumulated heavy exposure to China's near-monopoly on several critical manufacturing inputs, particularly in electronics — a genuine strategic vulnerability.

---

Q: Why is India's position that the LAC must be "mutually agreed" self-interested?
A. Because India does not want peace
B. Because India claims territory it did not control in 1962
C. Because China proposed it
D. Because the UN supports it
> B. India held forward positions in Arunachal that it lost in 1962 and never regained. So "settle the LAC by agreement" means negotiating from a claim over ground it never actually held. That is a fact to hold alongside India's stated position, not an accusation.

---

Q: What makes India-Pakistan structurally different from a normal rivalry?
A. It is not a negotiation but a situation — there is nothing to bargain over
B. India is stronger
C. Both are nuclear powers
D. Pakistan is smaller
> A. Both countries claim the same territory, both have nuclear weapons, and no government on either side has domestic political space to compromise. Unlike India-China, there is no bargaining structure at all. The dispute survives seventy years and shows no sign of ending.

---

Q: What happened in the 1947-48 Kashmir war?
A. Kashmir voted in a referendum to join India
B. The Maharaja delayed deciding, tribal fighters crossed from Pakistan, and the Maharaja signed the Instrument of Access joining India; the ceasefire left roughly a third under Pakistani administration
C. The UN partitioned Kashmir
D. Pakistan annexed all of Kashmir
> B. That division is the Line of Control. And critically, the UN passed Resolution 8 in 1948 calling for a plebiscite that was never held — a specific, documented, unresolved international commitment underneath the whole dispute.

---

Q: Why did India fighting and winning the 1965 war make relations worse?
A. Because India lost territory
B. Because it broke India's ties with both the West and China, and pushed Pakistan into becoming America's Cold War partner
C. Because Pakistan surrendered
D. Because the UN intervened
> B. India ended up with territory it handed back, but proved it could fight — which pushed Pakistan firmly into the US camp. The war reshaped the entire strategic balance of South Asia, showing that what looks like a border war is really about alliances.

---

Q: What does India's "No First Use" doctrine commit it to, and why is it destabilising?
A. Never using nuclear weapons at all
B. Never using first, but a massive and disproportionate response to any nuclear attack
C. Using nuclear weapons in response to a conventional attack
D. Retaining a second-strike capability
> B. The two together mean India is willing to launch a full-scale nuclear attack in response to a small one. That is genuinely destabilising and is why India-Pakistan concerns nuclear scholars more than most nuclear pairs.

---

Q: How have India and Pakistan avoided nuclear war for twenty-five years without settling Kashmir?
A. Through a peace treaty
B. Through crisis hotlines, back-channel communication and notifications
C. Through UN intervention
D. Through demilitarisation
> B. Hotlines between commanders, prior notification of missile tests, agreements not to attack nuclear installations, and regular foreign secretary talks. They never needed to settle Kashmir — they built enough infrastructure to prevent the one outcome nobody wants, which is a real and under-rated achievement.

---

Q: Why is Afghanistan the central issue in modern India-Pakistan relations?
A. Because India occupies Afghanistan
B. Because India's interest is small and symbolic while Pakistan's is enormous — a presence in Afghanistan would destroy Pakistan's strategic depth
C. Because both want oil from there
D. Because of UN membership
> B. If India gains a presence in Afghanistan and is a Pakistani enemy, Pakistan's strategic depth doctrine fails. India reopened its embassy in Kabul in 2023 under Taliban rule to signal this. India knows the asymmetry and has proceeded carefully.

---

Q: What shared interest do India and Pakistan have?
A. Both want a stable, internationally recognised Afghanistan with large foreign forces out
B. Both want oil
C. Both want to join the Quad
D. Both want to resolve Kashmir
> A. It is one of very few genuine shared interests between them, and it has kept their border quieter than the Kashmir story would suggest. It is rarely admitted publicly because acknowledging it would undermine the "permanent enemy" framing both countries use.

---

Q: Why is India's relationship with its neighbours structurally harder than China's?
A. India's neighbours are larger
B. India's neighbours are all smaller and economically dependent on it, and China has more comparable neighbours
C. India has fewer neighbours
D. China's neighbours are not friendly
> B. The asymmetry runs one way, and asymmetry breeds resentment. China's neighbours — Vietnam, India, Russia, Mongolia, North Korea — are more comparable in size and power, and none is dependent on China the way Bangladesh is on India.

---

Q: Is China "encircling" India?
A. Yes, definitively
B. No — China's port building is global, not India-specific, though it does include India's doorstep and is not innocent of the effect
C. No, China has no interest in the Indian Ocean
D. Only since 2020
> B. China builds ports across the developing world as it expands. But China's western provinces are landlocked, so it needs the network regardless, and India's doorstep is part of it. The problem is not the intention but the effect — which is what matters strategically.

---

Q: Why is the Teesta water dispute so persistent in India-Bangladesh relations?
A. Bangladesh needs the water more
B. It converts Bangladesh's structural weakness into domestic political gain
C. India wants the water
D. The river has changed course
> D is true — the river has shifted — but the deeper reason is B. Dhaka governments come to power on anti-India platforms and can then appear to stand up for Bangladesh's water against India. The dispute converts weakness into political gain, which is why it persists despite being a technical issue.

---

Q: What makes Nepal's constitutional language unique in South Asia?
A. It renounced the monarchy permanently
B. It states explicitly that Nepal is sovereign and that no power including India has authority over it
C. It abolished the army
D. It declared neutrality
> B. This emerged after 2006, when the monarchy was deposed and a Maoist-led government came to power. It is the only such constitutional statement in the region, and every Nepali government since has maintained it.

---

Q: What is the fundamental contradiction in India's relationship with Bhutan?
A. India and Bhutan disagree about the boundary
B. India treats Bhutanese sovereignty as secondary to its own China policy, despite the relationship being as close as any country's
C. Bhutan is a member of the Quad
D. India does not trade with Bhutan
> B. India has repeatedly acted as though its China policy outranks Bhutan's sovereignty — the 2024 espionage allegations, which Bhutan publicly denied, are an example. This is not a criticism of individuals but of the structural contradiction of India's neighbourhood policy.

---

Q: Why is India in an uncomfortable position on Myanmar?
A. It does not recognise the junta
B. Its options are bad and it has chosen the less bad one — not recognising the junta means China becomes the only power there
C. Myanmar has no strategic value
D. India has no embassy
> B. India recognised the junta despite its severe human rights record and the imprisonment of Aung San Suu Kyi. The alternative was worse and India knows it, but it is still a choice that should be named as one.

---

Q: What does the Maldives case reveal about India's neighbourhood policy?
A. That India has lost the Maldives
B. That India has repeatedly treated its strategic interests as outranking Maldivian sovereignty
C. That the Maldives wants India out permanently
D. That China controls the Maldives
> B. The 2025 suspension of currency supply — which the Maldivian government publicly criticised — showed India treating economic dependency as leverage. This is the central criticism of India's neighbourhood policy.

---

Q: Why did India and the United States go from enemies to partners?
A. Because of shared ideology
B. Because after 2001 the US needed help in Afghanistan and someone who could restrain Pakistan, and India was the only country that could do both
C. Because India left the Non-Aligned Movement
D. Because of the nuclear tests
> B. It is the interest framework from Phase 3 working perfectly. Shared ideology was not the driver — America's need was.

---

Q: Why were the 1974 US sanctions on India harsher than on Pakistan?
A. Because India tested first
B. Because the US refused to acknowledge India's test at all and cut off food aid during a famine
C. Because India was communist
D. Because Pakistan apologised
> B. India's experience of American power is associated with hunger and coercion rather than partnership, which is why trust took four decades to build.

---

Q: What is the Quad, and what is it not?
A. An alliance with a mutual defence treaty
B. A grouping of the US, Japan, India and Australia — not a military alliance, with no mutual defence treaty
C. A trade bloc
D. An Indian organisation
> B. It has no mutual defence obligation. But it was revived in 2017, the year of Doklam, and has become India's main forum for engaging with the US, Japan and Australia. India has never called it anti-China and never denied it.

---

Q: What did India's abstention on Ukraine actually cost it?
A. Nothing
B. Real strain with the US, and exposure to US sanctions on the discounted Russian oil it buys
C. Its membership of the Quad
D. Its relationship with China
> B. India now buys discounted Russian oil, often from sanctioned tankers and via intermediaries — so it is exposed to sanctions on the very thing it depends on. And that oil is not a solution, because most of India's critical inputs come from the Gulf.

---

Q: Is India's argument about NATO expansion coherent?
A. No, it is Russian talking points
B. Yes — it is a genuine position made in the West too, and it does not make the invasion acceptable
C. It is only about oil
D. India has never made it
> B. India argued the West provoked this by expanding NATO and then expanding further rather than negotiating. It is an uncomfortable argument to hear if you are European, and it is entirely coherent — and it is made by people in the West who do not support the invasion.

---

Q: What is the "internal critique" of India's foreign policy posture?
A. That India is too close to the West
B. That India's cautious compromise produces the worst of both worlds — Western technology, discounted Russian oil, and neither as a real partner
C. That India should leave the Quad
D. That non-alignment was a mistake
> B. The strategic community is more Atlanticist than successive governments have been, and they argue India should commit more. Whether that critique is valid depends on what you think India's strategy should be.

---

Q: Why did the Indian Ocean become strategically important in about twenty years?
A. Because oil was discovered under it
B. Because the oil trade moved east across it, China became the world's factory needing those routes, and over half the world's shipping goes through it
C. Because the UN declared it so
D. Because India discovered oil in the Arabian Sea
> B. Three things happened at once, and the result was that an ocean nobody had fought over became the central strategic question in Asia — with India at its centre.

---

Q: What is different about the Indian Ocean for India compared to any other ocean?
A. India owns most of it
B. India is the largest littoral country and the only regional navy of real weight, so it does not need anyone's permission there
C. It is the largest ocean
D. The US has no presence there
> B. In the Atlantic and Pacific India is one of several powers. In the Indian Ocean it is the only state that could project power anywhere in it. It does not need permission — it needs to keep it.

---

Q: What is the "String of Pearls"?
A. A Chinese naval fleet
B. China's chain of ports across the Indian Ocean — Gwadar, Hambantota, Kyaukpyu, Chittagong — plus a base in Djibouti
C. An Indian naval formation
D. A trade agreement
> B. Coined by Robert Dali in 2004. It is overwhelmingly commercial infrastructure rather than military network, which is precisely why it is built as ports — a port is much harder to criticise than a base.

---

Q: Why is China's base in Djibouti the most serious single development?
A. It is the largest overseas base
B. It sits at the southern entrance to the Red Sea, directly across from India's own base at Chaken
C. It gives China control of the Indian Ocean
D. It threatens the Strait of Malacca
> B. Two permanent foreign military bases a few kilometres apart at one of the world's most important chokepoints is not a commercial arrangement. And it is not much discussed in India.

---

Q: What is the "Necklace of Diamonds" and what is its logic?
A. An Indian naval formation
B. India's chain of access agreements on the far side of the Indian Ocean — notably Duqm in Oman, outside the Strait of Hormuz
C. A Chinese port network
D. A trade agreement
> B. Coined by Air Commodore Manpreet Singh in 2012. If China's string runs around India's neighbourhood, India's answer runs through the Indian Ocean to Africa. Duqm matters most because it sits outside Hormuz, so it remains accessible in the crisis India worries about most.

---

Q: Is the Necklace of Diamonds a real strategy?
A. Fully realised
B. Partly — Duqm is genuinely significant, but several agreements have delivered very little
C. Purely symbolic
D. It has replaced the String of Pearls
> B. The reason is not weakness but that treaties are much harder than construction. China builds a port; India writes an agreement and hopes it produces a facility. Different timescales, different difficulties.

---

Q: What is India's real naval weakness?
A. It has no navy
B. Submarines, basing, and the cost of maintaining its carriers
C. Airbases
D. Coast guard
> B. Submarines matter most, because they are the only weapon that can threaten a surface fleet unseen, and India's nuclear submarine programme is behind where it wants to be. Basing is the other — India's friends in the ocean are small states with limited infrastructure, which is what the Necklace of Diamonds is for.

---

Q: Why is the Diego Garcia base a genuine problem for India's position?
A. It is on Indian territory
B. India has argued Western positions on indigenous island populations should apply generally while itself granting the US a long lease on such an island
C. It is Chinese
D. India built it
> B. India called the Western position inconsistent while granting the US Diego Garcia, whose indigenous population was expelled. India's position is strategically coherent — a legal dispute should not create a strategic vacuum — but it is a real inconsistency that India's own civil society has pointed out.

---

Q: Why does the Kashmir dispute survive?
A. Because international law is weak
B. Because no leader in either country has the domestic political space to compromise
C. Because of the UN
D. Because of geography
> B. This is the structural answer. Any Indian government conceding anything on Kashmir faces severe domestic backlash, and any Pakistani government abandoning the claim would be accused of selling out. So the dispute is not a negotiation that failed — it is one that domestic politics prevents from starting.

---

Q: How did coalition mathematics shape Indian foreign policy?
A. It did not
B. In 2011–2014 the UPA government was effectively run by coalition partners, and India's abstention on Bahrain was partly a consequence; that government then collapsed over corruption allegations
C. Coalition governments were always more pro-West
D. It only affected domestic policy
> B. A domestic political crisis, not a strategic decision, ended that government. Non-alignment itself also emerged from domestic coalition arithmetic, which is why it held so long and why weakening it has been contested.

---

Q: Why is India's foreign policy a domestic compromise?
A. Because the government is weak
B. Because the strategic community is Atlanticist while public opinion resists alignment
C. Because the opposition blocks everything
D. Because of the courts
> B. India pursues practical cooperation with the West and careful relations with Russia and China, refusing to commit — a compromise between an Atlanticist establishment and a public opinion unwilling to be a second-order partner.

---

Q: How do India's domestic political problems connect to its external vulnerabilities?
A. They are unrelated
B. They come from the same history — the 1905 Bengal partition, state reorganisation boundaries, administrative decisions treated as strategic facts
C. External problems cause domestic ones
D. There is no connection
> B. The Chicken's Neck exists because of an administrative convenience in 1905. State boundaries were drawn along linguistic lines that put groups together who had not chosen to live together. India's internal and external problems are not separate — they are both consequences of decisions taken for administrative reasons.

---

Q: How does India's economic structure constrain its foreign policy?
A. It does not
B. India wants Western investment and technology, which requires openness, but political incentives push toward economic nationalism
C. India is protectionist
D. India does not need technology
> B. "Made in India" and openness pull in different directions, and the result is that India remains heavily import-dependent for critical inputs, including some from China. India's economic policy is constrained by what it needs more than by what it would prefer.

---

Q: What is the realistic scenario in the Indian Ocean?
A. War between India and China
B. Dependency, and the slow erosion of Indian influence
C. Nothing changes
D. China withdraws
> B. Nothing here points to military conflict — the facilities are commercial and defensive. But commercial dependence accumulates into strategic position, and that accumulation is what a rival would be planning for.

## TrueFalse

Q: China and India have a long shared history of war between their peoples.
- True
- False
> False. No Indian state ever fought a Chinese state. The relationship is entirely geographic, which is precisely why there is room to negotiate where there is none with Pakistan.

---

Q: India has publicly acknowledged that the LAC was never agreed and must be mutually agreed.
- True
- False
> True. This is India's stated position, and it is a genuine position — though it is also self-interested, since India claims territory it did not control in 1962.

---

Q: India gained territory during the 1962 war.
- True
- False
> False. India lost territory, including Aksai Chin and forward positions in Arunachal. The 1965 war was the one where India gained and then handed back territory.

---

Q: The 1990s India-China cooperation agreements have been consistently respected by both sides.
- True
- False
> False. Both sides violated them repeatedly, with military exercises conducted without the prior notification the agreements require. The cooperation produced paperwork, not trust.

---

Q: The Line of Control is a peace agreement.
- True
- False
> False. It is a ceasefire line from 1949, maintained with active military positions. The agreement governing the relationship is the Simla Agreement of 1972, which does not resolve the dispute.

---

Q: A plebiscite in Kashmir was never promised by the international community.
- True
- False
> False. UN Resolution 8 in 1948 called for it. India's position is that circumstances changed in the intervening decades. Either way, the commitment exists and was never fulfilled.

---

Q: India and Pakistan have not fought a full-scale war since 1971.
- True
- False
> True. Kargil in 1999 was a limited operation, deliberately restricted because both had nuclear weapons, and it was contained within weeks through US pressure.

---

Q: The String of Pearls is primarily a Chinese military network.
- True
- False
> False. It is overwhelmingly commercial port infrastructure. Treating it as purely military is the most common analytical error, and it makes China look more aggressive than the evidence supports.

---

Q: India's Necklace of Diamonds agreements have all been fully realised operationally.
- True
- False
> False. Duqm is genuinely significant, but the Mauritius Agalega agreement and others have delivered very little. Treaties are harder than construction.

---

Q: China's port network in the Indian Ocean would be built regardless of India.
- True
- False
> True. China's western provinces are landlocked and it needs those routes anyway. The question is not whether China will build them but whether India is part of the system or bypassed by it.

---

Q: Something at war involving Pakistan is more likely than something involving China.
- True
- False
> True. Because Pakistan's strategic depth doctrine requires India to be hemmed in, and India's strategy has relied on staying out of Pakistan's sphere — which makes actions there especially risky.

---

Q: India's abstention on the Ukraine war meant it supported the invasion.
- True
- False
> False. India has consistently called the invasion wrong and has repeatedly called for negotiations. It found the Western framing of the war selective, which is a different claim.

---

Q: The Quad commits members to defending each other.
- True
- False
> False. It has no mutual defence treaty. India joined because the practical cooperation was valuable and the deterrent effect was real, not because it had taken on an alliance obligation.

---

Q: India's strategic community and India's governments agree about how much to align with the West.
- True
- False
> False. The establishment is substantially Atlanticist; successive governments have been considerably more cautious, because public opinion resists alignment.

---

Q: Public opinion is a genuine constraint on Indian foreign policy.
- True
- False
> True. The Ukraine abstention aligned with genuinely unpopular opinion in India, and governments have also taken positions they knew would be unpopular at home.

---

Q: India-Pakistan is a situation rather than a negotiation.
- True
- False
> True. There is no bargaining structure, because both claim the same territory, both have nuclear weapons, and neither side has domestic political space. So the dispute has no mechanism to resolve it.

---

Q: China "encircles" India through the String of Pearls.
- True
- False
> False. China's network is global, not India-specific. The effect is real, but the intention is not — and confusing the two produces wrong conclusions.

---

Q: A huge share of the world's undersea internet cables pass through the Indian Ocean.
- True
- False
> True. Most international internet traffic runs through undersea cables, not satellites, and the routes between East Asia, Europe, the Gulf and Africa cross the Indian Ocean. This is one of the strongest strategic arguments for India.

---

Q: India is a signatory to the Belt and Road Initiative.
- True
- False
> False. India has consistently declined to join it, citing sovereignty concerns around the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor through disputed territory. So BRI is a source of tension, not cooperation.

---

Q: India's neighbourhood problems are caused mainly by hostility from its neighbours.
- True
- False
> False. They are also caused by India's own actions — trade conditionality, territorial disputes, economic pressure, and treating neighbouring sovereignty as secondary to Indian strategic interest. Both things are true.

---

Q: Sanctions against a country always hurt that country's government most.
- True
- False
> False. They generally hurt ordinary people most. Sanctions work only when internal opposition can use them — South Africa — and fail when they only impoverish civilians, as with Cuba.

---

Q: Understanding why a country acted on interest means you support what it did.
- True
- False
> False. Understanding improves prediction; it does not create approval. You can understand an interest completely and still think the action was wrong.

## ShortAnswer

Q: If you had to explain India's foreign policy posture to someone who thought India should pick a side, what would you say?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would say India's position is not indecision but a deliberate trade. Aligning with America would bring security guarantees Japan and South Korea have and India does not — but it would cost India the freedom to act in its own region and would cost it goodwill in the Global South, which is where India's long-term influence actually rests. Staying unaligned means India can work with both. The cost is real: India got neither an alliance nor the supply of advanced weapons it wanted. Whether that trade is worth it depends on whether you think threats to India's interests are more likely to come from China or from the failure of any system of mutual restraint. That is a genuine strategic judgement, not a settled question.

</details>

---

Q: A friend says "India is being encircled by China and should respond." How would you answer using this phase?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would separate the effect from the intention. China's ports would be built anyway — it needs them for its own landlocked west, and it is building them across Africa, the Mediterranean and Central Asia too. So this is not an encirclement plan. But the effect is real: China is developing a network around India's neighbourhood, and if India is not part of that system it is bypassed by it. So the honest position is that the framing is a myth and the concern is real, and India's response — access agreements on the far side of the ocean — is about being inside the system rather than outside it.

</details>

---

Q: Why is India-Pakistan more dangerous than India-China, even though China is the bigger power?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because of intent, not capability. China is building a position in the Indian Ocean and competing commercially; Pakistan is in a live territorial dispute that has produced three wars, and both have nuclear weapons. That combination — unresolved territory, active military lines, and mutual nuclear capability — is the specific configuration that has produced the dangerous near-misses since 1998. India-China can be managed without a shot being fired; India-Pakistan has almost been the opposite more than once.

</details>

---

Q: Explain why India's domestic politics make Kashmir unresolvable, and connect it to everything else in this phase.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** No Indian leader has ever had the domestic space to concede anything in Kashmir, and no Pakistani leader has had the space to abandon the claim. So it is not a failed negotiation — it is one that domestic politics prevents from starting. This connects to the whole phase because the same pattern appears elsewhere: the neighbourhood policy is shaped by elections in Dhaka and Malé, the Russia relationship by calculations about energy prices, and the Quad policy by what survives domestically. India's foreign policy is heavily decided inside India, and understanding that is what tells you which initiatives are realistic and which are announcements.

</details>
