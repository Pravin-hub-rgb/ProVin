# 3.4 Sanctions, Tariffs and Trade War

## Sanctions — punishment without war

**A sanction is a restriction placed on a country to change its behaviour.**

**The point of a sanction is to hurt a country without going to war.** That is its entire appeal. It is cheaper, safer, and reversible. And it has become one of the most-used tools in world politics.

**Here are the main types, and the distinction matters.**

**UN sanctions** are agreed by the Security Council and are treated as international law. **The US unilaterally sanctioned Israel in 1978**, in response to the Israeli invasion of Lebanon. This remains one of the most significant actions in UN history, and Israel has never accepted it.

**Unilateral sanctions** are imposed by one country without international agreement. **The United States has imposed more unilateral sanctions than the rest of the world combined.** These are legally domestic — affecting US banks and US companies — but they have global reach.

**And here is the type that matters most for India, and it is different from the others.**

**Secondary sanctions** punish a country for doing business with a sanctioned country. **This is the tool that has hit India directly, and the next section explains it in detail.**

## The India case — Iranian oil

This is the most concrete thing in this file, and it is worth understanding properly because it is a real example of the limits of power.

**The setup.** In 2015, the United States and other countries agreed with Iran to limit its nuclear programme, in return for sanctions relief. In 2018, the United States withdrew from that agreement and reimposed sanctions.

**Now the tool.** The United States told India to stop buying Iranian oil. **Not because America had any treaty requiring it, but because it threatened consequences against Indian banks.**

**And this is the specific mechanism: many of the world's largest banks have operations in the United States.** So if a bank does business with a sanctioned Iranian entity, it can lose access to the US financial system. **And in a global economy, losing that access is close to a death sentence** — you cannot settle international transactions, you cannot fund trade.

**Why would American banks want to defy their own government?** They would not. **So the threat works without anyone technically being ordered to do anything.** The enforcement is implicit.

**What did India do?** At first, India bought a lot of Iranian oil. In 2018 and 2019 India was among the largest buyers. **Then the pressure began, and India's purchases collapsed.**

**And here is the crucial fact: India did not comply because it agreed with American policy on Iran.** India has its own views on the Iran nuclear deal and has not always followed the international consensus. **India reduced Iranian oil purchases because it calculated that the economic cost of defiance was too high.**

**This is the single clearest demonstration of economic power in this course** — a country successfully pressuring a much larger economy by threatening the financial system rather than by threatening anything physical.

**Why is this important to understand?** Because it shows the limits of India's power honestly. **India is a large economy, but its economy is exposed to the international financial system in a way that gives it vulnerabilities.** And that is a vulnerability worth knowing about rather than being surprised by.

## Why sanctions rarely work as intended

Here is the uncomfortable part, and it is worth being straight about it.

**The stated purpose of sanctions is to change a government's behaviour. The actual effect is usually different, and the two frequently diverge.**

**Some of them work. Some clearly do not.**

**Sanctions that did have a real effect:** The sanctions against **apartheid South Africa** were comprehensive and sustained by many countries, and the international economic pressure contributed to the end of apartheid in 1994. **But also — internal resistance and negotiation did most of the work.** The sanctions helped an existing process rather than creating one.

**Sanctions that did not achieve their stated purpose:** The United States embargo on **Cuba** began in 1960 and lasted over fifty years. **It did not change Cuba's government.** It produced enormous hardship for ordinary Cubans and had no effect on the leadership it was aimed at. That is now accepted almost universally, and US policy has been largely reversed.

**And the hardest case: Iraq.** In 1990 Iraq invaded Kuwait, and the United Nations imposed comprehensive sanctions on Iraq. The sanctions continued for over a decade, including through the 1990s oil-for-food scandal, which was a serious corruption scheme involving the UN itself. **The sanctions were never lifted through negotiation.** Iraq's government was removed in 2003 by a military invasion.

**So: two decades of sanctions, and the stated objective was not achieved.** The invasion achieved it — but that was a war, not sanctions, and it cost enormously.

**And the pattern nobody says out loud: sanctions hurt ordinary people far more than governments.** The Cuban embargo harmed ordinary Cubans. Iraq sanctions in the 1990s contributed to severe humanitarian hardship. **The people making the decision are not the people paying the price**, and this is why sanctions attract criticism on human rights grounds.

## The instrument that hurts India

Back to the thing that matters most for you.

**India is not a sanctions target in the way Cuba or Iran is.** India is a large economy that the West wants to work with.

**But there is one category where India has felt the consequences of someone else's sanctions, and it is worth being precise about it.**

**The US imposes sanctions on some Indian entities — individuals and small companies — for various reasons.** The most common is the allegation of involvement in nuclear or missile activity, or in terrorism.

**And here is the important distinction for Indian business.** These sanctions typically target **specific entities** — a particular company, a particular person — rather than India as a country.

**So what does it mean in practice?** If a designated Indian company tries to trade internationally, it will find that international banks are unwilling to deal with it, because dealing with it would violate US law for any bank with US exposure. **That is a real cost and it is disproportionate to the target's actual size** — a mid-sized Indian firm being shut out of global finance over a designation it may appeal.

**And that is the right thing to be watchful about, because the tool has a disproportionate effect on smaller actors.** A government can designate a company with little domestic political cost, but that company may not survive what follows.

## Tariffs and trade wars — the other economic tool

**A tariff is a tax on imports.** Simple. And it is the second great instrument of economic pressure.

**The logic is straightforward: if country A puts a 30 percent tariff on country B's goods, then B's exports to A become expensive, and B's producers suffer.** It is meant to hurt B's economy and make A's demands more attractive.

**And in the last ten years this has become a systematic tool rather than an occasional one.**

**The biggest example is the US-China trade war.** Under President Trump, from 2018, the two countries imposed tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of each other's goods. **This was deliberate economic warfare between the two largest economies in the world.** And it continued under his successor, though the tone changed.

**What is notable is how much both economies were hurt by it.** Studies found that American consumers paid part of the cost through higher prices, and American firms that imported from China lost revenue. **So tariffs worked as intended in the sense that they caused pain — but they did not achieve much of what they were supposed to achieve.**

**And tariffs are used in a much less sophisticated way than sanctions, and much more often.** Many countries use them as **domestic politics.** A government imposes a tariff on steel, and domestic steel producers make money and jobs appear, so the government gets votes. This is sometimes called **"tariff theatre"** — tariffs as politics rather than as strategy.

**The other thing to know about tariffs is that they are legally constrained.** Countries are bound by the rules of the **World Trade Organization (WTO)**, and many of these tariffs have been challenged there. The WTO has become less effective in recent years, partly because its appellate body has been unable to function, which has been a major loss for the international trading system.

## Why trade is a tool at all

Something worth understanding, because it is not obvious until you look at it.

**Goods are not the only thing that moves between countries. Money is.**

**And because money moves easily, trade creates dependence, and dependence creates leverage.**

This is the core mechanism of economic power, and it works like this:

- If country A depends on country B to buy its goods, then **B can hurt A economically at any time it wants.**
- If country A depends on country B for something it cannot produce itself — oil, fertiliser, critical minerals — **then A has very little choice.**

**And this is where Phase 0 comes back in.** **Countries with few resources have little choice.** India imports about 85 percent of its oil. Saudi Arabia has a lot of oil and little else. **Both are exposed, but in opposite ways — and that shapes everything about how they behave in the world.**

**The last thing to understand: trade is the reason peace is worth keeping.** **Most countries are much better off under peace than war**, because trade requires stability. This does not make countries pacifist, but it is a constant pressure working against war.

## What this file is not

**Sanctions are not a punishment tool aimed at poor people.** They usually are, in practice, whether or not that is the intention. **The Cuban embargo and the Iraq sanctions both harmed ordinary people far more than the governments they were aimed at.** Anyone claiming otherwise should be asked how the cost is distributed.

**Nor is the answer to sanctions simply "more sanctions."** The Cuban embargo's failure is a consequence of decades of sustained pressure on a population. **Sanctions can entrench the very behaviour they aim to change.** That is a real and well-documented mechanism.

**Nor does using economic tools make something inherently right.** **Iran was right that the nuclear deal was being withdrawn from. And economic coercion was still a real pressure on a large population.** Being on the receiving end does not feel like being right, and there is no version of this in which the pressure is costless.

**And "tariff theatre" is not a conspiracy.** It is ordinary politics. Governments respond to domestic constituencies, and tariffs against imports are one of the few visible things a government can do that feels like action.
