# 3.5 Global South, Multipolarity and Strategic Autonomy

## First — what does "the Global South" mean?

This term comes up in almost every serious discussion of world affairs, and it is more contested than most people realise.

**What it roughly means:** the group of countries that were colonised by European powers and are now economically developing — most of Asia, most of Africa, and much of Latin America.

**But here is the problem: there is no agreed list.** And the argument about who belongs is not academic. It changes what countries actually get.

**Who is usually counted in the Global South?**

**India**, **China**, **Brazil**, **South Africa**, **Indonesia**, **Saudi Arabia**, **Pakistan**, **Bangladesh**, most of Africa.

**And who argues they should not be?**

**There are two live disputes.** Some analysts say **China and Russia should not count**, because they are major powers and not developing countries. Others say **they absolutely should count**, because they are building the alternative institutions that make the Global South's claim credible.

**There is a second dispute about India itself.** Some argue India should not count, because it is already a large economy and a nuclear power. **But India rejects this entirely** — it insists it is a developing country because a large share of its people still live on very low incomes, and because per-person income is what matters.

**India's position is: 300 million Indians still live in poverty. Under any honest measure, India is a developing country.**

**And that argument has won in most international forums.** India is treated as the leading voice of the Global South in negotiations on climate, development and trade.

## The Global South's actual power — and the reality

**Here is the surprising part, and it is genuinely counter-intuitive.**

**The Global South contains most of the world's population, most of the land, most of the natural resources, and it is growing economically faster than the rich countries.**

**By population alone, the Global South is the majority of humanity.** The G77 — the coalition of developing countries at the UN — has 134 members. **And these countries are not a marginal group. They are the world.**

**But — and this is the critical tension — the Global South does not behave as one bloc.** And this is where the label becomes dangerous.

**Because the members disagree with each other constantly.** India and China are in the same bloc and compete with each other. Brazil and India are often aligned and disagree on agriculture. Saudi Arabia and Iran are counted as Global South and are rivals. Turkey is often excluded and insists it belongs.

**So "the Global South" describes a huge majority of humanity that cannot actually act together.**

**And that is why India is so focused on the phrase.** Because if the Global South could act together, it would change the world order completely. **India's interest is in the phrase working — not in the label being precise.**

## Multipolarity, properly explained

**We touched on this in Phase 2. Now let me say it precisely, because the term is misused constantly.**

**"Polar" means two. "Bipolar" means two.** The Cold War world had two superpowers — the United States and the Soviet Union — and every other country was somewhere in between.

**"Multipolar" means several.** Not many equal powers, but **several centres of power, none of which can decide things alone.**

**And here is the thing that must be understood clearly, because it is the single most common misconception:**

**Multipolarity does NOT mean the United States is no longer the strongest country. It is still, by a wide margin, the largest military power and one of the two or three largest economies.**

**Multipolarity means something narrower and more specific: that there are now enough other powers that the US cannot get its way without agreement.**

**Which is a real change, and it is worth being clear about what it is not.** It is not the end of American power. It is the end of automatic American control.

## The specific fault lines

**Let me give you the actual structure of the current world, because the labels in the news do not make it clear.**

**The first fault line: the US versus China.**

This is the defining rivalry of our time. It is **not purely ideological** — it is economic, technological, military and geographic.

**Where they compete:** technology, especially semiconductors and AI. Military basing in the Indo-Pacific. Trade. And **increasingly, supply chains** — both are trying to reduce their dependence on the other, which is expensive and makes the trade war worse.

**And the uncomfortable part for India.** Phase 2 established that China's rise created genuine danger for India's oil supply. So **India is being courted by both, which means India is contested by both.** That is the double-edged position.

**The second fault line: Russia versus the West.**

This is not a new rivalry. It dates back centuries. What changed in 2022 is that Russia's invasion of Ukraine turned a competition into a war.

**And here is something worth noticing for an Indian reader.** India's position on this has been notably cautious. **India has not condemned Russia's actions as sharply as the West has, and has continued to buy Russian oil.** This is a genuine point of tension between India and the Western countries.

**The third fault line: the Middle East, which is not really a "line" at all.** The Gulf states, Iran, Turkey and Israel are all pursuing their own separate agendas, often contradicting each other. **This is the region where the idea of a bipolar world is least useful**, because there are too many separate players.

**The fourth fault line is where India sits.** India is not really on any of these lines. **India is a separate pole** — large, nuclear, democratic, non-aligned. And that is precisely why both the US and China want it.

## Strategic autonomy — the phrase that explains India's position

**Now the phrase itself, which you will hear constantly, and which has a specific meaning worth getting right.**

**"Strategic autonomy" means the ability to make your own decisions about which partners you deal with, case by case, without being obliged to anyone.**

**And it has two quite different meanings, which get confused constantly.**

**In Europe, "strategic autonomy" usually means one thing: France's determination not to let European policy be decided by the United States.** When Macron says the EU needs strategic autonomy, he means the EU should be able to act without Washington. **It is about independence from America specifically.**

**In India's case, it means something broader: freedom from both sides.** India does not want to be dependent on American permission, and does not want to be dependent on Chinese permission either.

**And this is where the phrase "multi-alignment" comes in.** India has signed defence agreements and strategic partnerships with many countries — the United States, Japan, France, Russia, Israel — without joining any of them.

**So India can buy American aircraft and Russian missiles and French technology, and cooperate with all of them, without owing any of them anything.**

**And this is the strategy that has worked remarkably well since 1949.** In every major crisis India has been able to work with both sides, because it never tied itself to one.

## But is it actually working?

**Now let me be properly sceptical, because this phase has been building toward India as a success story and that deserves testing.**

**The case that it has worked: over eighty years, India has avoided being trapped in anyone else's conflict.** When the Soviet Union collapsed, India's defence suppliers changed overnight but India survived. **When the Cold War ended, India did not have to choose.** And today, when both the US and China want India, India has options that neither of them has.

**The case against: India has paid a real price, and 2020 showed it.**

**In 2020, when the Chinese army attacked at Galwan, India was alone.** There was no alliance, no treaty, no guarantee. **India responded with economic pressure on Chinese companies, and had to travel to Washington to negotiate bilaterally with the United States.**

**That is the reality behind "strategic autonomy": India had to go and ask.** And the asking revealed a gap between India's ambition and its guaranteed protection.

**So the honest summary is this: strategic autonomy has bought India enormous freedom of action, and left it without a defender.** Both of those are true simultaneously, and any account that gives you only one of them is incomplete.

**And there is a further problem India has not fully solved: having no alliances means having no automatic friends.** When India needs technology urgently, it has to negotiate bilaterally with whoever will sell it — which is exactly the position it was in when it needed aircraft spares.

## What this file is not

**"Global South" is not a bloc.** It has no agreed membership, its members disagree constantly, and it cannot reliably act together. Treating it as a bloc is the single most common error in discussion of it.

**Nor is it the same as "poor countries" or "Third World."** Those are different groups with different meanings. India rejects being called "Third World" and insists on "Global South."

**And multipolarity does not mean the United States is weak.** America remains the largest military power and one of the largest economies. It means it can no longer decide everything alone.

**Nor does "no alliances" mean India has no partners.** India has defence agreements with many countries, extensive strategic partnerships, and joint exercises with several. **What India lacks is a promise that someone will fight for it.**

**And "strategic autonomy" does not mean India is neutral.** India takes positions — it has criticised the Russia-Ukraine war, it voted against Israel at the ICJ in 2024, it has taken a consistent position on decolonisation and climate justice. It is independent, not neutral. **There is a real difference, and confusing them leads to wrong conclusions.**
