Because they do. They sign treaties. They form blocs. They promise to come to each other's aid.

India has refused since 1949. This file explains what it gains and what it gives up.

# 3.3 Alliances, Blocs and the Price of Independence

## What an alliance actually is

Let us start precise, because "alliance" gets used loosely.

**An alliance is a promise. Typically it is a promise that if one member is attacked, the others will help.**

**What that means in practice varies enormously**, and the variation matters more than the name.

**There are three quite different things people call alliances:**

**1. A mutual defence treaty.** A legal promise. The clearest example is **Article 5 of the NATO treaty**, which says an attack on one member is an attack on all.

**2. An informal security partnership.** No legal promise, but coordination, joint exercises and intelligence sharing. **The Quad — the United States, Japan, India and Australia — is this.** It has no treaty. It is not an alliance. If one member were attacked, the others would not be obliged to do anything.

**3. An economic bloc.** Countries cooperating on trade rather than security. **The European Union is this.**

**Conflating these three is one of the most common errors in public discussion**, and it produces genuinely wrong conclusions. **When people say "India is in the Quad" or "India is part of the BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa)," they often do not know which of these three they mean.**

## What alliances are actually for

Here is the list, and all four are worth knowing.

**1. Deterrence.** This is the main one. **An alliance exists to make an attack too expensive.** If country A attacks country B, country B's allies respond. So no country wants to attack B in the first place.

**Note how this works: alliances are about preventing wars, not winning them.** NATO has never fought a war to defend a member. It has deterred.

**2. Access.** Being in an alliance gets you into things — military planning, intelligence, procurement, exercises. **Being outside means being outside.**

**3. Legitimacy.** An alliance is a way of saying "this is not just one country's opinion." If the United States says something, it carries more weight when it does not sound alone.

**4. Burden sharing.** Big countries are expensive to defend alone. **An alliance divides the cost.**

## What alliances cost

Now the part that does not get discussed enough.

**An alliance is not free, and it is not just about money.**

**First: you give up some independence.** This is the real cost, and nobody states it clearly. **If you are in an alliance, you cannot entirely go your own way.** You have to consider your allies in almost every decision.

**The best example of how expensive this can be: Greece and Turkey.** Both are NATO members. For decades they had a serious dispute over Cyprus. **But because they were both in NATO, neither could simply act against the other.** The alliance held them in a strait-jacket for years.

**Second: you can be dragged in.** The central fear of any small ally. **If you are allied to a country that gets into a fight, you may be pulled in too — and you may not agree with why they started it.**

**Third: you inherit your ally's enemies.** This is the most important one and it is not widely understood. **If you are allied to the United States, then to a large part of the world you are an ally of the United States.** That means you get the benefits, and you get the hostility too.

**Fourth: dependency.** The more you rely on one partner, the more costly it becomes to leave them, and the more your own decisions get interpreted as that partner's decisions.

## NATO — the case that made alliances respectable

**NATO was founded in 1949** and is still running, so let us use it as the main case.

**Why was it created?** In 1945 the Soviet Union controlled most of Eastern Europe. **And there was a genuine fear, particularly in Britain and France, that the Soviet Union would continue westwards.**

**Note what that fear was actually about.** It was not about ideology in the abstract. It was about **security** — and specifically about a recently power-hungry Soviet Union that had taken a million prisoners in eastern Europe.

**How does NATO work?** Any country can formally request to join, and then every other member must consent. **That rule is the whole reason NATO works.**

Because it means **no country can join NATO against the wishes of another.** Not Germany in 1955, not France in 1966. **Nobody could be dragged in against the alliance's consensus.**

**And there is one more crucial rule: there is no mechanism to leave.** The treaty does not allow a member to withdraw. **That has to be agreed by consensus.** It is a remarkable design, and it explains why NATO has stayed together despite Britain voting for leaving in 2016.

**But NATO also shows the cost.** For most of its life, several European members were close to neutral because they did not want to be in an alliance that included the United States.

## The Cold War blocs

**The Soviet side** had its own alliance. The **Warsaw Pact** was formed in 1955, named after the Polish capital, and included most of Eastern Europe.

**And here is the crucial historical fact, and it is the reason this matters:** **the Warsaw Pact was used, in 1956 and again in 1968, to crush reform movements inside its own members.** Hungary in 1956, Czechoslovakia in 1968.

**Which tells you something about the difference between the two alliances.** NATO members have left or threatened to leave — Greece, France, Britain voted for Brexit. **Warsaw Pact members were not allowed to leave.** That asymmetry is one of the reasons the Cold War ended the way it did.

## The Non-Aligned Movement — and India's version of it

**Now the Indian part, and it is the most interesting case of all.**

**The Non-Aligned Movement was founded in 1961**, and its founding leaders were **Jawaharlal Nehru of India, Josip Broz Tito of Yugoslavia, and Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt.**

**What was the idea?** That countries should not be forced to choose a side in the Cold War. **That they should be free to trade with everyone, and ally with whoever they liked, case by case.**

**And this is the crucial thing to understand about India's approach: India never actually joined the Non-Aligned Movement as a formal bloc member in the way the Soviet Union led the Warsaw Pact.**

**Instead India invented its own thing**, which you saw in Phase 2: **India stayed out of military blocs entirely while deepening relations with individual countries case by case.**

**India signed a defence treaty with the United States in 1954 while also keeping ties with the Soviet Union.** **India bought equipment from both sides.** **India attended non-aligned conferences while also having a strategic partnership with Washington decades later.**

**This is not a contradiction. It is the strategy, and it is what the last two files have been explaining.** The strategy has a name, and you will meet it in file 3.5: **strategic autonomy.**

**And the value of it has been enormous.** **Because in every crisis of the last forty years, India has been able to work with both sides.** When the Soviet Union collapsed, India was not left with a dead patron. **When the US-China competition tightened, India was able to deal with both.**

## But there is a real cost, and India pays it

**Now let me be honest, because this file has been fairly favourable to India's position so far, and that is misleading.**

**India's independence has cost it something specific, and everyone in India's strategic establishment knows exactly what it is.**

**Cost 1: no one has to come to India's defence.** **This is the biggest one and almost nobody outside India realises it.** There is no mutual defence treaty between India and any major power. **There is no guarantee. If China attacked India tomorrow, India would be on its own.**

**Compare this to Japan or South Korea. They have explicit security guarantees from the United States.** **India does not, and this is deliberate.**

**Cost 2: India does not get the access allies get.** Allies get consulted on the design of weapons, they get advance notice of plans, they get priority access to technology. **India is a partner, not an ally — and partners get less.**

**Cost 3: India pays a price for being close to China.** **When India wants to do something China dislikes, it now has to weigh Chinese response.** That is a real constraint on Indian foreign policy that Americans do not face.

**Cost 4: India carries the moral discomfort without the mutual benefit.** **India wants to be the leader of the "Global South" and criticise the international order — but it has no military guarantee backing that position.**

**And the strategic problem underneath all of this is this: if a major power decided it did not want India to succeed, there is nobody India could turn to.** **That is what strategic autonomy buys, and it is also what it costs.**

## What this file is not

**"India is not in any alliance" is not the same as "India has no alliances."** India has defence agreements with many countries, joint exercises, and a strategic partnership with the United States. **What India does not have is a promise that someone else will fight for it.** Those are different things and the difference matters enormously.

**Nor is being in an alliance the only way to be secure.** Some countries are safer without one, particularly where no one is willing to help anyway. **But it is a real trade, not a free choice.**

**Nor is NATO a "good alliance" and the Warsaw Pact a "bad one."** The Warsaw Pact's use against Hungary and Czechoslovakia was a real and serious abuse. **But alliances generally reflect the behaviour of the great power leading them, and it is worth asking what America would have allowed inside NATO.**

**And "multi-alignment" is not free.** It buys options and flexibility, and it costs depth. **A country that will not commit gets served second.**
