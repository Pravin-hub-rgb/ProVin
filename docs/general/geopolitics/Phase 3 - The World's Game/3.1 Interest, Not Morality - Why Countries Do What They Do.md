# 3.1 Interest, Not Morality - Why Countries Do What They Do

## The most famous sentence in geopolitics

There is a quote you will meet everywhere in this subject. It goes something like this:

> **"Nations have no permanent friends and no permanent enemies, only permanent interests."**

It is usually attributed to **Lord Palmerston**, the British Prime Minister who served in the 1860s.

**Let me be honest about the quote before we use it.** Nobody is entirely certain he said exactly that. The sentiment is genuinely his, and it became famous partly through a French diplomat who reported something close to it. But treat it as a **summary of a way of thinking**, not a verified quotation.

**The idea is what matters, and the idea is this: countries are like individuals, but they are not individuals.** They do not have friends in the way a person does. **What they have is interests, and interests change far faster than friendships would.**

Let me show you why that is true, using real examples where the countries involved did not expect to end up where they did.

## Example 1 — Britain and Germany were allies, then enemies

This is the clearest case, and it happened twice within one generation.

**In 1914, Britain and Germany went to war.** But look at *why*.

**The reason Britain entered the war in 1914 was not primarily because of Germany.** It was because Britain had an alliance with France, and France had been attacked by Germany over Alsace-Lorraine. **Britain's interest in 1914 was to support its ally against a threat.**

**Now go back to 1904.** In that year, Britain and France signed the **Entente Cordiale** — an agreement that ended a centuries-long series of wars between the two countries.

**So Britain went to war in 1914 against Germany because of an agreement it had signed just ten years earlier, which itself came out of colonial rivalry over Egypt.**

Now take the other half. **Britain and Germany were allies in the First World War** — against Russia. And **Britain and the Soviet Union were allies in the Second World War** — against Germany.

**Read that again. Britain fought alongside the Soviet Union in 1941 to 1945. Twenty years earlier it had gone to war to stop Russia. And thirty years later, the Cold War made them the defining enemies of the world.**

**Same two countries. Allies, then enemies, then allies, then enemies. All within fifty years.**

## Example 2 — The United States and the Soviet Union

**The United States and the Soviet Union fought on the same side in the Second World War.** Millions of American and Soviet soldiers died together, defeating Nazi Germany.

**Then within five years of victory, they were the two great enemies of the world, and the Cold War began.**

**Why?** Because the thing they had in common — opposition to Nazi Germany — was the only thing they had in common. **Once that common enemy was gone, the underlying competition reappeared.** And that competition had never gone away; it had simply been suspended.

**This is the pattern to watch for.** Countries that fight together do not become friends. **They discover how much they dislike each other once the shared enemy is removed.**

## Example 3 — India's own relationships

Now the most useful set of examples, because these are yours.

**India and the United States.** Britain signed a defence treaty with India in 1954 and maintained military ties for decades. After 2001, and especially after 2005, the relationship became much closer — defence cooperation, trade, and eventually a description of India as a "strategic partner."

**What changed? Not America's view of Indian culture. America's need for a counterweight to China.**

**India and China.** In the early 1990s, India and China were beginning to normalise relations. The relationship was cautious but improving. India even made China its "priority partner" — meaning China was treated as a priority country for engagement.

**Then two things happened:** China's economic rise made it confident, and the border situation produced repeated standoffs. **And the relationship deteriorated to the point where the 2020 Galwan clash killed soldiers on both sides.**

**India and Russia.** India was close to the Soviet Union from the 1950s through the 1980s — the Soviet Union was India's main supplier of military equipment. **When the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, India's entire defence supply chain changed at once.**

**India and the United States after 2001.** After the 9/11 attacks, India's relationship with the United States improved dramatically. **Because suddenly America needed help in the region, and India was the obvious partner.**

**Look at that pattern.** India went from close to the USSR, to aligning with the US, to being courted by the US. **And every single shift was driven by what each side needed at that moment.**

## But wait — if it is all interest, why do any alliances last?

This is the right question, and if you skip it you have misunderstood this file.

**Because "interest" is not the same as "convenience." Some interests are deep and structural, and they last for a very long time.**

**NATO is the best example.** Founded in 1949, and still there. **Why does it last?** Because for most of that time, Europe faced a large hostile power next door. That threat is structural. It does not change because a government changed.

**And here is the deeper point.** Alliances are not usually kept together by affection. They are kept together by **shared threat and repeated, costly cooperation.** When you have trained with the same army for sixty years and share intelligence, unwinding that relationship has a cost. **So even an alliance of countries with no genuine affection can persist for decades.**

**And there is a much more important point, which is this: some things genuinely do not change.**

**India and Pakistan have been rivals since 1947.** Seventy-odd years of wars, nuclear tests, and distrust. **That does not look like interest doing what interests do — interests usually find a way to coexist.**

**What this tells you is that some conflicts are structural rather than transactional.** India and Pakistan are not enemies because of a disagreement that could be negotiated away. **They are rivals because they are two large nuclear-armed states sharing a contested border, created by a hurried partition.** That is not a bargaining position. It is a situation.

**So the honest summary is: interest explains most of what happens, and you should assume it explains a lot more than you would like.** But some relationships are held together by genuine affection, and some enmities are structural rather than negotiable. Phase 5 will cover the difference.

## The most useful application of this idea

Here is what you actually do with this file.

**When you read a headline about countries A and B, you ask one question: "What does each of them get out of this?"**

**And this works even for the cases that seem most outrageous.** **In 2020, China and India fought at Galwan Valley.** That seems insane — two large economies with nuclear weapons and huge trade ties, fighting over a valley neither could farm or settle.

**But ask the interest question and it starts to look less insane.** For China, the road through Aksai Chin connects its landlocked west to its economy. For India, letting China control the high ground there sets a precedent along the whole border. **Neither of those is about land. Both are about who gets to decide where the line is.**

**When you can say what each side gets, you have explained most of the behaviour.** And when you cannot, be suspicious — because that usually means you are missing a piece of information, not that there is no logic in it.

## What this file is not

**"Countries have no permanent friends" is not an excuse for bad behaviour.** Understanding that a country acted out of interest tells you what to expect next time. It does not make the action acceptable, and it certainly does not mean you have to support it.

**Nor does it mean countries are dishonest about their motives.** Governments often genuinely believe their stated reasons. **The statement "we did it for security" is usually true, even when the real driver was domestic politics, jobs, or pride.** Interests and stated reasons coexist comfortably.

**Nor is "interest" a justification for every outcome.** Some things are genuinely indefensible, and understanding why someone did something has never made it right.

**The most important limitation: interest explains behaviour, but it does not predict it.** Knowing that India will prioritise oil supply tells you a great deal about what India will do about a shipping lane. It tells you almost nothing about what India will do about a human rights issue that costs nothing. **Phase 5 deals with this properly.**

## Bridge to the next file

You now have the central idea of Phase 3: countries act on interest, and interests explain most of what happens between them.

**But if interest is what drives countries, the obvious question is what they are actually competing for.** And this is where the second question comes in.

The last file mentioned the four dials from Phase 0: land, people, resources, position. **Those are what a country has.** They are not the same as what a country can *do*.

This is a distinction most people never make, and it leads to the second big idea of this phase: **there is no single definition of power.** There are several, they behave differently, and a country strong in one can be helpless in another.

That is `3.2 What Power Actually Is - Hard, Soft and Economic`.
