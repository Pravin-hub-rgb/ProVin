# 3.5 Quiz - The World's Game
## MCQ

Q: The idea behind "nations have no permanent friends, only permanent interests" is that:
A. Countries are dishonest about their real reasons
B. Countries form relationships by calculation, so they change when the calculation changes
C. International law does not exist
D. Economic cooperation is more important than security
> B. Countries are not individuals. Individuals form personal, emotional friendships; countries form calculated ones. So when the advantage changes, the relationship changes with it — which is why Britain fought beside the Soviet Union in 1945 having fought it in 1914.

---

Q: Why did Britain go to war in 1914, and what does that show?
A. Because Britain hated Germany
B. Because it was allied to France, which had been attacked over Alsace-Lorraine
C. Because of a desire for German territory
D. Because France had colonies
> B. Britain's interest was to support an ally against a threat, and that alliance came from the Entente Cordiale of 1904, which itself came from colonial rivalry over Egypt. The whole chain traced back to imperial competition rather than to any single grievance.

---

Q: Why did the United States and the Soviet Union fall out so soon after winning the Second World War together?
A. Because of a personal quarrel between Roosevelt and Stalin
B. Because opposition to Germany was the only thing they had in common
C. Because of the atomic bomb
D. Because Britain demanded it
> B. Once the shared enemy was removed, the underlying competition reappeared. Countries that fight together do not become friends; they discover how much they disliked each other once the common enemy is gone.

---

Q: What were the three meanings people intend by the word "alliance"?
A. Military, economic and cultural
B. A mutual defence treaty, an informal security partnership, and an economic bloc
C. NATO, the UN and the EU
D. Strong, weak and medium alliances
> B. A legal mutual defence promise, an informal partnership with no obligation, and economic cooperation are three genuinely different things. Conflating them produces wrong conclusions, because the obligations are entirely different.

---

Q: What is the most important purpose of an alliance?
A. To win wars
B. To deter wars by making attack too expensive
C. To trade more
D. To gain prestige
> B. Alliances are about preventing wars, not fighting them. NATO has never fought a war to defend a member; it has deterred. That is the primary function, and it is why allies do not need to like each other.

---

Q: What is the biggest thing an alliance costs you?
A. Money
B. Independence — you can no longer entirely go your own way
C. Territory
D. Prestige
> B. Greece and Turkey is the clearest example: a serious dispute over Cyprus that neither could act on because both were in NATO. Smaller members can also be dragged into a conflict they did not choose, and they inherit their ally's enemies.

---

Q: Why did NATO survive for seventy years while the Warsaw Pact collapsed?
A. NATO was more democratic
B. NATO required unanimous consent to admit members and had no mechanism for leaving, while Warsaw Pact members were forced to stay
C. NATO was richer
D. Warsaw Pact countries were communist
> B. NATO required every member's consent to admit a new one, and withdrawal required unanimity. The Warsaw Pact used force against Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968, so members were not free to leave. NATO members, by contrast, have left or threatened to leave — Greece, France, Britain voted for Brexit.

---

Q: Why did India never formally join the Non-Aligned Movement the way the Soviet Union led the Warsaw Pact?
A. India rejected the idea of non-alignment
B. India invented something different — staying out of military blocs while deepening ties with individual countries case by case
C. India was not invited
D. India was a Warsaw Pact member
> B. India signed a defence treaty with the United States in 1954 while keeping ties with the Soviet Union, and bought equipment from both. That is not a contradiction — it is the strategy that gives India freedom of action, and it is what this phase has been explaining.

---

Q: What is soft power?
A. Deception and propaganda
B. Getting what you want by making others want to give it
C. Economic sanctions
D. Military deterrence
> B. Coined by Joseph Nye in 1990. It is attraction rather than coercion, and it is not propaganda — propaganda is deception, soft power is genuine attractiveness. It works slowly and cannot override hostility.

---

Q: What is "sharp power," and who uses it?
A. A new form of nuclear deterrence, used by India
B. Economic coercion, cyber attacks and information operations without declaring war — used mainly by China and Russia
C. Soft power directed at allies
D. The power of small countries
> B. Coined by Wael Ghonim. It exists as a category because traditional labels did not fit — China's methods are neither classic soft power nor classic hard power.

---

Q: Why is economic power often more effective than hard power?
A. It is cheaper
B. It is continuous, reversible, and does not kill anyone
C. It is more visible
D. It is faster
> B. Economic power operates by changing what is possible rather than what is visible — you do not notice sanctions working until someone cannot travel or cannot buy spare parts. Hard power is decisive but costly and irreversible.

---

Q: What was the Indian experience with Iranian oil, and what did it demonstrate?
A. India refused to buy Iranian oil throughout, showing independence
B. India reduced purchases under threat of consequences against Indian banks, showing the power of financial pressure
C. India bought as much Iranian oil as possible, showing defiance
D. India had no trade with Iran
> B. Because most large banks have US exposure, losing that access is close to fatal for international trade, so the threat works implicitly. India reduced purchases not because it agreed with US Iran policy, but because it calculated the cost of defiance was too high.

---

Q: Why can the US impose secondary sanctions on India?
A. Because India agreed to them
B. Because US law applies to US persons, and most large banks have US exposure
C. Because the UN ordered it
D. Because of the WTO
> B. The sanctions are legally domestic, but the enforcement is global because the banking system depends on US access. Banks have to comply because the alternative is losing the US financial system entirely.

---

Q: The Cuban embargo is important as an example because it shows:
A. Sanctions can work if applied consistently
B. Sanctions can fail completely at their stated objective while harming ordinary people severely
C. Cuba was the strongest country in Latin America
D. The UN can force regime change
> B. The embargo began in 1960 and lasted over fifty years. It did not change Cuba's government, it produced enormous hardship for ordinary Cubans, and US policy has since been largely reversed. It is now accepted almost universally as a failure.

---

Q: Why is the Iraq sanctions case considered difficult?
A. The sanctions were too harsh
B. The stated objective was not achieved through sanctions at all — the government fell to a military invasion
C. Iraq never wanted sanctions lifted
D. The oil-for-food scheme was successful
> B. Comprehensive sanctions continued for over a decade and were never lifted through negotiation. Iraq's government was removed by invasion in 2003 — a war, not sanctions — which achieved in months what twenty years of sanctions had not.

---

Q: What is "tariff theatre"?
A. Using tariffs as domestic politics rather than strategy
B. A theatre built near border crossings
C. Negotiating tariffs secretly
D. A type of trade agreement
> B. No — tariffs are sometimes used to win domestic votes rather than to achieve a strategic goal. A government taxes steel, domestic producers make money, jobs appear, and the government gets votes. It is visible, cheap and feels like action.

---

Q: How does trade create leverage?
A. By making countries dependent on each other
B. By reducing tariffs
C. By making goods cheaper
D. By creating currency unions
> A. If country A depends on B to buy its goods, B can hurt A economically whenever it wants. And the dependence is far stronger when a country cannot produce the thing itself — which is why countries lacking resources have far less room to manoeuvre.

---

Q: What happens to an Indian company designated by US sanctions?
A. Nothing, unless it is large
B. International banks avoid dealing with it, shutting it out of global finance disproportionately
C. It is automatically frozen
D. It is banned from trading with India
> B. Because the sanctions are designed to cut off access to the US financial system, and most global banks value that access. For a small or mid-sized Indian firm the effect can be close to fatal, regardless of the size of the target.

---

Q: What does "Global South" roughly mean?
A. The countries in the southern hemisphere
B. Countries that were colonised by European powers and are now developing
C. The poorest fifty countries
D. Countries that are not in NATO
> B. Most of Asia, Africa and Latin America. It is often confused with "poor countries" or "Third World", which are different groups with different meanings, and India specifically rejects "Third World".

---

Q: Why does India insist it belongs to the Global South?
A. Because it is a large economy
B. Because a very large number of its people still live on low incomes, and per-person income is the honest measure
C. Because it is in the southern hemisphere
D. Because it is not in the UN
> B. Hundreds of millions of Indians remain poor despite India's economic size. India argues that economic size is not the same as development when the per-person numbers are still low. This argument has won India recognition as the leading voice of the Global South.

---

Q: Why is "Global South" not a bloc?
A. Because its members are too poor
B. Because there is no agreed membership and its members disagree with each other constantly
C. Because it is led by India
D. Because it is only an economic term
> B. India and China compete within it, Saudi Arabia and Iran are rivals within it, Turkey argues it belongs and others disagree. It describes a huge majority of humanity that cannot reliably act together — which is exactly why the label is contested.

---

Q: What does "multipolar" mean, and what does it NOT mean?
A. That power is distributed among several centres; it does NOT mean the United States is no longer strong
B. That several countries have nuclear weapons
C. That the world has divided into regions
D. That no single country is ever dominant
> A. Multipolarity means there are enough other powers that the US cannot get its way without agreement. It is the end of automatic control, not the end of American power — America remains the largest military power by a wide margin.

---

Q: What is India's "strategic autonomy"?
A. Refusing to trade with any major power
B. The ability to choose partners case by case, free from both American and Chinese pressure
C. Being militarily independent of everyone
D. Refusing to join the Non-Aligned Movement
> B. In Europe, "strategic autonomy" usually means independence from the United States specifically. In India it means freedom from both sides — not wanting to depend on American permission, and not wanting to depend on Chinese permission either.

---

Q: How can India be "multi-aligned" while joining no alliances?
A. The terms mean the same thing
B. Multi-alignment means partnerships with many countries, which create no obligation; alliances create obligations
C. India changed its policy in 2020
D. Multi-alignment is a military strategy
> B. India has defence agreements and strategic partnerships with the US, Japan, France, Russia and Israel without joining any of them. Alliances obligate you; partnerships do not. India can buy American aircraft, Russian missiles and French technology and owe none of them anything.

---

Q: What was the real price of India's strategic autonomy, revealed in 2020?
A. India lost territory
B. When China attacked at Galwan, India was alone and had to go to Washington to negotiate bilaterally
C. India lost a UN seat
D. India's trade collapsed
> B. There was no alliance, no treaty and no guarantee. India responded with economic pressure and bilateral negotiation, which revealed the gap between its ambition and its guaranteed protection. Strategic autonomy buys freedom of action and leaves India without a defender — both true simultaneously.

---

Q: Why is India "independent" rather than "neutral"?
A. They mean the same thing
B. India takes clear positions — on Ukraine, on Israel at the ICJ, on decolonisation — but decides them itself
C. India has no allies
D. Neutral means military alliances
> B. Independent means choosing your own position; neutral means having no position. India has criticised the Russia-Ukraine war, voted against Israel at the ICJ in 2024, and taken consistent positions on climate and decolonisation. It just does not adopt someone else's position.

---

Q: Why is economic power harder to see than military power?
A. Because it is illegal
B. Because it changes what is possible rather than what is visible, so it is only noticed when it is used
C. Because it is small
D. Because it is temporary
> B. Nobody notices sanctions working while they work. You notice them when a foreign minister cannot travel or a country cannot buy aircraft spare parts. Military power is visible the moment it is used; economic power is invisible until it denies something.

---

## TrueFalse

Q: Understanding that a country acted out of interest means you support what it did.
- True
- False
> False. Understanding why something happened tells you what to expect next time; it does not make it acceptable. You are allowed to know why a country did something and still think it was wrong.

---

Q: The quote "nations have no permanent friends" is a verified exact quotation by Lord Palmerston.
- True
- False
> False. The sentiment is genuinely his and the phrase is widely attributed to him, but nobody is certain of the exact wording. It is best treated as a summary of a way of thinking rather than a verified quotation.

---

Q: Governments usually state interests honestly, even when those differ from their stated reasons.
- True
- False
> True. A government saying "we did it for security" is usually sincere, even where domestic politics, jobs or pride were also involved. Interests and stated reasons coexist comfortably — the two are not mutually exclusive.

---

Q: The India-Pakistan rivalry is explained by "interest" in the same way as the US-Soviet rivalry was.
- True
- False
> False. It is structural rather than transactional. It does not arise from a bargaining position that could be traded away; it arises from two nuclear-armed states sharing a border created by a hurried partition. That is a situation, not a negotiation.

---

Q: Soft power means persuading people through deception.
- True
- False
> False. That is propaganda. Soft power is genuine attraction — people choosing to associate with you because they want to. The distinction matters, because propaganda relies on deception working and soft power does not.

---

Q: Soft power can override a country's hostility toward you.
- True
- False
> False. Soft power works in one direction only. If a country dislikes you, your soft power will not overcome that — and being associated with you may damage your standing further.

---

Q: The United States imposed the most significant UN sanction, on Israel in 1978.
- True
- False
> True. It remains one of the most significant actions in UN history, and Israel has never accepted it. It is a rare case of a permanent member sanctioning an ally.

---

Q: Sanctions are designed to hurt governments, not civilians.
- True
- False
> False. Whatever the intention, the burden usually falls on civilians. The Cuban embargo and the Iraq sanctions both harmed ordinary people far more than the leadership they targeted. This is the central human rights criticism of sanctions.

---

Q: Secondary sanctions are imposed by one country on another without international agreement.
- True
- False
> False. Those are unilateral sanctions. Secondary sanctions punish a country for doing business with an already-sanctioned country — which is how US pressure reached Indian firms dealing with Iran.

---

Q: Tariffs always achieve the strategic result they are intended to achieve.
- True
- False
> False. They reliably cause pain — the US-China trade war hurt American consumers and importers — but they often achieve little beyond that. Many tariffs are used as domestic politics rather than strategy.

---

Q: Most of the world's trade is now conducted through the WTO's appellate mechanism.
- True
- False
> False. The appellate body has been unable to function for years because appointments have been blocked, which has removed the WTO's main enforcement tool. Countries increasingly use unilateral measures because there is no effective forum to challenge them.

---

Q: A country with a large army but no economic leverage can still buy whatever it needs.
- True
- False
> False. Economic power and hard power are not substitutes. A country with enormous financial leverage can bankrupt one with a much larger army. Military strength does not substitute for the ability to trade.

---

Q: "Global South" is a clearly defined group with agreed membership.
- True
- False
> False. There is no agreed membership. Some exclude China and Russia as great powers, some exclude India as a large economy. The membership dispute is not academic — it affects what countries actually get in negotiations.

---

Q: The Global South acts as a single voting bloc in international negotiations.
- True
- False
> False. Its members disagree constantly — India and China compete within it, Saudi Arabia and Iran are rivals within it. It describes a majority of humanity that cannot reliably act together.

---

Q: Multipolarity means the United States is no longer a global power.
- True
- False
> False. America remains by far the largest military power and one of the largest economies. Multipolarity means it can no longer decide things automatically — it is the end of automatic control, not of American power.

---

Q: India's "strategic autonomy" means the same thing as the European version.
- True
- False
> False. In Europe it usually means independence from the United States specifically — France's position within the EU. In India it means freedom from both sides, avoiding dependence on American permission and Chinese permission equally.

---

Q: The US-China rivalry is purely ideological.
- True
- False
> False. It is economic, technological, military and geographic — over semiconductors and AI, military basing in the Indo-Pacific, trade, and increasingly supply chains. Reducing it to ideology misses most of what drives it.

---

Q: India is a neutral country.
- True
- False
> False. India is independent, not neutral. It takes clear positions — on the Russia-Ukraine war, on Israel at the ICJ in 2024, on decolonisation and climate justice. It simply decides its own positions rather than adopting someone else's.

---

Q: Having no alliances means India has no partners.
- True
- False
> False. India has defence agreements and strategic partnerships with many countries, plus joint exercises with several. What it lacks is a promise that someone will fight for it. Partnerships give access; alliances give obligation. India has the first and not the second.

## ShortAnswer

Q: A friend says "Country X is a warmongering bully, so nothing can justify opposing it." How would you use the interest framework honestly?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** I would say the interest framework helps me understand and does not help me approve. Asking what each side gets — what Country X gains from the aggression, and what it would lose from a different outcome — genuinely improves my understanding of the situation. But understanding is not the same as justification. I can hold two things at once: that the action is serious power politics, and that it is also something I believe is wrong. If the framework only let me do the second, it would not be a framework but a way of excusing behaviour.

</details>

---

Q: Why does India insist on belonging to the Global South, and why does it matter?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** India argues that hundreds of millions of its people still live on low incomes, so per-person income — not total economic size — is the honest measure of development. It matters because the Global South is the majority of humanity, and if those countries could act together they would reshape the international order. India's interest is in that grouping being credible, which is why it spends diplomatic capital on it and why it objects to being classed as a developed country.

</details>

---

Q: Explain why "no alliances" has helped India enormously and hurt it at the same time. Use specific examples from the course.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** It has helped by giving India freedom of action. It signed a defence treaty with the US in 1954 while keeping Soviet ties, and when the USSR collapsed in 1991 it simply switched suppliers. It could work with both sides in the Cold War, and today it can deal with the US and China simultaneously, which is rare. It has hurt because there is no one obliged to defend India. In 2020, when China attacked at Galwan, India was alone and had to go to Washington to negotiate bilaterally. Strategic autonomy buys options and leaves India without a guarantee — both are true at once.

</details>

---

Q: How would you use the four kinds of power to explain why sanctions on Cuba failed but sanctions on South Africa contributed to change?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Both were economic power, but the political conditions differed. Cuba's government faced no domestic movement capable of using the economic pressure to force change, so the pressure only hurt ordinary Cubans. In South Africa there was a large internal movement — the anti-apartheid struggle — that economic pressure could reinforce by making the government's position untenable at home. This shows the general point: sanctions work when they give internal opposition something to use, and fail when they only hurt civilians.

</details>

---

Q: Why is India's position on Russian oil a good test case for economic power?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Because it separates power from agreement. India did not agree with US policy on Iran, and it had its own view of the nuclear agreement, but it still reduced purchases. It did so because most large international banks have US exposure, and losing that access is close to fatal for anyone who wants to trade globally. So the threat did not need to be explicit — it worked through the structure of the financial system. It shows that power over trade operates by changing what is possible rather than by persuasion.

</details>

---

Q: Explain the difference between saying a country acted on "interest" and saying it acted on "ideology," using the 1991 reforms as an example.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

> **Sample Answer:** Ideology would say India became free-market because it came to believe in capitalism. Interest says India reformed because it had about two weeks of foreign reserves and could not pay for oil it imports at 85 percent. Manmohan Singh deliberately framed the reforms as economic necessity rather than belief. The distinction matters because it predicts behaviour: a country acting on interest will reverse course when the interest changes, whereas one acting on ideology is more consistent regardless of circumstances.

</details>
