# 1.5 Legal & Regulatory Framework (SEBI & Global Guidelines)

In the previous file you got the full flow — code talks to API, broker reaches the exchange. Meaning technically you can now build and run an algo. But wait — can you build any algo and put it in the market? No. There are rules, and knowing these rules is just as important as coding. If you want to become an algo trader in the future, you must understand the regulatory landscape first — otherwise there will be problems later.

## What SEBI Regulates

In India, algo trading is regulated by **SEBI** (Securities and Exchange Board of India). Its primary goal is to establish a strong regulatory framework for algos in the stock market.

Let's start with SEBI's definition, because this is what decides whether your system is an "algo" or not:

> **"Algorithmic trading = any order generated using automated execution logic."**

Meaning if any order is generated through automatic execution logic, SEBI counts it as an ALGO. Your simple moving average crossover bot and Jane Street's HFT system — both fall under this same definition.

This framework has 3 core objectives:

1. **Protect investors**
2. **Ensure market integrity**
3. **Prevent unfair trading practices**

And what's the actual purpose of regulation? There are unregulated strategies running in the market — like strategies running on bid-ask spread, or the work of high-frequency firms. Regulating these HFT (High Frequency Trading) players is SEBI's main purpose. With the rise of API-based trading, oversight has become stricter — focus is on transparency, risk control, and regulated strategies.

So far SEBI has roughly 2 circulars regarding algo trading. Read both and you'll get an idea of which regulatory framework applies to your ALGO if you want to work in the market.

## Algo Classification — White Box vs Black Box

SEBI has classified ALGOs into two broad categories:

**White Box ALGO:** The user can see and understand the logic. Transparency exists — rules are visible. Simple definition: **white box = the ALGO that YOU build yourself.**

**Black Box ALGO:** The rules and logic are kept hidden from you. It's something proprietary. Simple definition: **black box = the ALGO that others sell, whose logic you can't see.**

Now compare both point by point:

**Logic:** In white box it's transparent — you can see and modify it. In black box it's hidden/proprietary — you can't see it.

**Retail use:** White box algo is used by you through the broker API. Black box comes in commercial/subscription models — like many websites that sell ready-made algos on subscription; all of those fall under black box category.

**RA License (Research Analyst):** Not required for white box. Mandatory for black box — if you want to build a black box algo that you can sell to someone, you must get SEBI's Research Analyst license.

**Documentation:** Lighter requirement for white box. For black box, keeping a paper of the logic is mandated — and that paper must be submitted to SEBI (as part of compliance with the research analyst license).

**Exchange approval:** Not required for white box. Mandatory for black box.

**Marketing:** Marketing must be fair for white box. For black box, misleading performance claims are absolutely not allowed — no false/misleading claims of any kind.

Let's nail it with a real example. Imagine you want to build a website like TradeTrun — where you sell your algo to others. Then you'd need: (1) Research Analyst license, (2) you'd have to mandate keeping papers of the logic, (3) you'd have to write detailed documentation of the logic you sell to users, (4) you'd have to get exchange approval.

But if you build an algo for yourself — white box — you don't need to worry about any of this. Just connect to the broker API and run it.

## Order Frequency Threshold — The 10 Orders Per Second Rule

White/black box is one classification. The other classification is by order frequency. And SEBI has clearly set this threshold:

> **10 orders per second**

This number categorizes participants, and it's what decides how much compliance is needed:

```
                ┌──────────────────────────┐
                │  Order Frequency Check   │
                └────────────┬─────────────┘
          ┌──────────────────┴──────────────────┐
          ▼                                     ▼
< 10 orders per second                > 10 orders per second
     = LFT                                  = HFT
(Low Frequency Trading)            (High Frequency Trading)
```

### LFT — Low Frequency Trading

Your normal strategies fall here. Indicator-based strategies, rule-based strategies, moving average crossover, Bollinger Bands strategy, Donchian Channel breakout — all of these are LFT, because in none of them do you place 10 orders in 1 second.

LFT requirements:

- Exchange approval: **not required**
- Broker approval: **required** — broker can tag you
- SEBI registration: **not required**
- RA license: **not required**
- Audit trail: **must maintain**
- Client information: **necessary**

See? For a retail algo trader this is quite light. Just maintain audit trail and client info.

### HFT — High Frequency Trading

Exceed 10 orders per second? Then requirements get very strict:

1. The ALGO must be registered + approved by NSE/BSE
2. SEBI registration required
3. System audit mandatory — SEBI's registered auditor will audit you
4. Regulatory scrutiny — SEBI and exchanges conduct inspections and stress tests to ensure market resilience and fairness
5. SEBI/NSE can scrutinize at any time

HFT examples: bid-ask trading where you place orders above the threshold at high speed in a single second, and the work of HFT firms like Jane Street.

You're a beginner now, so your strategies will be LFT — don't stress. But you should know the boundary, because when your strategy gets fast, this line gets crossed.

## Compliance Pathway — Whenever You Build a New Algo

Whenever you create an ALGO in the future — for yourself or for a client — follow this 3-step pathway:

```
STEP 1: Define Your ALGO
        │
        ├── White Box?  → Transparent logic (what you built)
        └── Black Box?  → Proprietary logic (sold by others)
        │
        ▼
STEP 2: Check Your Order Frequency
        │
        ├── Low frequency?   → < 10 orders per second
        └── High frequency?  → > 10 orders per second
        │
        ▼
STEP 3: Follow That Category's Compliances
        │
        ├── Low frequency  → LFT compliances
        └── High frequency → HFT compliances
```

Step by step understanding:

1. First check — is my ALGO white box or black box?
2. Then check — is my frequency above 10 orders/sec or below?
3. If low frequency, look at the LFT compliances list
4. If high frequency, look at the HFT compliances list
5. This decides what you can and can't do in the regulatory framework

## Nutshell

Remember SEBI's definition: **any order generated through automated execution logic = algo trading.** Classify your algo on two axes — **White Box / Black Box** (whether the logic is visible or not) × **LFT / HFT** (below or above 10 orders/sec) — and follow that category's compliances. For retail, white box + LFT is the normal path.

## What It Is NOT

- ❌ **Not every algo needs SEBI registration.** Heavy requirements are only for black box (those who sell) and HFT (>10 orders/sec). You can run your white box LFT algo without registration.
- ❌ **White box doesn't mean a cheap/weak algo.** White box only means the logic is transparent, which you built yourself. Even a Jane Street level strategy can be white box if the logic is visible.
- ❌ **Regulation isn't only for big players.** Maintaining an audit trail and providing client information — these apply to retail LFT traders too.
