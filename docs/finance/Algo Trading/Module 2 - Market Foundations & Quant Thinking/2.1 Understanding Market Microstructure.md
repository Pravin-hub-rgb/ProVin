# 2.1 Understanding Market Microstructure

In Module 1 we saw where your order goes — from code to API, API to broker, broker to exchange. Now let's go one level deeper. When you press the button (place an order), what all happens between that order getting executed and the PnL reaching you? This whole system is called **market microstructure** — and understanding it is a must for an algo trader. You can also call it the **engine of the market** — just as an engine takes the car from point A to point B, microstructure is all the machinery inside the market.

This file covers 4 parts:

1. Introduction to Market Microstructure
2. Order Types and Order Book Dynamics
3. Price Formation, Bid-Ask Spread and Liquidity
4. Transaction Cost and Slippages (most important for algo trading)

## Part 1 — What is Microstructure

The formal definition is: **"It is the study of how trades happen, how prices move, and how orders interact inside the exchange."**

And in simple words — microstructure means understanding everything that happens between pressing the button and the order getting executed, and the PnL reaching you.

What all does it include? See:

- How does your order get executed?
- Order has a timestamp, a price stamp
- If two people's orders are the same, does the partially filled one get it first or the fully filled one?
- Do hidden orders get preference first or who gets it?
- How does the server react?

Understanding ALL of this = understanding market microstructure.

### What Does the Exchange Do

To understand the base, let's see what the exchange does:

1. The exchange collects orders from everyone — many people have placed bids, many asks, for many shares, for many indexes
2. It collects all of these and creates an **ORDER BOOK**
3. In the order book there's bid on one side, ask on the other
4. It gathers all bids together, gathers all asks together
5. Then matches them

In microstructure we'll understand what's inside this — order matching, order time, iceberg order, hidden order, price formation, liquidity, order flow, total volumes, bid-ask spread, market participants' behavior, impact cost.

### Why This Matters for Algo Trading

Four solid reasons:

**1. Determining slippage:** Once you understand microstructure, you'll know what your slippage will be like. Example — if placing market orders in the strategy gives too much slippage, you can use limit orders there. BUT don't use limit orders everywhere!

**2. The limit order trade-off:** Why not everywhere? Because with limit orders some trades get missed — 10% to 15-20% trades get missed. And if a big trade gets missed among those missed trades, the whole dynamics/metrics get ruined. The solution is to place limit orders on small differences — with the help of the order book (we'll learn this ahead).

**3. High probability entries:** Only with microstructure knowledge can you take high probability entry and execution decisions.

**4. Directly affected strategies:** Scalping, option selling, intraday strategies — microstructure has a direct effect on all of these.

## Part 2 — Order Types and Order Book

In theory there are many order types, but there's no point understanding them until used in real life. We'll understand only 3 major types that are helpful in the course and in real life:

1. Market Order
2. Limit Order
3. Stop Loss Order

First understand order book basics, because all three types are based on it. In the order book there's a buyer on one side, seller on the other. Buyer gives their best price, seller gives their best price.

- **Best Bid = highest buy price**
- **Best Ask = lowest sell price**

Real example — Pakka Limited: the highest price on the buy side (best bid) was ₹116.16, the lowest price on the sell side (best ask) was ₹116.42/₹116.43.

### Type 1 — Market Order

Definition: *"Whatever the price is, BUY RIGHT NOW at that price"* — no price specified, instant execution wanted.

To understand this, let's use the house-bid analogy. Imagine Pakka Limited's share is a house you're going to buy. Bidding is happening inside that house:

- One person says: *"this house will sell for 116 rupees"*
- One comes: *"I'll sell this house for 117 rupees"*
- One comes: *"116 rupees 80 paise"*
- Then someone comes: *"I'll sell this house for 116 rupees 43 paise"*

So sell-side bids are being placed like this. Now:

**When placing BUY market order:** You say "buy me this house at whatever price it's at, right now." You get the sell side's LOWEST price (best ask) → ₹116.43. Remember the rule: on a buy market order you always get the lowest sell price.

**When placing SELL market order:** You get the buy side's HIGHEST price (best bid). In the example, the highest on the buyer side in the order book was 116.16 → that's what you get. When a sell market order was placed on the chart, the price received was 116.21 — which was the top price on the buyer side.

Practical check: when bought, the chart showed the lowest sell price 116.42 — that's what was received. Meaning: **when buying you get the lowest sell-side bid; when selling you get the highest buyer-side bid.**

### Type 2 — Limit Order

Now you tell the market your own price: *"I want to buy this at THIS price; until that price comes, do NOT purchase."*

Continuing the house analogy. First I went into the room and said "buy it at whatever price is going" — that was a market order. Now I come and say: "I'm telling my price — Pakka is at 116.16, I'll buy it at 114 rupees, because I don't see value at 116."

This 114 rupees = limit price. Instruction to the market:

- Whenever any seller becomes ready to sell at 114 → execute then
- If seller isn't ready to sell at 114 → do not execute

What do you see in the order book? Bids running at 116.14, 5 bids visible at 116.07. I placed a limit order at 114 rupees. That order shows OPEN — showing its price 114. Whenever 114 rupees comes, my order will execute.

### Type 3 — Stop Loss Order

Scenario: you have a security (which you bought at 116.16). You don't want it to keep falling to zero. So what will you do? You'll tell the market a predefined price. At that price your order will be executed.

> **Stop Loss Order = telling a predefined price at which the order should be executed (to control loss).**

### Order Matching — Price-Time Priority

Now the core logic — how does the exchange decide which order executes first? The answer is **PRICE-TIME PRIORITY**, and it's so core that even if you search on ChatGPT or Google, you'll find the same thing.

**Price priority:** The highest bid gets the most priority. The lowest ask gets priority.

**Time priority:** If two orders are at the same price, the one whose order came FIRST gets filled FIRST. The later one gets filled later.

Let's nail it with two examples:

**Example 1 (time priority):** I placed a buy bid at 116.16 at 9:16. You placed a bid at 116.16 at 9:17. Same price! → Mine executes first, yours later — because mine came first.

**Example 2 (price priority):** I placed at 116.16 at 9:16. You placed at 116.40 at 9:16 itself. Your price is better → yours executes FIRST. Price priority beats time.

When building an algo, this logic must be known — because your bot also stands in this same queue.

## Part 3 — Price Formation, Spread and Liquidity

### How LTP is Formed

What we see in candlestick charts and line charts — that price formation creates it.

Real example: Loopin. Market had closed. Loopin's final price was ₹2080.99. Why? Because Loopin's LAST TRADE happened at this price. Open the order book and the sell price/best ask shows different (~2500), but someone transacted at the last traded price.

- **LTP = Last Traded Price** — the price at which the last transaction happened
- Someone else transacts → LTP immediately comes to that price

Recap: highest buyer = best bid, lowest seller = best ask.

### Bid-Ask Spread

> **Spread = Best Ask − Best Bid** (the difference between buyer and seller)

**Example 1 — Pakka Limited (narrow spread):** Buyer standing at 100 rupees, seller standing at 100.05. Difference = 5 paise → NARROW SPREAD.

**Example 2 — Eco-Reco (wide spread):** Eco-Reco is a small company. Buyer at 100, seller at 101.50. Big difference → WIDE SPREAD.

Why does wide spread form? Because there are NOT too many sellers there. When sellers are few → price discovery doesn't happen well.

Golden rules:

- **Narrow spread = HIGH liquidity**
- **Wide spread = LOW liquidity**

Derivatives note: In Bank Nifty weekly options, spreads widen after 3 PM — because liquidity drops.

Key line: **"Spreads are the HIDDEN COST of trading."** The more the spread, the more cost will be incurred in your trading. That's why narrow spread is good — more liquidity, less costing.

### Liquidity — 3 Forms

Liquidity has 3 forms, one by one:

**1. Market Depth:** How many buy/sell orders are waiting at different price levels. Example — in the order book at 101, 100, 100.10, 100.20, orders are present at every level. 40 orders at 100, 50 orders at 100.10... pending buy/sell orders at different price points = market depth.

**2. Order Flow:** In real time, how many orders are actually being executed in the market. How many market sells, how many market buys. Differentiation between limit vs market orders — how many limit sell orders, how many market orders. Separate analysis of order flow = **TPO (Time Price Opportunity)**. The whole information of Market Profile is related to TPO and order flow.

**3. Trading Volume:** Total trading volume in the market at that time (from morning till now).

### Effect of High vs Low Liquidity

With high liquidity, enter/exit impact cost is ~0. What happens with low liquidity? Example — there are 1000 buyers, but only 20 sellers available. Result: many failed orders, many entries won't execute.

### Impact Cost — Reality Check

This section is most important, because it tells the real gap between backtest and live trading.

A real story: a trader ran a strategy whose backtest result showed ~188%/150%. But in reality the return was ~60-70% LESS. That gap in between = **IMPACT COST**.

Where impact cost is incurred:

- At entry: 20-30 points are spent
- At exit: 10-15 points are spent

Quantity also has an effect: impact cost of executing 1 quantity ≠ impact cost of executing 1000 quantity. As quantity increases, impact cost increases.

Company comparison: Pakka Limited's impact cost will be low (more liquidity). Eco-Reco's impact cost will be high — because of liquidity.

## Part 4 — Transaction Costs and Slippage

There are 2 types of transaction costs:

**Implicit costs** (generated by the security itself): bid-ask spread, slippage, market impact cost.

**Explicit costs** (fixed/outside): brokerage, exchange fees, STT, GST, stamp duty.

### What is Slippage — Understand Through Calculation

```
Algo sent order:            ₹200
Executed at:                ₹202
                            ─────
Slippage = 202 − 200 =      2 points

In money terms:
2 points × lot size 75 =    ₹1,500  ← this is your cost!
```

Just a 2 point difference, but multiplied by lot size it's ₹1,500 cost. Now you understand why slippage is so serious?

### Why Slippage is High

Three main reasons:

**1. High volatility:** When volatility is high, bid-ask spread becomes WIDE. Wide spread = very high slippage.

**2. News events:** What happens in a news event — everyone places MARKET ORDERS. Everyone thinks "I'll react first." With so many market orders the spread widens → very high slippage.

**3. Weekly expiry (~2:45 PM):** Using market orders around 2:45 during weekly expiry gives very high slippage.

### How to Reduce Slippage

**Option 1 — Use limit orders. BUT not the ultimate solution!** Because limit orders have their own problems: if you blindly use limit orders, ~20% orders get missed. And if a big profit trade got missed among those 20% missed orders, the whole metrics get ruined.

**Option 2 — Smart execution techniques (real solution):**

- Smart Order Slicing
- VWAP Execution

We'll work on these ahead in code — using tools through Python for smart order slicing, so orders execute with minimum impact cost and slippage is reduced.

## Nutshell

Microstructure = the entire system from pressing the button to order execution + PnL arriving. The exchange collects orders and creates an order book (bids vs asks), then matches using price-time priority. 3 order types: Market (instant, best available price), Limit (your price, wait), Stop Loss (predefined exit). Narrow spread = high liquidity = low hidden cost; wide spread = low liquidity. Impact cost eats the difference between backtest and live — 188% backtest can become 60-70% less live. Slippage increases with volatility/news/expiry — solution is smart order slicing + VWAP execution.

## What It Is NOT

- ❌ **Microstructure is not just theory.** It directly affects your PnL — slippage, spread cost, impact cost all come from this.
- ❌ **Limit orders are not always better.** Yes they save slippage, but they can miss 15-20% of trades — and if a big profit trade got missed, the whole metrics get ruined.
- ❌ **Backtest return is not live return.** Impact cost and slippage aren't properly modeled in backtests — that's why live results always come a bit lower.

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

**Answer:** Two rules. Price priority: the order with the better price executes first — highest bid, lowest ask at top. Time priority: at the same price, the quantity of the order that came first gets filled first. 3. What's the relation of narrow spread and wide spread with liquidity?

<details>

When sellers are few, price discovery doesn't happen well — spread widens. And spread is a hidden cost, so trading cost is lower in narrow-spread stocks.

</details>
