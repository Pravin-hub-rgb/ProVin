# 2.5 Statistical Thinking — Mean, Variance, Correlation & Distribution

In the previous file a question was left open: strategy A and strategy B both gave 15% return — which is better? Same return, but one was predictable and the other a roller-coaster. To judge this you need **statistics.** And the truth is — finance is all data, and many traders fail precisely because they don't know statistics. Statistics gives you three things: understanding market behavior, measuring risk, and building profitable systems.

Don't be scared — in Python all of this calculates in 2 minutes (we'll do it in the next file). First understand the concept, because the computer will do the calculation — you'll have to understand it.

## Mean — "This Much You Can Expect"

Mean = average = sum of all values ÷ count of values.

```
Values: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5
Sum = 15, n = 5 → Mean = 3
```

In trading, mean means **expected value of returns** — on average this much return you can expect.

- Nifty's daily return average is ~**0.05%** — this is the expected daily return
- An algo trader's 5-day returns averaged to **0.46% per day**

Why does this number matter?

- **Evaluates expected profitability** — does the system have substance or not
- **Mean reversion strategy** runs on this — the assumption that price will come back toward mean (we'll build these strategies later)
- **Compares strategies** — look at both means, the one with better expectation wins
- **Helps calculate expectancy per trade**

In one line, mean: *"this much you can expect"* — not guaranteed, but expectation setting.

## Variance & Standard Deviation — Risk's Measure

Now the second question — how spread out are returns from their mean?

**Variance** tells how much data is spread from the mean. But one problem — variance comes in square terms (units squared), which is hard to understand directly.

That's why we use **Standard Deviation (SD)** = square root of variance. The same spread, in simple units.

In trading, SD means:

- **How volatile the asset is** — higher SD = more volatility
- **How risky the strategy is** — higher SD = more risk
- **How uncertain the return is** — profit is also uncertain

### Strategy Comparison — The Same Question from 2.4

Two strategies, both with same mean:

| Strategy | Returns | SD | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 0.5, 0.4, 0.6 | Low | Predictable ✅ |
| **B** | 3, -2, 4 | High | Unpredictable ⚠️ |

Both have similar averages. But strategy A gives consistent returns every day; B gives 3 sometimes, -2 sometimes. Now the previous file's question is answered — **even with same mean, low variance strategy is better.**

Golden insight, write it down:

> **High return + high variance < Moderate return + low variance**

More SD = more volatility = more risk = lower chances of sustainable profit.

Practical uses: volatility measurement, position sizing (2.4's pillar 2!), stop loss levels, portfolio risk control, and Sharpe ratio calculation — all stand on this one number.

## Correlation — Relationship Between Two Things

Correlation tells — when one thing moves, how does the other move. Range: **+1 to -1.**

- **+1:** Perfectly same direction — one up, other up
- **0:** No relationship — both in their own worlds
- **-1:** Perfectly opposite — one up, other down

Real example: equity market and Gold have negative correlation — if equity goes up, gold goes down (sometimes vice versa).

### Why So Important in Trading

Biggest use case: **multi-strategy portfolio.** Run two strategies, calculate their correlation:

- Correlation **0.9** = both move almost together. When one goes into drawdown, the other also in drawdown → **drawdown increases**, risk concentrated
- Correlation **0.1** = both independent. If one is in drawdown, the other might be in profit → **drawdown dramatically reduces**

Remember the 2.4 story — 5 strategies, all drawdowns came together? The real reason was: those strategies were highly correlated, even though instruments were different. Correlation must be checked, not just instrument names.

Other uses: hedging (join opposite-correlation assets), pair trading (trade correlated pairs), market neutral strategies.

## Distribution — Data's Pattern

Distribution tells — how often which value appears in data. Frequency pattern.

Three types important for trading:

### Normal Distribution (Bell Curve)

Symmetric pattern around mean. Its famous rules:

- 68% data within **1 SD**
- 95% data within **2 SD**
- 99% data within **3 SD**

Volatility models are built on this assumption.

### Fat Tail — Financial Data's Real Truth

Here's the twist. **Financial data does NOT follow normal distribution — it has fat tail.** Meaning extreme events occur MORE frequently than expected.

Black Swan events — circuit hitting, gap-up/gap-down openings — theoretically should be rare, but practically happen a lot. Consequences:

- Stop losses will hit more than you thought
- If your backtest assumes normal distribution → **results will be over-optimistic**
- Gap risk is real — that's why overnight position risk was counted in 2.4

### Skewed Distribution — Asymmetric Pattern

When data puts more weight on one side:

| Type | Pattern | Example | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Negative Skew** | Small wins + occasional large loss | Option selling | Dangerous ⚠️ |
| **Positive Skew** | Small losses + occasional large win | Trend following | Excellent ✅ |

This table is the cheat code for the whole trading career:

- **Trend following is positively skewed** — small losses keep happening, but sometimes a big trend gets caught that covers everything. That's why trend following is considered a good strategy.
- **Option selling is negatively skewed** — small premium earned every day (feel-good), but sometimes such a big loss comes that months of premium goes in one go.

Both can have the same average profit — but seeing the skew tells you in which strategy you'll sleep peacefully at night.

## Nutshell

Statistics = data-driven decisions, not feeling-based. **Mean** = expected value (Nifty avg 0.05%/day) — for expectation setting and strategy comparison. **SD** = spread from mean in simple units — the more, the riskier; golden rule: moderate return + low variance > high return + high variance. **Correlation** (+1/0/-1) — in multi-strategy portfolio, correlated strategies (0.9) increase drawdown, uncorrelated (0.1) decrease it. **Distribution** — financial data has fat tail (extreme events more, backtest becomes over-optimistic), and check skew: positive skew (trend following) excellent, negative skew (option selling) dangerous.

## What It Is NOT

- ❌ **Mean is not guaranteed return.** Just expectation. On a single day it can go 10x above mean, or below.
- ❌ **Don't blindly apply normal distribution to financial data.** Fat tail is reality — extreme events occur more than normal assumption, that's why backtests become over-optimistic.
- ❌ **High correlation is not just numbers' coincidence.** Two strategies with 0.9 correlation means underlying logic is similar — different instruments isn't enough, risk is still concentrated.
