## Chapter 1: Money — Let's Start From Scratch

Before we talk about business finance, let's talk about money itself. Because if you think about it — what even *is* money?

You go to a dhaba. You eat. You pay ₹120. The dhaba owner takes your ₹120 and pays his vegetable supplier. The supplier pays his farmer. The farmer buys seeds. And so on, forever.

Money is just a **medium of exchange** — a universally agreed-upon token that represents value, so that we don't have to barter. Instead of you paying for dal makhani with 2 hours of web development work, you pay ₹120 and everyone's happy.

But here's the deeper thing about money in business:

**Money is not the goal. Money is the fuel.**

A car needs petrol to move. A business needs money to operate — to pay people, buy materials, run ads, rent space. Run out of money? The business stops. Completely. Even if you have the best product, the best team, the most loyal customers — no money means no business.

This is why every concept in finance comes back to one question: **where is the money, where is it going, and how do we make sure we don't run out?**

Keep that in mind as you read everything else.

## Chapter 2: Value — What Makes Something Worth Money?

A bottle of water costs ₹20 at a shop. The same bottle costs ₹100 at a movie theatre. The same bottle — if you're lost in a desert — is worth everything you own.

The water didn't change. The *situation* changed. Things are worth what someone is willing to pay, in a given context.

In business, your job is to create something people value enough to pay for. The CFO's job is to make sure the cost of creating that thing is less than what people pay for it. The difference is called **profit**.

Simple equation that runs the entire world of business:

```
Revenue - Costs = Profit (or Loss)
```

- Revenue > Costs → Profit → business survives and grows
- Revenue < Costs → Loss → business is burning through savings or loans
- Revenue = Costs → Break-even → alive but not growing

Everything in finance is a variation of this equation.

## Chapter 3: The Business — What Is It, Financially?

When your brother starts a company, what has he actually created — financially?

He's created an entity that:
1. **Owns things** — laptop, cash, maybe inventory
2. **Owes things** — loans, unpaid bills, taxes
3. Has **people who funded it** — himself, you, maybe future investors

These three things map directly to the most fundamental equation in all of accounting:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

This is called the **Accounting Equation**. It is always, always, always true. Every single financial transaction in history — from a street vendor buying vegetables to Amazon acquiring a company for $10 billion — fits inside this equation.

Let's break each piece down.

---

## Chapter 4: Assets — What You Own

An **asset** is anything the business owns that has value.

Think of it as everything on the "our side" of the scoreboard.

### Current Assets — things that will turn into cash within 1 year

**Cash** — money in the bank account or in hand. The most liquid asset there is. Liquid means instantly usable.

**Accounts Receivable** — money that customers owe you but haven't paid yet. You've done the work, sent the invoice, waiting for payment. It's yours — just not in hand yet. Very common in agencies and B2B businesses.

**Inventory** — if your company sells physical goods, the unsold stock sitting in your warehouse counts as an asset because you'll eventually sell it and get cash.

**Prepaid Expenses** — you've paid for something in advance, like 6 months of office rent upfront. That future benefit counts as an asset until it's "used up."

### Non-Current Assets (Fixed Assets) — things that last more than 1 year

**Equipment** — computers, machinery, vehicles. Physical things you use to run the business.

**Furniture** — office furniture, fixtures.

**Intangible Assets** — things you own that aren't physical. A patent. A brand name. Software you built. Real value, just untouchable.

**Investments** — if the company put money into stocks or other businesses.

### The Asset Rule
Assets eventually become cash — either directly (you sell inventory) or indirectly (you use equipment to make products that you sell). If something can't generate cash, it's not really a business asset.

**Simple example — your brother's digital agency:**
- ₹2 lakh cash in the bank *(cash)*
- ₹50,000 a client owes him for last month's work *(accounts receivable)*
- 2 laptops worth ₹1.5 lakh *(equipment)*
- Annual software subscriptions paid upfront *(prepaid expense)*

**Total Assets ≈ ₹4 lakh**

---

## Chapter 5: Liabilities — What You Owe

A **liability** is anything the business owes to someone outside the company. These are obligations — promises to pay.

### Current Liabilities — due within 1 year

**Accounts Payable** — bills you owe to suppliers but haven't paid yet. You received the goods or service, you owe the money.

**Salaries Payable** — employee salaries that have been earned but not yet paid.

**GST Payable** — GST you've collected from customers but not yet sent to the government. That money is sitting in your account but it is NOT yours.

**TDS Payable** — tax you've deducted from payments but not yet deposited with the Income Tax department.

**Advance from Customers** — a client paid you upfront for work you haven't done yet. That cash is in your account, but it's a liability — you owe them the work.

**Short-term Loans** — loans due within the year.

### Non-Current Liabilities — due after 1 year

**Long-term Loans** — bank loans repaid over multiple years.

### The Liability Reality
Liabilities are not inherently bad. Almost every company in the world carries them. Taking a loan to buy equipment that generates revenue is smart. The problem is when liabilities become unmanageable — when you owe more than you can realistically pay back.

**Same example continued:**
- ₹30,000 owed to a freelancer *(accounts payable)*
- ₹15,000 GST collected from clients, to be filed *(GST payable)*
- ₹1 lakh loan from a friend *(short-term loan)*

**Total Liabilities ≈ ₹1.45 lakh**

## Chapter 6: Equity — What's Left for the Owners

**Equity** is what remains after you subtract what you owe from what you own.

```
Equity = Assets - Liabilities
```

It's the owners' actual stake in the business. If you shut the company down today, sold everything (assets), paid off everyone (liabilities) — what's left belongs to the owners. That's equity.

Also called: Net Worth, Shareholders' Equity, Owner's Capital — all the same idea.

### What makes up Equity?

**Share Capital / Owner's Capital** — money founders actually put in. If your brother invested ₹5 lakh of his own savings — that's share capital.

**Retained Earnings** — profits the company made but *didn't* pay out to owners. Left inside the company to fund growth. Profit reinvested.

**Accumulated Losses** — if the company lost more than it made over its lifetime, retained earnings go negative. Many startups run this way for years. Zomato had thousands of crores in accumulated losses before turning profitable — and was still valued at ₹50,000+ crore because investors believed in future earnings.

**Continuing our example:**
Assets (₹4L) − Liabilities (₹1.45L) = **Equity ₹2.55 lakh**

If the company shut down today, after paying all debts, the owners walk away with ₹2.55 lakh.

## Chapter 7: The Accounting Equation in Action

Let's watch the equation work in real time. Every transaction, two things happen — and both sides always stay equal.

**Brother starts company, puts in ₹5 lakh:**
```
Assets (Cash ₹5L) = Liabilities (₹0) + Equity (₹5L) ✅
```

**Buys a laptop for ₹1 lakh cash:**
```
Assets (Cash ₹4L + Laptop ₹1L) = Liabilities (₹0) + Equity (₹5L) ✅
Assets still total ₹5L — just changed form.
```

**Takes a ₹2 lakh bank loan:**
```
Assets (Cash ₹6L + Laptop ₹1L) = Liabilities (Loan ₹2L) + Equity (₹5L) ✅
Both sides grew by ₹2L.
```

**Does a project, earns ₹50,000 cash:**
```
Assets (Cash ₹6.5L + Laptop ₹1L) = Liabilities (₹2L) + Equity (₹5.5L) ✅
Equity grew — profit increases owner's value.
```

**Pays ₹20,000 rent:**
```
Assets (Cash ₹6.3L + Laptop ₹1L) = Liabilities (₹2L) + Equity (₹5.3L) ✅
Cash down, equity down — expense reduced owner's value.
```

Notice: every single transaction keeps both sides equal. Always. This is why it's called *double-entry* accounting — every transaction has two effects, and they always balance.

## Chapter 8: Revenue, Income, Profit — These Are NOT the Same

People use these words interchangeably casually. In finance, they mean very different things. Mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes.

**Revenue (also called Turnover or Sales)**
The total money coming in from selling your product or service. Before any deductions. The top line.
*Your brother bills clients ₹10 lakh this month. Revenue = ₹10 lakh.*

**Gross Profit**
Revenue minus the direct cost of delivering that revenue.
```
If delivering ₹10L of work required ₹4L in freelancer costs:
Gross Profit = ₹10L - ₹4L = ₹6L
Gross Margin = ₹6L / ₹10L = 60%
```

**Operating Profit (EBIT)**
Gross Profit minus operating expenses — rent, full-time salaries, software, marketing.
```
Operating Profit = Gross Profit - Operating Expenses
If operating expenses = ₹3L: Operating Profit = ₹6L - ₹3L = ₹3L
```

**Net Profit (The Bottom Line)**
Operating Profit minus interest on loans and taxes.
```
Net Profit = Operating Profit - Interest - Taxes
If interest = ₹15,000 and taxes = ₹50,000:
Net Profit = ₹3L - ₹15,000 - ₹50,000 = ₹2.35 lakh
```

**The full picture:**
```
Revenue                    ← Top line (total sales)
  - Direct Costs
= Gross Profit             ← How efficient is delivery?
  - Operating Expenses
= Operating Profit         ← How efficiently do we run?
  - Interest & Taxes
= Net Profit               ← Bottom line (what owners get)
```

## Chapter 9: Expenses — Where Does Money Go?

An **expense** is money spent to run the business. Not all expenses are equal.

### Direct vs Indirect

**Direct Expenses (Cost of Goods Sold)** — directly tied to making or delivering your product. If you don't sell, you don't incur these.
- Raw materials, freelancer costs, packaging, delivery cost per order

**Indirect Expenses (Overhead)** — exist regardless of how much you sell.
- Office rent, full-time salaries, internet, accounting fees

### Fixed vs Variable

**Fixed Costs** — stay the same regardless of sales volume.
- Rent: ₹30,000/month whether you make ₹1L or ₹10L revenue.

**Variable Costs** — change directly with sales.
- More orders = more delivery costs. More projects = more freelancer costs.

**Why this matters:** A business with mostly fixed costs — once it crosses break-even — becomes very profitable very fast because each extra rupee of revenue costs very little to generate. This is why software companies are so profitable at scale. Selling one more software subscription costs almost nothing, but earns full revenue.

## Chapter 10: The 3 Financial Statements — Simple Version

Three documents tell the complete financial story of any company.

**Income Statement (P&L)**
Shows revenue, expenses, and profit over a *period of time*.
Answers: Did we make money?
Analogy: Monthly salary slip — shows earnings and deductions.

**Balance Sheet**
Shows assets, liabilities, equity at a *single point in time*.
Answers: What do we own, owe, and what's the net worth?
Analogy: Photograph of financial health on one specific day.

**Cash Flow Statement**
Shows how cash actually moved in and out over a *period of time*.
Answers: Where did the money actually go?
Analogy: Bank statement — but organised by activity type.

**The critical connection:** You can show profit on P&L but be running out of cash. This happens when customers haven't paid yet, or when you spent heavily on assets. The Cash Flow Statement is the reality check that the other two can't give you.

## Chapter 11: Debit & Credit — The Most Misunderstood Concept

Most people get confused because they think of "debit" like a debit card — money going away. In accounting, it's completely different.

Forget your bank account completely.

**Debit = Left side of a ledger entry. That's it.**
**Credit = Right side of a ledger entry. That's it.**

Every account type behaves predictably:

| Account Type | Increases with | Decreases with |
|-------------|---------------|----------------|
| Assets | Debit (Left) | Credit (Right) |
| Liabilities | Credit (Right) | Debit (Left) |
| Equity | Credit (Right) | Debit (Left) |
| Revenue | Credit (Right) | Debit (Left) |
| Expenses | Debit (Left) | Credit (Right) |

**Real example — client pays ₹50,000:**
- Cash (Asset) goes UP → **Debit Cash ₹50,000**
- Revenue goes UP → **Credit Revenue ₹50,000**
Balanced. Done.

**Pay ₹10,000 rent:**
- Rent Expense goes UP → **Debit Rent Expense ₹10,000**
- Cash (Asset) goes DOWN → **Credit Cash ₹10,000**
Balanced. Done.

You don't need to memorise the table above. You need to understand the logic — every transaction has two effects and they always balance. The more you practice in GnuCash, the more natural this becomes.

## Chapter 12: Key Ratios — The Health Check Numbers

Ratios turn raw numbers into meaningful signals. Like a doctor checking blood pressure — the number tells a story.

### Liquidity Ratios (Can we pay short-term bills?)

**Current Ratio**
```
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
```
If Current Assets = ₹5L and Current Liabilities = ₹2L → Ratio = 2.5
For every ₹1 owed short-term, you have ₹2.50 to cover it. Healthy.
Rule of thumb: above 1.5 is comfortable. Below 1 is a warning.

### Profitability Ratios (Are we efficient?)

**Gross Margin %**
```
Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue × 100
```
Higher = better. Software: 70–90%. Retail: 20–40%. Manufacturing: 30–50%.

**Net Profit Margin %**
```
Net Profit Margin = Net Profit / Revenue × 100
```
What % of every rupee earned becomes actual profit.

### Leverage Ratios (How much debt?)

**Debt-to-Equity Ratio**
```
D/E Ratio = Total Debt / Total Equity
```
D/E of 2 means for every ₹1 of owner money, there's ₹2 borrowed. High. Risky.
Rule of thumb: below 1 is generally safe. Above 2 starts getting risky.

## Chapter 13: Working Capital — The Pulse of Daily Business

**Working Capital** is the money available to run the business day-to-day.

```
Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities
```

If Current Assets = ₹8L and Current Liabilities = ₹3L → Working Capital = ₹5L

This ₹5L pays salaries, buys materials, keeps the lights on.

**Positive** → more short-term assets than obligations. Healthy.
**Negative** → you owe more short-term than you have. Cash crisis territory — even if you're technically profitable.

### The Working Capital Cycle

1. You have **Cash**
2. Use cash to buy **Materials/Resources**
3. Resources become a **Product or Service**
4. Delivered and sold → becomes **Accounts Receivable**
5. Customer pays → back to **Cash**

The time it takes to complete this cycle is the **Cash Conversion Cycle**. Shorter = better. Longer = you need more working capital to keep the engine running.

**Why Indian SMEs struggle here:** Large clients often delay payment 60–90 days. Small vendors do the work, wait months to get paid, but keep paying their own bills immediately. This is a working capital problem — not a profitability problem. Many profitable businesses have died from this.

## Chapter 14: Depreciation — Assets Get Old

You buy a laptop for ₹1 lakh. After 3 years it's worth ₹20,000. After 5 years, nearly nothing.

**Depreciation** is spreading the cost of an asset over its useful life — instead of recording the full cost as an expense in the year of purchase.

**Why it matters:**
- Makes profit calculations more accurate — you use the laptop for 3 years, so the cost should spread across 3 years
- Reduces taxable profit each year (a real tax benefit)
- It's a non-cash expense — no money actually leaves the account when depreciation is recorded

**Straight-line depreciation (simplest method):**
```
Annual Depreciation = (Cost - Salvage Value) / Useful Life
= (₹1,00,000 - ₹10,000) / 3 years = ₹30,000/year
```

Each year, ₹30,000 appears on the P&L as an expense — even though no cash was paid that year.

## Chapter 15: GST — India's Tax on Every Transaction

GST replaced a dozen different taxes in 2017. Every time goods or services are sold in India, GST is collected.

**How it works — simply:**

Your agency charges a client ₹1 lakh for a website. You add 18% GST.
Client pays: ₹1L + ₹18,000 = **₹1.18 lakh total.**

That ₹18,000 is NOT your money. You collected it on behalf of the government.

**But here's the benefit — Input Tax Credit (ITC):**

You bought software tools for ₹20,000 + ₹3,600 GST.
That ₹3,600 GST you paid? Claim it back.

```
GST collected from client:    ₹18,000
GST paid on your purchase:  - ₹3,600
GST you actually pay govt:    ₹14,400
```

This prevents double-taxation and is real cash savings when tracked properly. Many small businesses miss ITC completely and overpay.

**GST Rates:**
- 0% — essential food items
- 5% — basic goods and some services
- 12% — mid-range goods
- 18% — most services including digital, standard goods
- 28% — luxury goods, automobiles

## Chapter 16: TDS — Tax Deducted at Source

India's system of collecting income tax at the point of payment — before money reaches the recipient.

**Example:**
Brother's company pays a freelancer ₹50,000. TDS on professional fees = 10%.
He deducts ₹5,000, pays the freelancer ₹45,000, and deposits ₹5,000 with the Income Tax department.
The freelancer gets credit for ₹5,000 already paid toward their tax bill.

**What this means for you:**
- You'll *deduct* TDS when paying vendors above certain thresholds
- You'll *receive* TDS deducted when large clients pay you
- Not depositing TDS = serious penalties and interest
- TDS certificates (Form 16A) must be issued to vendors quarterly

## Chapter 17: Time Value of Money — Why ₹100 Today Beats ₹100 Tomorrow

The most powerful idea in finance. Simple to understand, endlessly applicable.

**₹100 today is worth more than ₹100 a year from now.**

Because ₹100 today can be invested. At 6% in a savings account — it becomes ₹106 next year.

So if someone says "₹100 now or ₹100 next year" — take it now. Obviously.

"₹100 now or ₹110 next year?" — depends what you can earn elsewhere. If you can earn 6% elsewhere, ₹110 next year (10% return) wins. If you can earn 12%, ₹100 now wins (becomes ₹112).

**Present Value (PV) — what is a future amount worth today?**
```
PV = Future Value / (1 + rate)^years

What is ₹1 lakh one year from now worth today at 10%?
PV = ₹1,00,000 / (1.10)^1 = ₹90,909
```

**Future Value (FV) — what will today's amount be worth later?**
```
FV = Present Value × (1 + rate)^years

₹1 lakh at 10% for 3 years:
FV = ₹1,00,000 × (1.10)^3 = ₹1,33,100
```

**Why every CFO needs this:** Every investment decision — buy this equipment? take this loan? accept this deal? — comes down to: is the future value I get worth more than the present value I'm giving up? TVM answers that.

## Chapter 18: Break-Even — The Survival Threshold

The point where revenue exactly covers all costs. No profit, no loss.

```
Break-Even Revenue = Fixed Costs / Gross Margin %
```

Brother's agency:
- Fixed costs (rent, salaries, software): ₹2 lakh/month
- Gross margin: 60%

```
Break-Even = ₹2,00,000 / 0.60 = ₹3,33,333/month
```

He must bill at least ₹3.33 lakh/month to not lose money.

**This is the first thing to calculate for any new business.** Before growth plans, valuations, investor decks — know your break-even. Below it, every month burns money. Above it, every extra rupee earned is increasingly profitable.

## Quick Reference Glossary

| Term | One-line explanation |
|------|---------------------|
| Asset | Something the company owns that has value |
| Liability | Something the company owes to someone else |
| Equity | What's left for owners after paying all liabilities |

| Net Profit | What's left after all expenses, interest, and tax |

| Runway | Months until cash runs out at current burn rate |

| Accounts Payable | Money you owe suppliers |
| Balance Sheet | Snapshot of assets, liabilities, equity at one moment |

| Audit | Independent check that your books are accurate |
