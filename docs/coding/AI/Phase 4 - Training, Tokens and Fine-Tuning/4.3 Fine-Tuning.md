The last file gave the model language: pre-training taught it to predict the next token. But a brilliant text predictor isn't yet a helpful assistant. Ask it "What's the capital of France?" and it might continue with "What's the capital of Germany?" — because that's what text on the internet often looks like after a question. It knows the answer is Paris, but it was never taught that answering is its job. Phase two fixes that.

**Fine-tuning** (also called tuning) is the second phase of training, and its job is to change the model's *behavior*, not its knowledge. The model already knows language from pre-training. Now we teach it to respond to an instruction with an actual answer, politely and helpfully. The knowledge stays; the habit changes.

The first method of phase two is **supervised fine-tuning**, or **SFT**. Humans write many example conversations, each with an instruction and a good answer. The model trains on those pairs using the same guess, compare, and nudge method as before. After enough examples, it learns the pattern: when text looks like an instruction, the right continuation is a helpful answer, written the way a good human would reply. Supervised just means the correct answers are provided by humans, so the model has examples to copy. SFT teaches it to answer — but the quality is still uneven. Some answers are great, some mediocre, some wrong or rude.

That unevenness is what the second method fixes: **reinforcement learning from human feedback**, or **RLHF**. The model is asked the same question and writes two different answers, A and B. A human reads both and picks the better one — not necessarily the correct one, because often both are fine and one is just clearer, safer, or more helpful. That pick is the feedback. The model then gets nudged toward the style of answer the human picked, and away from the one they rejected — tiny adjustments to its parameters, so answers like the winner become more likely next time. Repeat across a huge number of questions and picks, and the model's behavior drifts toward what people prefer: clearer, more helpful, safer answers. Reinforcement just means rewarding good behavior so it happens more often.

Humans can't rate millions of answers one by one, so there's a helper: a separate model called the **reward model**. It's trained on the humans' picks, and its only job is to look at an answer and give it a score — higher if humans would likely prefer it, lower if not. Then the main model practices writing answers, the reward model scores each one, and the main model gets nudged toward answers that earn high scores. The reward is just a score standing in for what humans would pick, and it lets the training run at huge scale. The reward model only judges; it doesn't write answers.

The two methods aren't alternatives — they're usually stages done in sequence. SFT comes first, to teach the basic behavior of answering. Then RLHF polishes the quality. Big assistant models like Claude and ChatGPT generally use both, though the exact recipe differs by company and isn't always public.

The nots. Fine-tuning is *not* teaching new facts — it changes behavior, not knowledge; the facts came from pre-training. SFT is *not* rule-writing — it's copying from human-written examples, not hand-coded instructions. RLHF is *not* humans writing answers — humans only pick the better of two model-written answers. And the reward model is *not* the assistant — it only scores; it never writes.
