Phase 3 closed the agent loop — the LLM drives itself through tools until the job is done. But we've treated the LLM as a box of patterns that "predicts the next text" without ever asking: what is "text" to a model? It doesn't read words the way you do. It reads smaller pieces. Time to find the smallest unit.

A **token** is the smallest chunk of text the LLM reads and writes. It's usually a word, or a piece of a word. "Cat" might be one token. A long word like "unbelievable" might be split into two or three. Punctuation, spaces, even parts of words all become tokens. The model never sees "text" — it sees a stream of tokens.

Before the model sees your message, it gets chopped into tokens. And its reply is also generated one token at a time. So the whole pipeline — your message in, answer out — is really: text → tokens → numbers → prediction → tokens → text. The token is the bridge between human language and the model's math.

Why not just use words? Because words are messy — too many of them, and they change form (run, running, ran). Tokens are a fixed, manageable set, often a few tens of thousands, that can be combined to spell anything. That fixed set is called the tokenizer's vocabulary, and every model has its own.

Here's where tokens connect to something you already know: the context window. The table from 2.2 — the one that fills up and gets compacted — isn't measured in messages or words. It's measured in tokens. Each slot on the table is one token. So when we said the table has thousands of slots, we meant thousands of tokens. A long conversation or a big document eats tokens fast, and when the table fills, the controller compacts the oldest ones into a short summary, losing small details.

One small consequence worth holding: because tokens are the unit, the model's sense of "length" is token count, not word count. A short sentence with rare or long words can cost more tokens than a long sentence of common short words. That's why the same chat can hold more of one kind of text than another.

The nots. A token is *not* a word — it's often a piece of one, and the split isn't always where you'd guess. A token is *not* a letter — it's bigger than that, usually. And tokens are *not* universal — different models chop text differently, so the same sentence can be a different number of tokens in different models.
