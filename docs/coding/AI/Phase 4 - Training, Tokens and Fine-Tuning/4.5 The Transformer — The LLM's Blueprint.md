The last file assembled the full pipeline: pre-training builds the base model, SFT teaches it to answer, RLHF polishes the quality. But all of that runs on top of a specific design. We've said "the model is a neural network" and "training nudges the numbers" without ever answering: what is the model actually made of, so those numbers have a place to live? Time to open the architecture.

**Architecture** means the design, the blueprint of how the model is built inside. The LLM is a big neural network — a stack of layers of numbers — and the transformer is the particular blueprint that network follows. Almost all modern LLMs, including the ones you use, use it. The transformer is not a different kind of model; it's the floor plan of the neural network inside the LLM.

To see why it matters, a little history. Before transformers (around the 2010s), the main way to handle text with neural networks was a design called a **recurrent neural network**, or RNN. It read a sentence one word at a time, in order, and kept a small running memory of what it had seen so far. That worked for short sentences. But by the end of a long paragraph, the memory of the beginning had faded, and reading one word at a time was also slow — it had to finish word one before starting word two. So early text models read word by word with a fading memory, and they got worse the longer the text got.

In 2017, researchers at Google published a paper called "Attention Is All You Need," and introduced the transformer. The big change: instead of reading the essay one word at a time, the transformer looks at all the words at the same time. Every word can directly look at every other word, whether it's right next to it or 900 words back. The memory doesn't fade, because nothing has to be carried along — the first line is just as visible as the last. And since it reads everything together, it's much faster to train.

How can it read everything at once? It's not reading like a person. All the words are turned into numbers at the start, and the math runs on all of them at the same moment, in parallel. Nothing waits for anything. That parallel math is why modern chips help: a **GPU** (graphics processing unit) was built with thousands of tiny calculators that all work at the same moment, originally for graphics — calculating the color of millions of pixels every frame. It turns out the math behind AI is the same kind of bulk number-crunching, so GPUs are useful for AI even though they were made for games. The understanding doesn't come from the GPU, though — the GPU only provides the speed. The understanding comes from the design itself, specifically how each word's numbers get combined with the numbers of the other words. That combining step is called **attention**, and it's the next file.

One more piece to connect the dots: before the transformer can do any of this math, the tokens have to become numbers. That conversion is an embedding step (the same idea as the meaning fingerprints from Phase 2, though here it's about turning each token into a vector of numbers the transformer can compute on). The transformer then processes those numbers through its layers. So the chain inside the model is: tokens → numbers (embeddings) → layers of attention and feed-forward → next-token prediction.

One fix on a tempting confusion: RAM and the GPU do different jobs. RAM is storage — a workspace that holds the numbers. The GPU is the worker doing the calculations on them (and it has its own fast memory, VRAM, to hold the model's numbers close by). A normal CPU has a few very smart calculators going step by step; a GPU has thousands of simple ones working together. For AI, thousands working together wins.

The nots. The transformer is *not* a different kind of model — it's the blueprint of the neural network inside the LLM. RNNs are *not* gone — they're just the older approach the transformer replaced for most text tasks. The GPU is *not* where the understanding lives — it provides speed; the design provides the understanding. And attention is *not* the whole transformer — it's the main part inside each layer; the transformer is the whole blueprint.
