The last file named the combining step that gives the transformer its understanding: attention. Time to pull it apart — how each word looks at the others and decides which ones matter, and the second job in every layer that refines what attention gathered.

**Attention** is how each word looks at the other words in the sentence and decides which ones matter most for understanding it. The reason it exists is simple: when the numbers for one token pass through a layer, that token needs to know about the other tokens around it, otherwise it only sees itself. Without attention, "it" is just "it" — no clue it refers to the dog.

A classic example. Take the sentence "The dog chased the ball because it was fast." What does "it" mean — the dog or the ball? To work that out, the word "it" has to look back at the other words and figure out which one it relates to. Attention is that looking and weighing. Each word gets a score for how much it should pay attention to every other word, and the important ones count more. "Dog" or "ball" gets a high score for "it"; "the" gets a low one. Then the token pulls in more information from the high-scoring ones, so its numbers now carry some of that context.

One precision on what it's doing: attention isn't mainly finding similarity, it's finding relevance. For "it," the model works out which other words matter most to understanding it. The relationships aren't written as labels like "name of the father" — they're stored as numbers, mixed into each token's list, so the model can use them in the next layers.

A richer example to lock the idea. Imagine a sentence: "Raj is the father, Ravi is the child." Without attention, "Ravi" and "child" are just two separate tokens with no link. Attention connects them, so the numbers for "child" now carry information about Ravi, and "father" carries information about Raj. That building of relationships between tokens is what attention does.

Now the second job in each layer: the **feed-forward step**. After attention has mixed context into each token, the feed-forward step takes each token's numbers, one token at a time, and processes them further through a small neural network inside the layer. It doesn't look at the other tokens anymore — attention already did that. Think of it as the thinking step: attention gathers the relevant information, and feed-forward works with it, transforming the numbers into a richer, more useful form for the next layer. That small network is part of the layer itself, not a separate model, and a lot of the model's learned knowledge is believed to be stored in these feed-forward weights.

So each layer does the same two moves: attention gathers context, then feed-forward processes it. And this pair repeats, stacked, many times — dozens of layers, sometimes a hundred or more, each with its own attention and its own feed-forward step, each with its own weights. It's a design choice, not a fixed rule; more layers generally mean a bigger model that can capture more complex patterns, but also costs more to train and run.

As the tokens move up the stack, each layer refines their numbers a bit more, so by the top they carry much richer information than they started with. The early layers tend to pick up simple things, like grammar and which words are related. The later layers pick up bigger things, like the overall meaning and what should come next. At the very top, those final numbers get turned into a prediction of the next token — and that prediction is based mostly on the last token's numbers, because that's the position that has gathered context from everything before it.

So the whole inside story: tokens in, a stack of layers each doing attention then feed-forward, and a next-token prediction out. Training sets the numbers; the transformer is the structure holding them; attention is how each word weighs the others; feed-forward is how each token gets refined. That's how an LLM works inside.

The nots. Attention is *not* finding similarity — it's finding relevance; "dog" matters more than "the" for "it." Feed-forward is *not* a separate model — it's part of each layer. The layers are *not* identical in effect — early and late layers pick up different kinds of information. And the prediction is *not* based on all tokens equally at the top — it leans on the last token's numbers, which have gathered context from everything before.
