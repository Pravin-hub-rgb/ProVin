RAG fetches by meaning — but "by meaning" was hand-waved. Your question says "time off", the policy page says "leave": no shared words, exact matching finds nothing. So how does the fetcher know these two are close? With a fingerprint.

> **An embedding is a list of numbers that captures the meaning of a piece of text.**

Text goes in, numbers come out — and they're not random: similar meaning produces similar numbers. Your example, kept exactly: "leave policy" might be `1, 2, 3`; "time off rules" might be `1, 2, 2` — different words, nearby numbers, because the meanings are close. "Pizza recipe" lands at `9, 7, 4` — far in every direction. Nearby fingerprint, nearby meaning. That's the whole mechanism.

Two size notes, so the picture stays honest: real fingerprints are much longer than three numbers — often hundreds or thousands. And the closeness math is simple, but you don't need it yet — you need the idea. Also: fingerprints are for *meaning*, not spelling, so "fruit" could land near "apple" too.

One precision on who does what, because it's easy to picture the embedding itself running around the database: **the embedding doesn't search.** It's just the number version of the text. The *search step* compares your question's fingerprint against the stored fingerprints and picks the closest ones. Embeddings make matching possible. Search is the matching that uses them. RAG is the whole job.

Where do the fingerprints live? Not on the original documents — on *pieces* of them. Documents get chopped into small **chunks**, roughly a paragraph each, and every chunk gets fingerprinted separately. You don't retrieve a 90-page manual for one question — you retrieve the three paragraphs that matter. Those chunks' fingerprints are stored in a **vector database**. *Vector* is just the technical word for a list of numbers: a fingerprint is a vector, and a database that stores and compares them by closeness is a vector database.

Then your question — the one that mattered: *when are the document fingerprints made?* Ahead of time, and the split is the whole story. **Setup moment** — once, before anyone asks anything: a developer loads the company documents, chops them into chunks, fingerprints every chunk with the embedding model, stores them in the vector database. Like preparing the library before visitors arrive — once up front, and again only when documents change. **Question moment** — every time: only the question gets a fresh fingerprint, because it's unpredictable and can't be pre-made, and it's compared against the stored ones. The closest chunks come back.

And both sides must use the **same embedding model** — same ruler. Different models put meaning in different numeric spaces, so a question fingerprinted by one model means nothing next to documents fingerprinted by another. The company's fingerprint system, applied to both sides: that's what makes the numbers comparable.

Your CRUD doubt deserves its own answer, because you reasoned it out: a normal database stores plain data and never needs fingerprints. An agriculture site's insert-read-update-delete has nothing to do with embeddings. They enter the picture only when you add an AI search-by-meaning feature: keep your normal database, add a vector database *beside* it — or a vector feature inside it — holding fingerprints of whatever text you want searchable by meaning. And live data — the internet, GitHub — is never pre-fingerprinted; it arrives as plain text through tools. Two doors, now with full precision: **RAG for your stored collection, searched by meaning. Tools for live stuff, plain text, no fingerprints.**

The nots: an embedding is *not* the retrieving itself — it's the fingerprint that makes matching possible. Not random numbers — constructed to encode meaning. Not needed by every database — plain CRUD is fine. And not magic understanding — numbers and math, a compressed pattern of what the text is about.
