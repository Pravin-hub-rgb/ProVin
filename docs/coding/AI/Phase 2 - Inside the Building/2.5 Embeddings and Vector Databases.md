RAG is the fetcher: controller searches first, bundles, LLM answers. But one word in that story was promised and not delivered — how does the fetcher recognize which documents are *close in meaning* to the question?

Exact word matching would fail the moment your question says "time off" and the policy page says "leave". The search needs to match *meaning*. That matching has a mechanism, and the mechanism starts with a fingerprint.

---

## The Fingerprint

> **An embedding is a list of numbers that captures the meaning of a piece of text.**

Text goes in, numbers come out — and the numbers aren't random. They're built so that **similar meaning produces similar numbers.**

Your example, kept exactly as you built it:

- "leave policy" → something like `1, 2, 3`
- "time off rules" → something like `1, 2, 2` — different words, *nearby* numbers, because the meanings are close
- "pizza recipe" → something like `9, 7, 4` — far away in every direction

That list of numbers is the **fingerprint** — a meaning-in-numbers version of the text. Nearby fingerprint = nearby meaning. That's the whole mechanism.

Two size notes so the picture stays honest:

- Real fingerprints are much longer than three numbers — often hundreds or thousands.
- The closeness math is simple but not needed yet. You need the *idea*: numbers close, meanings close.

Also: the fingerprint is for *meaning*, so "fruit" could land near "apple" too — it's not spelling matching, it's meaning matching.

## Who Actually Searches?

One precision on roles, because it's easy to picture the embedding *itself* running around the database:

**The embedding doesn't search. It's just the number version of the text.** The search step compares your question's fingerprint against the stored fingerprints and picks the closest ones.

So the pieces are:

- **Embedding** = the fingerprint (makes matching *possible*)
- **Search** = the comparing step (uses the fingerprints)
- **RAG** = the whole fetching job (search is one step inside it)

Embeddings make the matching possible; retrieving is the matching that uses them.

---

## Chunks and the Vector Database

Now, where do fingerprints live? Not on the original documents — on *pieces* of them.

Documents get chopped into small pieces called **chunks** — roughly a paragraph each. Every chunk gets fingerprinted separately. Why chunks? Because you don't retrieve a 90-page manual for one question — you retrieve the three paragraphs that matter.

Those fingerprints are stored in a special database called a **vector database**. *Vector* is just the technical word for a list of numbers — a fingerprint is a vector, and a database that stores and compares them by closeness is a vector database.

When your question's fingerprint arrives, the vector database finds the stored fingerprints closest to it and returns the matching chunks.

---

## Two Moments — Setup and Question Time

You asked the sharp question: *when are the document fingerprints made?* Ahead of time — and the split matters:

**Setup moment (once, before anyone asks anything):**

```
developer loads company documents
  → chops into chunks
  → fingerprints every chunk (embedding model)
  → stores fingerprints in the vector database
```

Like preparing a library before visitors arrive. Happens once up front — and again only when documents change.

**Question moment (every time someone asks):**

```
question arrives
  → question gets a fresh fingerprint (unpredictable — can't be pre-made)
  → compared against the stored fingerprints
  → closest chunks returned
```

The documents are known ahead of time, so their fingerprints wait ready. The question is unpredictable, so it's fingerprinted the moment it's asked.

**The same-ruler rule:** both fingerprints must be made by the **same embedding model** — otherwise the numbers aren't comparable, like measuring two things with different rulers. The company's fingerprint system, applied to both sides.

## Does Your CRUD App Need This?

Your instinct here was exactly right, and worth locking in: **a normal database stores plain data and never needs fingerprints.**

An agriculture site's CRUD backend — insert, read, update, delete — has nothing to do with embeddings. They only enter the picture when you add an **AI search-by-meaning feature** on top: then you'd keep your normal database and add a vector database *beside* it (or a vector feature inside it) holding fingerprints of whatever text you want searchable by meaning.

Same for the third question you raised: **things fetched live from the internet or GitHub are not pre-fingerprinted.** They arrive as plain text through tools and go straight to the LLM. Fingerprints exist only for RAG's search over your own stored collection.

Two doors, restated with full precision now: **RAG = your stored collection, searched by meaning. Tools = live stuff, plain text, no fingerprints.**

---

## What Embeddings Are NOT

- **Not the retrieving itself.** Retrieving is RAG's job; an embedding is the fingerprint that makes the matching possible.
- **Not random numbers.** They're constructed to encode meaning — nearby numbers, nearby meanings.
- **Not needed by every database.** Plain CRUD stores data fine; fingerprints only matter for AI search over meaning.
- **Not magic understanding.** It's still numbers and math — a compressed pattern of what the text is *about*, made by a model, compared with simple distance.
