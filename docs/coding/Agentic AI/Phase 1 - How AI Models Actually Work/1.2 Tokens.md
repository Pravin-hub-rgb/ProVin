Now that you know what a language model is — that giant autocomplete trained on the internet — let's talk about the actual units it works with. Because the model doesn't read words the way you do.

**A token is the smallest piece of text a model can process.** It's the atomic unit of its universe. When you send a message, the model isn't looking at letters or whole sentences — it breaks everything down into tokens first.

Here's where it gets interesting: tokens are not words. They're sub-word chunks.

Take the word "unbelievable." A model might split it into two tokens: "un" and "believable." The word "running" might be one token. A common word like "the" is usually one token. A rare word like "antidisestablishment" might be three or four.

Why does the model do this? Because it needs to handle any word it might encounter, including ones it's never seen before. If it only knew whole words, it'd be helpless against typos, compound words, or new terminology. By learning sub-word pieces, it can assemble meaning from familiar building blocks — like a child who's never seen "unbelievable" but knows what "un" and "believable" mean separately.

You can actually see this in action. Most AI providers have a tokenizer tool where you paste text and it shows you exactly how it gets chopped up. The results are sometimes surprising. "Hello!" might be one token, but "Hello?" might be two because the question mark is less common. Spaces, punctuation, even capitalization all affect how things get split.

Here's a rough rule of thumb: **1 token ≈ 0.75 words in English.** So a 500-word email is around 650 tokens. But this varies a lot — code tends to be more token-dense than prose because of indentation, special characters, and long variable names.

Why should you, as a vibe coder, care about any of this? Two big reasons:

First, **everything costs tokens.** Every message you send, every file you ask the model to read, every response it generates — that's all metered in tokens. When you're using Cline or opencode and you upload a large file, you're burning through your available token budget. Models have a maximum they can handle, called the context window.

Second, **token awareness helps you prompt better.** If you know the model sees "un" and "believable" as separate tokens, you understand why it might occasionally lose the relationship between them in a very long conversation. You start to think in terms of the model's granularity, not your own.

So that's tokens: not words, not letters — somewhere in between. They're the currency of every interaction you have with an AI. And they live inside something called a **context window**, which is exactly what's up next.
