You've created your branch. You're working on your feature. Life is good. But your teammates are also working — they're merging their own changes into `main` while you code away. Days pass. You finish your feature and try to merge back. Suddenly you're facing 15 merge conflicts. Files you barely touched are all colliding.

This is called a "conflict bomb" and it's totally preventable.

**The problem:**

When you created your branch, you got a snapshot of main at that moment. Since then, your teammates have been busy. They've merged new features, fixed bugs, updated dependencies. Main has moved forward. Your branch hasn't.

Your branch is now "behind" main by however many commits your teammates made. When you finally try to merge, Git has to reconcile all those changes at once. If you and a teammate both edited the same file (which happens all the time on real projects), those become conflicts. Big, messy, multiple-file conflicts.

![Branch before and after syncing with main — behind by 4 commits on the left, caught up on the right](/diagrams/gitgithub/sync-branch.svg)

The diagram above shows the before and after. On the left, your `feature/navbar` branch is behind main by 4 commits — a conflict bomb waiting to happen. On the right, after running `git fetch` + `git merge origin/main`, your branch now includes all of main's latest commits. Your work and the team's work are together.

**The fix is simple:**

Regularly pull the latest main into your branch. Like, once a day.

```bash
git switch feature/navbar       # make sure you're on your branch
git fetch origin                 # get the latest from GitHub
git merge origin/main            # merge main's changes into your branch
```

Now your branch has your work PLUS everything your teammates added. Any conflicts that come up will be small — you're only catching up on one day's changes, not two weeks'.

Do this at least once a day while your branch is alive.

**What actually happens when you merge main into your branch:**

Git finds the commit where your branch and main last agreed (the common ancestor). Then it applies all the commits from main that your branch is missing. Then it creates a "merge commit" that has two parents — your branch and main.

After the merge, the graph looks like:

```
*   Merge branch 'main' into feature/navbar
|\
| *  (main) Add database pooling (teammate's work)
| *  Fix footer alignment (teammate's work)
* |  Add dropdown menu (your work)
* |  Fix mobile spacing (your work)
|/
*  Initial commit
```

Your work and main's work are now together in your branch. When you eventually merge your pull request (PR), there won't be a big surprise because you've been resolving conflicts along the way.

**What about `git rebase`?**

There's another way to sync: `git rebase origin/main`. Instead of creating a merge commit, it rewrites your branch's history so it looks like you started from the latest main. The history becomes a straight line — no merge commits, no branching lines.

`Rebase` creates a cleaner history, but it rewrites your commits (they get new IDs). This is fine for your own branch, but **never rebase a branch that other people have also been working on** — it causes chaos for them.

For now, use `git merge origin/main`. It's simpler and safer. We'll cover rebase properly later.

**To remember:**

- Sync your branch with main at least once a day
- Sync before asking someone to review your code
- Sync after a teammate merges something big

Don't be the person who works in isolation for two weeks and then drops a conflict bomb on the team.

**In Your Own Words:**

<details>
<summary>1. What does it mean when someone says your branch is "behind" main?</summary>

It means new commits have been added to main since you created your branch. Your branch doesn't have those changes yet.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. What commands do you run to bring main's latest changes into your branch?</summary>

`git fetch origin` to get the latest from GitHub, then `git merge origin/main` to merge those changes into your branch.
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. What is a "conflict bomb"?</summary>

A massive pile of conflicts that shows up when you try to merge a branch that hasn't synced with main in a long time. Regular syncing prevents this.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. How often should you sync your branch with main?</summary>

At least once a day. More often if your teammates are actively merging changes.
</details>

<details>
<summary>5. What's the basic difference between merging main into your branch vs rebasing onto main?</summary>

Merge creates a merge commit preserving both histories. Rebase rewrites your commits on top of main for a cleaner, linear history but shouldn't be used on shared branches.
</details>
